    Gram-Schmidt: subtract the projections, then normalize

    October 2026 · 10 min read

    Before you start: the dot product. The first two vectors here are the columns in the QR example. This note is the process that example assumed, plus the third vector.

    This is video 5 of the Matrix Decompositions playlist, Gram-Schmidt Process Example by FiveMinuteLectures (29 June 2025, 4 minutes 50 seconds). It turns a basis into an orthonormal basis: first mutually perpendicular vectors, then unit length. The inner product in the example is the ordinary dot product. The norm has to come from that same product, \(\lVert w\rVert=\sqrt{\langle w,w\rangle}\).

    Orthogonal, then unit length

    An orthonormal set does two jobs. The vectors are mutually orthogonal: each pair meets at a right angle, so the dot product is 0. Each one is a unit vector: \(\lVert u\rVert=1\). The lecture splits the work the same way. Steps 1 and 2 only produce orthogonal \(w\)'s. Step 3 divides by the lengths.

    The picture is a perpendicular drop. From the tip of \(v_2\), drop a line onto the line through \(v_1\). That foot is the projection: how much of \(v_2\) points along \(v_1\). Subtract it. The remainder, drawn in blue, meets \(v_1\) at a right angle. It no longer points along \(v_1\), which is what the dot product being zero means.

    For the standard dot product the projection onto a vector that is not yet unit length is

    $$\operatorname{proj}_{w}(v)=\frac{\langle v,w\rangle}{\lVert w\rVert^2}\,w.$$

    The numerator is the inner product on the slide, written \(\langle v_2,w_1\rangle\). If you leave it out, the formula only works when that dot product happens to be 1. In this example it is 1 for the second vector, and it is not 1 for the third.

    Why the subtraction is perpendicular

    Set \(w_1=v_1\) and

    $$w_2=v_2-\frac{\langle v_2,w_1\rangle}{\lVert w_1\rVert^2}\,w_1.$$

    Dot with \(w_1\). The second term is built so that it cancels the first:

    $$w_2\cdot w_1=v_2\cdot w_1-\frac{v_2\cdot w_1}{\lVert w_1\rVert^2}\,(w_1\cdot w_1)=v_2\cdot w_1-v_2\cdot w_1=0.$$

    That is the check the lecture asks for after each new vector. A later vector subtracts one projection for every \(w\) already kept. Each of those dots comes out zero for the same reason, provided you use the \(w\)'s, not the original \(v\)'s, in the later projections. If a remainder is the zero vector, the new \(v\) was already in the span of the earlier ones. The set was not a basis, and there is no length to divide by.

    The three vectors

    The example is these three vectors in \(\mathbb{R}^3\), with the standard dot product:

    $$v_1=\begin{bmatrix}1\\1\\0\end{bmatrix},\qquad v_2=\begin{bmatrix}0\\1\\-1\end{bmatrix},\qquad v_3=\begin{bmatrix}1\\0\\-1\end{bmatrix}.$$

    They are a basis: the matrix with these columns has determinant \(-2\), so they are independent, and three independent vectors in \(\mathbb{R}^3\) span the whole space. The first two are the columns of \(A\) in the QR example. Their \(w\)'s and unit vectors will match that note. \(v_3\) is the extra vector.

    \(w_2\), and the dot product that equals 1

    \(w_1=v_1=(1,1,0)\). The slide computes \(\lVert w_1\rVert^2=1+1+0=2\). And \(\langle v_2,w_1\rangle=1\), so the coefficient is \(1/2\):

    $$w_2=\begin{bmatrix}0\\1\\-1\end{bmatrix}-\frac12\begin{bmatrix}1\\1\\0\end{bmatrix}=\begin{bmatrix}-1/2\\1/2\\-1\end{bmatrix}.$$

    That is the printed \(w_2\). The check is \(w_2\cdot w_1=-1/2+1/2+0=0\).

    Two projections for \(w_3\)

    The formula on the slide is

    $$w_3=v_3-\operatorname{proj}_{w_1}(v_3)-\operatorname{proj}_{w_2}(v_3).$$

    \(\langle v_3,w_1\rangle=1\), and \(\lVert w_1\rVert^2=2\), so the first projection is again half of \(w_1\): \((1/2,1/2,0)\).

    The second coefficient is not 1. \(\langle v_3,w_2\rangle=-1/2+0+1=1/2\), and \(\lVert w_2\rVert^2=1/4+1/4+1=3/2\), so the coefficient is \((1/2)/(3/2)=1/3\):

    $$\operatorname{proj}_{w_2}(v_3)=\frac13\begin{bmatrix}-1/2\\1/2\\-1\end{bmatrix}=\begin{bmatrix}-1/6\\1/6\\-1/3\end{bmatrix}.$$

    Subtract both from \(v_3=(1,0,-1)\):

    $$w_3=\begin{bmatrix}1-1/2-(-1/6)\\0-1/2-1/6\\-1-0-(-1/3)\end{bmatrix}=\begin{bmatrix}2/3\\-2/3\\-2/3\end{bmatrix}.$$

    That is the printed \(w_3\). The three dots the lecture asks for are all zero: \(w_3\cdot w_1=2/3-2/3=0\), and \(w_3\cdot w_2=-1/3-1/3+2/3=0\). \(w_2\cdot w_1\) was already 0.

    Divide by the length, not twice the length

    The last slide is \(u_n=w_n/\lVert w_n\rVert\), with the length from the same inner product. The first two match the QR note:

    $$\lVert w_1\rVert=\sqrt{2},\qquad u_1=\begin{bmatrix}1/\sqrt{2}\\1/\sqrt{2}\\0\end{bmatrix},\qquad \lVert w_2\rVert=\sqrt{3/2},\qquad u_2=\begin{bmatrix}-1/\sqrt{6}\\1/\sqrt{6}\\-\sqrt{2/3}\end{bmatrix}.$$

    For the third, \(\lVert w_3\rVert^2=3\cdot(4/9)=4/3\), so \(\lVert w_3\rVert=2/\sqrt{3}\). Divide componentwise:

    $$u_3=\frac{2/3}{2/\sqrt{3}}\begin{bmatrix}1\\-1\\-1\end{bmatrix}=\begin{bmatrix}1/\sqrt{3}\\-1/\sqrt{3}\\-1/\sqrt{3}\end{bmatrix}=\frac{\sqrt{3}}{3}\begin{bmatrix}1\\-1\\-1\end{bmatrix}.$$

    The normalize slide prints \(\sqrt{3}/6\) in each slot of \(u_3\). That vector has length \(1/2\), not \(1\). It is \(w_3\) divided by \(4/\sqrt{3}\), which is twice \(\lVert w_3\rVert\). Use \(\sqrt{3}/3\), or \(1/\sqrt{3}\).

    With that correction, each \(u_i\) has length 1, and the dots stay zero because scaling does not change a zero dot product. The three \(u\)'s are an orthonormal basis of \(\mathbb{R}^3\). In the QR note, \(u_1\) and \(u_2\) are the two columns of \(Q\). This lecture stops at the basis. It does not form \(R\).

    w3 = [2/3, -2/3, -2/3]
norm = sum(x*x for x in w3) ** 0.5
u3 = [x/norm for x in w3]
assert abs(sum(x*x for x in u3) - 1) < 1e-12
assert all(abs(u3[i] - (1 if i==0 else -1)/3**0.5) < 1e-12 for i in range(3))

    Checks

    Why is the coefficient in front of \(w_1\) equal to \(1/2\) for both \(v_2\) and \(v_3\)?

    Both dot \(w_1\) in 1, and \(\lVert w_1\rVert^2=2\). The numerator is not always 1. For \(w_2\) it is \(1/2\), and the denominator is \(3/2\), so that coefficient is \(1/3\).

    Why dot \(w_3\) against \(w_1\) and \(w_2\), not against \(v_1\) and \(v_2\)?

    The projections that were subtracted were onto the \(w\)'s. Those are the vectors the new remainder was built to miss. \(v_2\) is not perpendicular to \(v_1\).

    The slide prints \(u_3=(\sqrt{3}/6)(1,-1,-1)\). What is the length?

    \(1/2\). Three copies of \(3/36\) add to \(1/4\), and the square root of that is \(1/2\). The unit vector in that direction is \((1/\sqrt{3})(1,-1,-1)\).

    What fails if a remainder is the zero vector?

    The new \(v\) was already a combination of the earlier ones, so the set was not a basis. There is no nonzero direction to normalize.
