    Gram-Schmidt: subtract the projections, then read off R

    October 2026 · 18 min read

    Before you start: the dot product. This page is two lectures. The process is what the QR example animates and does not write out. The square version of QR is the reflector note.

    Playlist videos 4 and 5 build the same orthonormal columns. Video 5 is Gram-Schmidt Process Example (29 June 2025, 4 minutes 50 seconds). It turns a basis into an orthonormal basis: first mutually perpendicular vectors, then unit length. Video 4 is QR Factorization Example (10 July 2025, 4 minutes 34 seconds). It uses the first two of those vectors as the columns of a 3 by 2, reads off \(R\), and back-substitutes. Video 5 was uploaded first. In the playlist it is the later of the two. \(v_3\) is only in the process lecture. The normalize slide prints a third unit vector of length \(1/2\). The inner product in the example is the ordinary dot product. The norm has to come from that same product, \(\lVert w\rVert=\sqrt{\langle w,w\rangle}\).

    Orthogonal, then unit length

    An orthonormal set does two jobs. The vectors are mutually orthogonal: each pair meets at a right angle, so the dot product is 0. Each one is a unit vector: \(\lVert u\rVert=1\). The lecture splits the work the same way. Steps 1 and 2 only produce orthogonal \(w\)'s. Step 3 divides by the lengths.

    The picture is a perpendicular drop. From the tip of \(v_2\), drop a line onto the line through \(v_1\). That foot is the projection: how much of \(v_2\) points along \(v_1\). Subtract it. The remainder, drawn in blue, meets \(v_1\) at a right angle. It no longer points along \(v_1\), which is what the dot product being zero means.

    For the standard dot product the projection onto a vector that is not yet unit length is

    $$\operatorname{proj}_{w}(v)=\frac{\langle v,w\rangle}{\lVert w\rVert^2}\,w.$$

    The numerator is the inner product on the slide, written \(\langle v_2,w_1\rangle\). If you leave it out, the formula only works when that dot product happens to be 1. In this example it is 1 for the second vector, and it is not 1 for the third.

    Why the subtraction is perpendicular

    Set \(w_1=v_1\) and

    $$w_2=v_2-\frac{\langle v_2,w_1\rangle}{\lVert w_1\rVert^2}\,w_1.$$

    Dot with \(w_1\). The second term is built so that it cancels the first:

    $$w_2\cdot w_1=v_2\cdot w_1-\frac{v_2\cdot w_1}{\lVert w_1\rVert^2}\,(w_1\cdot w_1)=v_2\cdot w_1-v_2\cdot w_1=0.$$

    That is the check the lecture asks for after each new vector. A later vector subtracts one projection for every \(w\) already kept. Each of those dots comes out zero for the same reason, provided you use the \(w\)'s, not the original \(v\)'s, in the later projections. If a remainder is the zero vector, the new \(v\) was already in the span of the earlier ones. The set was not a basis, and there is no length to divide by.

    The three vectors

    The example is these three vectors in \(\mathbb{R}^3\), with the standard dot product:

    $$v_1=\begin{bmatrix}1\\1\\0\end{bmatrix},\qquad v_2=\begin{bmatrix}0\\1\\-1\end{bmatrix},\qquad v_3=\begin{bmatrix}1\\0\\-1\end{bmatrix}.$$

    They are a basis: the matrix with these columns has determinant \(-2\), so they are independent, and three independent vectors in \(\mathbb{R}^3\) span the whole space. The first two are the columns of \(A\) in the QR example below. \(v_3\) is the extra vector.

    \(w_2\), and the dot product that equals 1

    \(w_1=v_1=(1,1,0)\). The slide computes \(\lVert w_1\rVert^2=1+1+0=2\). And \(\langle v_2,w_1\rangle=1\), so the coefficient is \(1/2\):

    $$w_2=\begin{bmatrix}0\\1\\-1\end{bmatrix}-\frac12\begin{bmatrix}1\\1\\0\end{bmatrix}=\begin{bmatrix}-1/2\\1/2\\-1\end{bmatrix}.$$

    That is the printed \(w_2\), and it is the vector the QR animation draws without writing the projection. The check is \(w_2\cdot w_1=-1/2+1/2+0=0\).

    Two projections for \(w_3\)

    The formula on the slide is

    $$w_3=v_3-\operatorname{proj}_{w_1}(v_3)-\operatorname{proj}_{w_2}(v_3).$$

    \(\langle v_3,w_1\rangle=1\), and \(\lVert w_1\rVert^2=2\), so the first projection is again half of \(w_1\): \((1/2,1/2,0)\).

    The second coefficient is not 1. \(\langle v_3,w_2\rangle=-1/2+0+1=1/2\), and \(\lVert w_2\rVert^2=1/4+1/4+1=3/2\), so the coefficient is \((1/2)/(3/2)=1/3\):

    $$\operatorname{proj}_{w_2}(v_3)=\frac13\begin{bmatrix}-1/2\\1/2\\-1\end{bmatrix}=\begin{bmatrix}-1/6\\1/6\\-1/3\end{bmatrix}.$$

    Subtract both from \(v_3=(1,0,-1)\):

    $$w_3=\begin{bmatrix}1-1/2-(-1/6)\\0-1/2-1/6\\-1-0-(-1/3)\end{bmatrix}=\begin{bmatrix}2/3\\-2/3\\-2/3\end{bmatrix}.$$

    That is the printed \(w_3\). The three dots the lecture asks for are all zero: \(w_3\cdot w_1=2/3-2/3=0\), and \(w_3\cdot w_2=-1/3-1/3+2/3=0\). \(w_2\cdot w_1\) was already 0.

    Divide by the length, not twice the length

    The last slide of the process lecture is \(u_n=w_n/\lVert w_n\rVert\), with the length from the same inner product. The first two are the columns the QR example will call \(Q\):

    $$\lVert w_1\rVert=\sqrt{2},\qquad u_1=\begin{bmatrix}1/\sqrt{2}\\1/\sqrt{2}\\0\end{bmatrix},\qquad \lVert w_2\rVert=\sqrt{3/2},\qquad u_2=\begin{bmatrix}-1/\sqrt{6}\\1/\sqrt{6}\\-\sqrt{2/3}\end{bmatrix}.$$

    The third entry of \(u_2\) is \(-\sqrt{2/3}\) because \(1/\sqrt{3/2}=\sqrt{2/3}\), and \(\sqrt{2/3}=2/\sqrt{6}\), so \(-1\cdot\sqrt{2/3}=-2/\sqrt{6}\). The first entry is half of that length, with a minus sign: \((-1/2)\sqrt{2/3}=-1/\sqrt{6}\). Same arithmetic for the middle entry, with a plus.

    For the third vector, \(\lVert w_3\rVert^2=3\cdot(4/9)=4/3\), so \(\lVert w_3\rVert=2/\sqrt{3}\). Divide componentwise:

    $$u_3=\frac{2/3}{2/\sqrt{3}}\begin{bmatrix}1\\-1\\-1\end{bmatrix}=\begin{bmatrix}1/\sqrt{3}\\-1/\sqrt{3}\\-1/\sqrt{3}\end{bmatrix}=\frac{\sqrt{3}}{3}\begin{bmatrix}1\\-1\\-1\end{bmatrix}.$$

    The normalize slide prints \(\sqrt{3}/6\) in each slot of \(u_3\). That vector has length \(1/2\), not \(1\). It is \(w_3\) divided by \(4/\sqrt{3}\), which is twice \(\lVert w_3\rVert\). Use \(\sqrt{3}/3\), or \(1/\sqrt{3}\).

    With that correction, each \(u_i\) has length 1, and the dots stay zero because scaling does not change a zero dot product. The three \(u\)'s are an orthonormal basis of \(\mathbb{R}^3\). The QR example uses \(u_1\) and \(u_2\) only. It does not use \(u_3\), and it does form \(R\).

    w3 = [2/3, -2/3, -2/3]
norm = sum(x*x for x in w3) ** 0.5
u3 = [x/norm for x in w3]
assert abs(sum(x*x for x in u3) - 1) < 1e-12
assert all(abs(u3[i] - (1 if i==0 else -1)/3**0.5) < 1e-12 for i in range(3))

    Semi-orthogonal means orthonormal columns

    Video 4 asks for \(A=QR\), then a triangular system for \(Ax=b\). The matrix is the first two vectors above, so two unknowns. The spoken "\(x\), \(y\), and \(z\)" does not match this matrix.

    $$A=\begin{bmatrix}1&0\\1&1\\0&-1\end{bmatrix}.$$

    The definition slide writes \(Q\) as columns \(q_1,q_2,\ldots,q_n\) and asks for two things: \(q_i\cdot q_j=0\) when \(i\neq j\), and \(\lVert q_i\rVert=1\). Those two lines are the same matrix equation

    $$Q^{\mathsf T}Q=I.$$

    If \(Q\) is square, the lecture calls it orthogonal, and then \(Q^{-1}=Q^{\mathsf T}\). The factorization also allows a tall \(Q\). A 3 by 2 matrix cannot have an inverse, but it can still have orthonormal columns. That is the rectangular case the lecture calls semi-orthogonal. The identity on the right is then the small one, 2 by 2 in the example, not 3 by 3. You never multiply by \(Q^{-1}\). You multiply by \(Q^{\mathsf T}\).

    The lecture says this QR works for any size and any rank. The procedure on the slides divides by the length of each new vector. A column that is already in the span of the earlier ones leaves a zero vector, and there is nothing to divide by. This example has independent columns, so that division is legal. A dependent column would put a zero on the diagonal of \(R\) and add no new column to \(Q\).

    The animation sets \(w_1=v_1\) and \(w_2=v_2-\operatorname{proj}_{w_1}(v_2)\). That remainder is the \(w_2\) above. The slide uses the un-normalized form. Projecting onto the unit vector \(q_1\) instead gives the same remainder: the coefficient is \(v_2\cdot q_1=1/\sqrt{2}\), and \((1/\sqrt{2})q_1=(1/2,1/2,0)\). \(Q\) is \(u_1\) and \(u_2\):

    $$Q=\begin{bmatrix}1/\sqrt{2}&-1/\sqrt{6}\\1/\sqrt{2}&1/\sqrt{6}\\0&-\sqrt{2/3}\end{bmatrix}.$$

    The two slides, before the numbers

    Assume \(A=QR\), with the columns of \(Q\) an orthonormal basis for the column space, and \(R\) square and upper triangular. Multiply on the left by \(Q^{\mathsf T}\):

    $$Q^{\mathsf T}A=Q^{\mathsf T}QR=R.$$

    The middle \(Q^{\mathsf T}Q\) is the identity from the definition slide. That is the whole of step 2. \(R\) is not a new factorization. It is the matrix of dot products \(R_{ij}=q_i\cdot a_j\). It is upper triangular because \(a_j\) was built from \(q_1,\ldots,q_j\) only, so the later \(q_i\) are perpendicular to it.

    For the linear system, replace \(A\) by \(QR\):

    $$QRx=b,\qquad Rx=Q^{\mathsf T}b.$$

    The second line is the first multiplied on the left by \(Q^{\mathsf T}\). \(R\) is square and upper triangular, so the unknowns come out by back-substitution. The lecture compares this with LU: you still finish on a triangular system, and this construction does not swap rows.

    This is not the Householder QR. There \(Q\) was a square product of reflectors. Here \(Q\) is the thin matrix whose columns are \(u_1\) and \(u_2\), and \(R=Q^{\mathsf T}A\) rather than a product of reflectors times \(A\).

    \(R=Q^{\mathsf T}A\), entry by entry

    Each entry is a dot product of a \(q_i\) with a column of \(A\). Those columns are \(v_1\) and \(v_2\).

    $$q_1\cdot v_1=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}+\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}=\sqrt{2},\qquad q_1\cdot v_2=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}.$$
    $$q_2\cdot v_1=-\frac{1}{\sqrt{6}}+\frac{1}{\sqrt{6}}=0,\qquad q_2\cdot v_2=\frac{1}{\sqrt{6}}+\sqrt{\frac{2}{3}}=\frac{3}{\sqrt{6}}.$$

    The last sum is \(1/\sqrt{6}+2/\sqrt{6}\), because \(\sqrt{2/3}=2/\sqrt{6}\). So

    $$R=\begin{bmatrix}\sqrt{2}&1/\sqrt{2}\\0&3/\sqrt{6}\end{bmatrix},$$

    which is the matrix on the slide. The zero under the first pivot is the Gram-Schmidt step: \(q_2\) was built to be perpendicular to \(v_1\).

    Two checks before using it. \(Q^{\mathsf T}Q=I_2\): each column has length 1, and \(q_1\cdot q_2=-1/\sqrt{12}+1/\sqrt{12}=0\). And \(QR=A\): the first column is \(\sqrt{2}\,q_1=(1,1,0)\). The second is \((1/\sqrt{2})q_1+(3/\sqrt{6})q_2=(0,1,-1)\).

    Back-substitution, on a right-hand side that \(A\) can hit

    The last slide is \(Rx=Q^{\mathsf T}b\). No numeric \(b\) is printed. \(A\) is 3 by 2, so a random \(b\) in \(\mathbb{R}^3\) need not be \(Ax\) for any \(x\). The formula still returns the \(x\) whose residual is perpendicular to the columns. It is an exact solution only when \(b\) is in the column space.

    One such \(b\), not on the slide, is \(A(1,1)^{\mathsf T}=(1,2,-1)\). Then the right-hand side should come back as \((1,1)\).

    $$Q^{\mathsf T}b=\begin{bmatrix}3/\sqrt{2}\\3/\sqrt{6}\end{bmatrix}.$$

    The second row of \(R\) has only the corner entry:

    $$\frac{3}{\sqrt{6}}x_2=\frac{3}{\sqrt{6}}\quad\Rightarrow\quad x_2=1.$$

    The first row is \(\sqrt{2}\,x_1+(1/\sqrt{2})x_2=3/\sqrt{2}\). Put in \(x_2=1\):

    $$\sqrt{2}\,x_1=\frac{3}{\sqrt{2}}-\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}=\sqrt{2}\quad\Rightarrow\quad x_1=1.$$

    That is back-substitution. Start at the bottom pivot, then clear the known term from the row above. For a larger \(R\) you walk up the diagonal the same way. No row swaps.

    from math import sqrt
A = [[1, 0], [1, 1], [0, -1]]
Q = [[1/sqrt(2), -1/sqrt(6)], [1/sqrt(2), 1/sqrt(6)], [0, -sqrt(2/3)]]
R = [[sqrt(2), 1/sqrt(2)], [0, 3/sqrt(6)]]
# QR should return A
got = [[Q[i][0]*R[0][j] + Q[i][1]*R[1][j] for j in range(2)] for i in range(3)]
assert all(abs(got[i][j] - A[i][j]) < 1e-9 for i in range(3) for j in range(2))

    Checks

    Why is the coefficient in front of \(w_1\) equal to \(1/2\) for both \(v_2\) and \(v_3\)?

    Both dot \(w_1\) in 1, and \(\lVert w_1\rVert^2=2\). The numerator is not always 1. For \(w_2\) it is \(1/2\), and the denominator is \(3/2\), so that coefficient is \(1/3\).

    Why dot \(w_3\) against \(w_1\) and \(w_2\), not against \(v_1\) and \(v_2\)?

    The projections that were subtracted were onto the \(w\)'s. Those are the vectors the new remainder was built to miss. \(v_2\) is not perpendicular to \(v_1\).

    The slide prints \(u_3=(\sqrt{3}/6)(1,-1,-1)\). What is the length?

    \(1/2\). Three copies of \(3/36\) add to \(1/4\), and the square root of that is \(1/2\). The unit vector in that direction is \((1/\sqrt{3})(1,-1,-1)\).

    What fails if a remainder is the zero vector?

    The new \(v\) was already a combination of the earlier ones, so the set was not a basis. There is no nonzero direction to normalize.

    Why is the identity \(Q^{\mathsf T}Q=I\) only 2 by 2 here?

    \(Q\) has two orthonormal columns in \(\mathbb{R}^3\). The product \(Q^{\mathsf T}Q\) compares those columns with each other. It does not say the two columns span all of \(\mathbb{R}^3\).

    Where does the \(-1/2\) in \(w_2\) come from?

    \(v_2\cdot w_1=1\) and \(w_1\cdot w_1=2\), so the projection is half of \(w_1\), which is \((1/2,1/2,0)\). Subtract that from \((0,1,-1)\).

    Why is \(R_{21}=0\) without multiplying the matrices?

    \(q_2\) was built perpendicular to \(v_1\). The entry \(R_{21}\) is that dot product.

    Can you left-multiply \(QRx=b\) by \(Q^{-1}\)?

    Not in this example. \(Q\) is 3 by 2. The legal left factor is \(Q^{\mathsf T}\), and it produces a 2 by 2 system.
