    Eigenvalue decomposition: diagonalize a square matrix

    October 2026 · 14 min read

    Before you start: matrix multiplication, a 2 by 2 determinant, and row reduction. The inverse steps are the same elimination used for a matrix inverse. Textbooks often write the same factorization as \(PDP^{-1}\). This lecture uses \(S\) and \(\Lambda\).

    This is video 1 of the Matrix Decompositions playlist, Eigenvalue Decomposition / Diagonalization by FiveMinuteLectures (21 September 2025, 5 minutes 25 seconds). The slides state the definition, the three steps, and a 3 by 3 example. They skip the second and third eigenvector calculations and the inversion arithmetic. Those steps are written out below, and they land on the same vectors and the same inverse as the slides.

    A direction the matrix does not turn

    Eigenvalues are defined for a square matrix. The lecture starts from

    $$Av=\lambda v.$$

    \(\lambda\) is the eigenvalue and \(v\) is an eigenvector. The geometric question on the slide is: for which nonzero \(v\) does \(A\) only stretch or flip \(v\), without turning it? On the lecture's grid, \(v\) and \(Av\) lie on the same line. The zero vector satisfies the equation for every \(\lambda\), and it is excluded. An eigenvector has to be a direction.

    Why the identity shows up

    Move \(\lambda v\) to the left:

    $$Av-\lambda v=0.$$

    Both terms contain \(v\), so it is tempting to factor. You cannot write \((A-\lambda)v\). \(A\) is a matrix and \(\lambda\) is a number. Subtracting them is not a legal operation. The fix is the identity: \(\lambda v=(\lambda I)v\), because \(I\) leaves \(v\) alone and \(\lambda\) then scales it. Factoring is now legal.

    $$(A-\lambda I)v=0.$$

    The lecture highlights \(I\) in yellow for this reason. On the example below, \(A-\lambda I\) is the matrix you actually row-reduce.

    A nonzero \(v\) forces a zero determinant

    \((A-\lambda I)v=0\) always has \(v=0\). A nonzero solution exists only when \(A-\lambda I\) is singular. The lecture marks that matrix and writes the test as a determinant:

    $$\det(A-\lambda I)=0.$$

    That is the characteristic equation. For an \(n\times n\) matrix it is a polynomial of degree \(n\). The roots are the eigenvalues, counted with multiplicity. A real matrix can have complex roots. This lecture's example factors into three real linear factors, so the rest of the calculation stays in real arithmetic.

    The three steps on the slide

    Once the eigenpairs are known, the factorization is assembly, not a new idea.

    1. Find the eigenvalues from the characteristic equation, then one eigenvector for each.
    2. Put the eigenvalues on the diagonal of \(\Lambda\), and stack the matching eigenvectors as the columns of \(S\).
    3. Invert \(S\) by Gauss-Jordan elimination on the augmented matrix \([S\mid I]\). The lecture's arrow is \([S\mid I]\to[I\mid S^{-1}]\).
    $$\Lambda=\operatorname{diag}(\lambda_1,\lambda_2,\ldots,\lambda_k),\qquad S=[v_1\mid v_2\mid \cdots \mid v_k],\qquad A=S\Lambda S^{-1}.$$

    For an \(n\times n\) matrix that can be diagonalized, \(k=n\). Then \(S\) is square. The slide's \(k\) is that count, not a smaller sample of the eigenvalues.

    Column \(j\) of \(S\) must be an eigenvector for the \(\lambda_j\) sitting in diagonal entry \(j\). Swapping two eigenvalues without swapping their columns builds a different product. It is not \(A\).

    Incomplete means too few eigenvectors

    The yellow warning on the steps slide: an incomplete matrix cannot be diagonalized, because the algebraic multiplicity and the geometric multiplicity are not equal.

    Algebraic multiplicity is how many times \(\lambda\) is a root of \(\det(A-\lambda I)=0\). Geometric multiplicity is the dimension of the kernel of \(A-\lambda I\): how many independent eigenvectors that one eigenvalue has. Geometric multiplicity is never larger than algebraic multiplicity. "Not equal" on the slide therefore means the kernel is too small. You cannot find \(n\) independent eigenvectors, \(S\) is not invertible, and \(S^{-1}\) does not exist.

    The lecture defers the replacement, Jordan form, to a later video. The 3 by 3 below is not that case. A 2 by 2 that is, and that is not on the slides:

    $$B=\begin{bmatrix}1&1\\0&1\end{bmatrix},\qquad \det(B-\lambda I)=(1-\lambda)^2.$$

    The only eigenvalue is \(1\), with algebraic multiplicity \(2\). Then

    $$B-I=\begin{bmatrix}0&1\\0&0\end{bmatrix}.$$

    The kernel is the multiples of \((1,0)\). Geometric multiplicity is \(1\), not \(2\). One independent eigenvector cannot fill two columns of an invertible \(S\). \(B\) is incomplete in the lecture's sense.

    The lecture's matrix

    The example slide asks for the eigenvalue decomposition of

    $$A=\begin{bmatrix}2&0&0\\2&6&0\\3&2&1\end{bmatrix}.$$

    This matrix is lower triangular: everything above the diagonal is \(0\). That is why the first row of \(A-\lambda I\) has two zeros, which is the shortcut the lecture uses. It also means the eigenvalues are the diagonal entries \(2\), \(6\), and \(1\). Triangular does not mean already diagonal. The \(2\) and \(3\) below the diagonal still mix coordinates, so the eigenvectors are not all standard basis vectors.

    One cofactor, then three roots

    The lecture expands \(\det(A-\lambda I)\) along the first row, because two entries there are zero. Only one cofactor survives. Its sign, for position \((1,1)\), is \(+\).

    $$\det\begin{bmatrix}2-\lambda&0&0\\2&6-\lambda&0\\3&2&1-\lambda\end{bmatrix}=(2-\lambda)\det\begin{bmatrix}6-\lambda&0\\2&1-\lambda\end{bmatrix}.$$

    The 2 by 2 determinant is \((6-\lambda)(1-\lambda)-0\). The characteristic equation on the next slide is already factored:

    $$(2-\lambda)(6-\lambda)(1-\lambda)=0.$$

    So \(\lambda=2\), \(\lambda=6\), and \(\lambda=1\), in the order the lecture writes them. Each root appears once. Algebraic multiplicity is \(1\) for each, so each kernel only needs one independent vector. There is no repeated root to be deficient. The lecture still puts a checkpoint here: if any eigenvalue had been deficient, you would stop and not form \(S^{-1}\).

    One kernel vector per eigenvalue

    For each \(\lambda\), solve \((A-\lambda I)v=0\). Any nonzero vector in that kernel works. The lecture does not ask for the parameterized general solution, only one vector from the line. Scaling is free: \(2v\) is the same direction. The slides pick one integer vector per eigenvalue. A different scale changes \(S\) and \(S^{-1}\) together, and the product \(S\Lambda S^{-1}\) stays \(A\).

    \(\lambda=2\)

    The slide shows

    $$A-2I=\begin{bmatrix}0&0&0\\2&4&0\\3&2&-1\end{bmatrix}.$$

    Divide row 2 by \(2\), then clear the first column of row 3:

    $$\begin{bmatrix}0&0&0\\1&2&0\\3&2&-1\end{bmatrix}\longrightarrow\begin{bmatrix}0&0&0\\1&2&0\\0&-4&-1\end{bmatrix}.$$

    Divide the new row 3 by \(-4\), then clear the second column of row 2:

    $$\begin{bmatrix}0&0&0\\1&2&0\\0&1&1/4\end{bmatrix}\longrightarrow\begin{bmatrix}0&0&0\\1&0&-1/2\\0&1&1/4\end{bmatrix}.$$

    Row 1 is gone, so the third coordinate is free. Call it \(z\). The other two rows say \(x=z/2\) and \(y=-z/4\). The choice \(z=-4\) clears the fractions: \(x=-2\), \(y=1\). That is the slide's vector.

    $$v_1=\begin{bmatrix}-2\\1\\-4\end{bmatrix}.$$

    Check it against the definition before trusting the reduction. \(Av_1=(-4,2,-8)=2v_1\).

    \(\lambda=6\)

    The lecture skips this reduction. The matrix is

    $$A-6I=\begin{bmatrix}-4&0&0\\2&0&0\\3&2&-5\end{bmatrix}.$$

    The first row forces \(x=0\). The second row is the same condition. The third row then says \(2y-5z=0\), so \(y=(5/2)z\). The choice \(z=2\) gives \(y=5\). The slide's vector is

    $$v_2=\begin{bmatrix}0\\5\\2\end{bmatrix}.$$

    \(Av_2=(0,30,12)=6v_2\).

    \(\lambda=1\)

    Again filled in, because the lecture skips it:

    $$A-I=\begin{bmatrix}1&0&0\\2&5&0\\3&2&0\end{bmatrix}.$$

    Subtract twice row 1 from row 2, and three times row 1 from row 3. Both new rows force \(y=0\), and row 1 already forced \(x=0\). The third coordinate is free. The unit choice is the slide's vector.

    $$v_3=\begin{bmatrix}0\\0\\1\end{bmatrix}.$$

    This one is visible without much algebra. Column 3 of \(A\) is already \((0,0,1)\), so \(Ae_3=1\cdot e_3\).

    Each slide vector, checked against \(Av=\lambda v\) before \(S\) is built.
    \(\lambda\)\(v\)\(Av\)
    \(2\)\((-2,1,-4)\)\((-4,2,-8)\)
    \(6\)\((0,5,2)\)\((0,30,12)\)
    \(1\)\((0,0,1)\)\((0,0,1)\)

    Three independent vectors, one per eigenvalue. The lecture's checkpoint passes. Diagonalization can continue.

    Stack them in the same order

    The arrangement slide is explicit: the first eigenvalue pairs with the first eigenvector, the second with the second, and so on. Using the lecture's order \(\lambda=2,6,1\):

    $$\Lambda=\begin{bmatrix}2&0&0\\0&6&0\\0&0&1\end{bmatrix},\qquad S=\begin{bmatrix}-2&0&0\\1&5&0\\-4&2&1\end{bmatrix}.$$

    \(S\) is the three slide vectors placed side by side. \(\Lambda\) scales the first coordinate system axis by \(2\), the second by \(6\), and the third by \(1\). If \(\Lambda\) were \(\operatorname{diag}(6,2,1)\) while \(S\) stayed in this column order, the first column would be scaled by \(6\) in the product and by \(2\) in \(Av_1\). Those are different matrices.

    Gauss-Jordan, written out

    The lecture shows \(S^{-1}\) and says the inversion steps are elementary, so they are not on the slide. Here is the reduction that produces that inverse. Start from \([S\mid I]\).

    $$\left[\begin{array}{ccc|ccc}-2&0&0&1&0&0\\1&5&0&0&1&0\\-4&2&1&0&0&1\end{array}\right].$$

    Divide row 1 by \(-2\). Subtract the new row 1 from row 2, and add \(4\) times it to row 3:

    $$\left[\begin{array}{ccc|ccc}1&0&0&-1/2&0&0\\0&5&0&1/2&1&0\\0&2&1&-2&0&1\end{array}\right].$$

    Divide row 2 by \(5\). Subtract twice that row from row 3. Row 1 needs nothing further: its second and third entries are already \(0\).

    $$\left[\begin{array}{ccc|ccc}1&0&0&-1/2&0&0\\0&1&0&1/10&1/5&0\\0&0&1&-11/5&-2/5&1\end{array}\right].$$

    The left block is \(I\). The right block is the inverse on the slide. The last right-hand entry, \(-2-2\cdot(1/10)=-11/5\), is the one worth recomputing if the fractions start to drift.

    $$S^{-1}=\begin{bmatrix}-1/2&0&0\\1/10&1/5&0\\-11/5&-2/5&1\end{bmatrix}.$$

    The finished factorization, boxed in yellow on the last slide, is

    $$A=S\Lambda S^{-1}.$$

    Multiply back before you trust the inverse

    First form \(S\Lambda\), which scales each column of \(S\) by its eigenvalue:

    $$S\Lambda=\begin{bmatrix}-4&0&0\\2&30&0\\-8&12&1\end{bmatrix}.$$

    The bottom-left entry of \((S\Lambda)S^{-1}\) uses every term in that row:

    $$(-8)\left(-\frac12\right)+12\left(\frac{1}{10}\right)+1\left(-\frac{11}{5}\right)=4+\frac{6}{5}-\frac{11}{5}=3.$$

    That is \(A_{31}\). The other eight entries recover \(A\) the same way. One more spot check: row 2 of \(S\Lambda\) against column 1 of \(S^{-1}\) is \(2\cdot(-1/2)+30\cdot(1/10)=2\), which is \(A_{21}\).

    def matmul(a, b):
    n = len(a)
    return [[sum(a[i][k] * b[k][j] for k in range(n)) for j in range(n)] for i in range(n)]

S = [[-2, 0, 0], [1, 5, 0], [-4, 2, 1]]
Lam = [[2, 0, 0], [0, 6, 0], [0, 0, 1]]
Sinv = [[-1/2, 0, 0], [1/10, 1/5, 0], [-11/5, -2/5, 1]]
A = [[2, 0, 0], [2, 6, 0], [3, 2, 1]]
got = matmul(matmul(S, Lam), Sinv)
assert all(abs(got[i][j] - A[i][j]) < 1e-9 for i in range(3) for j in range(3))

    What the product is doing

    The lecture stops once the product is on the board. The product has a coordinate reading that makes the three steps feel like one operation.

    Let \(y=S^{-1}x\), so \(x=Sy\). The columns of \(S\) are the eigenvector axes, and \(y\) holds coordinates along those axes. Then

    $$Ax=S\Lambda S^{-1}x=S(\Lambda y).$$

    \(\Lambda y\) multiplies coordinate \(1\) by \(2\), coordinate \(2\) by \(6\), and coordinate \(3\) by \(1\). \(S\) converts that scaled coordinate vector back to the original axes. Diagonalization did not remove the mixing in \(A\). It moved the mixing into the change of coordinates, and left a diagonal scale in the middle.

    Powers fall out of the same cancellation, though the lecture does not derive them. \(A^2=S\Lambda S^{-1}S\Lambda S^{-1}=S\Lambda^2 S^{-1}\), and \(\Lambda^2=\operatorname{diag}(4,36,1)\). Each extra power only changes the diagonal. That is the usual reason to keep \(S\) and \(\Lambda\) instead of multiplying \(A\) by itself.

    Checks

    Why can you not subtract \(\lambda\) from \(A\) directly?

    \(A\) is \(n\times n\) and \(\lambda\) is a scalar. \(\lambda v=(\lambda I)v\) is the legal way to write that scale as a matrix, which is what lets you factor \(v\) out of \(Av-\lambda v\).

    The kernel of \(A-2I\) is a whole line. Why does the slide publish only one vector?

    Every nonzero vector on that line is an eigenvector for \(\lambda=2\). \(S\) needs one column from the line, not the general solution. Another scale changes \(S^{-1}\) in the opposite way. \(S\Lambda S^{-1}\) is unchanged.

    What fails if \(\Lambda=\operatorname{diag}(6,2,1)\) while the columns of \(S\) stay in the order \(v_1,v_2,v_3\)?

    The product scales \(v_1\) by \(6\). But \(Av_1=2v_1\). The two sides disagree on the first column, so the product is not \(A\).

    How do you know the lecture's \(A\) is not incomplete?

    Each eigenvalue is a simple root, and each kernel supplied an independent vector. Three independent eigenvectors in \(\mathbb{R}^3\) make \(S\) invertible. The 2 by 2 \(B\) above fails this count: algebraic multiplicity \(2\), geometric multiplicity \(1\).
