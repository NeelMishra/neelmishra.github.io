    Gram-Schmidt QR: orthonormal columns, then a triangular solve

    October 2026 · 11 min read

    Before you start: a dot product and a projection. The process, including the third vector, is playlist video 5. The square, Householder version of QR is playlist video 2.

    This is video 4 of the Matrix Decompositions playlist, QR Factorization Example by FiveMinuteLectures (10 July 2025, 4 minutes 34 seconds). The target is \(A=QR\), then a triangular system for \(Ax=b\). The Gram-Schmidt animation is not written out on the slides. The projection below lands on the \(w_2\) and the \(Q\) the lecture prints.

    Semi-orthogonal means orthonormal columns

    The definition slide writes \(Q\) as columns \(q_1,q_2,\ldots,q_n\) and asks for two things: \(q_i\cdot q_j=0\) when \(i\neq j\), and \(\lVert q_i\rVert=1\). Those two lines are the same matrix equation

    $$Q^{\mathsf T}Q=I.$$

    If \(Q\) is square, the lecture calls it orthogonal, and then \(Q^{-1}=Q^{\mathsf T}\). The factorization also allows a tall \(Q\). A 3 by 2 matrix cannot have an inverse, but it can still have orthonormal columns. That is the rectangular case the lecture calls semi-orthogonal. The identity on the right is then the small one, 2 by 2 in the example, not 3 by 3. You never multiply by \(Q^{-1}\). You multiply by \(Q^{\mathsf T}\).

    The lecture says this QR works for any size and any rank. The procedure on the slides divides by the length of each new vector. A column that is already in the span of the earlier ones leaves a zero vector, and there is nothing to divide by. The example below has independent columns, so that division is legal. A dependent column would put a zero on the diagonal of \(R\) and add no new column to \(Q\).

    The two slides, before the numbers

    Assume \(A=QR\), with the columns of \(Q\) an orthonormal basis for the column space, and \(R\) square and upper triangular. Multiply on the left by \(Q^{\mathsf T}\):

    $$Q^{\mathsf T}A=Q^{\mathsf T}QR=R.$$

    The middle \(Q^{\mathsf T}Q\) is the identity from the definition slide. That is the whole of step 2. \(R\) is not a new factorization. It is the matrix of dot products \(R_{ij}=q_i\cdot a_j\). It is upper triangular because \(a_j\) was built from \(q_1,\ldots,q_j\) only, so the later \(q_i\) are perpendicular to it.

    For the linear system, replace \(A\) by \(QR\):

    $$QRx=b,\qquad Rx=Q^{\mathsf T}b.$$

    The second line is the first multiplied on the left by \(Q^{\mathsf T}\). \(R\) is square and upper triangular, so the unknowns come out by back-substitution. The lecture compares this with LU: you still finish on a triangular system, and this construction does not swap rows.

    This is not the Householder QR from the earlier notes. There \(Q\) was a square product of reflectors. Here \(Q\) is the thin matrix whose columns are the Gram-Schmidt vectors, and \(R=Q^{\mathsf T}A\) rather than a product of reflectors times \(A\).

    The 3 by 2 matrix

    The example is

    $$A=\begin{bmatrix}1&0\\1&1\\0&-1\end{bmatrix}.$$

    Two columns, so two unknowns. The spoken "\(x\), \(y\), and \(z\)" does not match this matrix. Call the columns \(v_1=(1,1,0)\) and \(v_2=(0,1,-1)\).

    The projection the animation skips

    The slide sets \(w_1=v_1\) and writes

    $$w_2=v_2-\operatorname{proj}_{w_1}(v_2).$$

    The projection onto a vector that is not yet a unit vector is

    $$\operatorname{proj}_{w_1}(v_2)=\frac{v_2\cdot w_1}{w_1\cdot w_1}\,w_1.$$

    \(v_2\cdot w_1=1\) and \(w_1\cdot w_1=2\), so the coefficient is \(1/2\):

    $$\operatorname{proj}_{w_1}(v_2)=\frac12\begin{bmatrix}1\\1\\0\end{bmatrix}=\begin{bmatrix}1/2\\1/2\\0\end{bmatrix},\qquad w_2=\begin{bmatrix}0\\1\\-1\end{bmatrix}-\begin{bmatrix}1/2\\1/2\\0\end{bmatrix}=\begin{bmatrix}-1/2\\1/2\\-1\end{bmatrix}.$$

    That is the \(w_2\) on the animation. Normalizing is the remaining step. \(\lVert w_1\rVert=\sqrt{2}\) and \(\lVert w_2\rVert=\sqrt{1/4+1/4+1}=\sqrt{3/2}\).

    $$q_1=\frac{w_1}{\sqrt{2}}=\begin{bmatrix}1/\sqrt{2}\\1/\sqrt{2}\\0\end{bmatrix},\qquad q_2=\frac{w_2}{\sqrt{3/2}}=\begin{bmatrix}-1/\sqrt{6}\\1/\sqrt{6}\\-\sqrt{2/3}\end{bmatrix}.$$

    The third entry is \(-\sqrt{2/3}\) because \(1/\sqrt{3/2}=\sqrt{2/3}\), and \(\sqrt{2/3}=2/\sqrt{6}\), so \(-1\cdot\sqrt{2/3}=-2/\sqrt{6}\). The first entry is half of that length, with a minus sign: \((-1/2)\sqrt{2/3}=-1/\sqrt{6}\). Same arithmetic for the middle entry, with a plus. The lecture's \(Q\) is these two columns:

    $$Q=\begin{bmatrix}1/\sqrt{2}&-1/\sqrt{6}\\1/\sqrt{2}&1/\sqrt{6}\\0&-\sqrt{2/3}\end{bmatrix}.$$

    The same \(w_2\) appears if you project onto the unit vector \(q_1\) instead of \(w_1\). Then the coefficient is \(v_2\cdot q_1=1/\sqrt{2}\), and \((1/\sqrt{2})q_1=(1/2,1/2,0)\). Subtracting that from \(v_2\) is the same vector. The slide uses the un-normalized form.

    \(R=Q^{\mathsf T}A\), entry by entry

    Each entry is a dot product of a \(q_i\) with a column of \(A\).

    $$q_1\cdot v_1=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}+\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}=\sqrt{2},\qquad q_1\cdot v_2=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}.$$
    $$q_2\cdot v_1=-\frac{1}{\sqrt{6}}+\frac{1}{\sqrt{6}}=0,\qquad q_2\cdot v_2=\frac{1}{\sqrt{6}}+\sqrt{\frac{2}{3}}=\frac{3}{\sqrt{6}}.$$

    The last sum is \(1/\sqrt{6}+2/\sqrt{6}\), because \(\sqrt{2/3}=2/\sqrt{6}\). So

    $$R=\begin{bmatrix}\sqrt{2}&1/\sqrt{2}\\0&3/\sqrt{6}\end{bmatrix},$$

    which is the matrix on the slide. The zero under the first pivot is the Gram-Schmidt step: \(q_2\) was built to be perpendicular to \(v_1\).

    Two checks before using it. \(Q^{\mathsf T}Q=I_2\): each column has length 1, and \(q_1\cdot q_2=-1/\sqrt{12}+1/\sqrt{12}=0\). And \(QR=A\): the first column is \(\sqrt{2}\,q_1=(1,1,0)\). The second is \((1/\sqrt{2})q_1+(3/\sqrt{6})q_2=(0,1,-1)\).

    Back-substitution, on a right-hand side that \(A\) can hit

    The last slide is \(Rx=Q^{\mathsf T}b\). No numeric \(b\) is printed. \(A\) is 3 by 2, so a random \(b\) in \(\mathbb{R}^3\) need not be \(Ax\) for any \(x\). The formula still returns the \(x\) whose residual is perpendicular to the columns. It is an exact solution only when \(b\) is in the column space.

    One such \(b\), not on the slide, is \(A(1,1)^{\mathsf T}=(1,2,-1)\). Then the right-hand side should come back as \((1,1)\).

    $$Q^{\mathsf T}b=\begin{bmatrix}3/\sqrt{2}\\3/\sqrt{6}\end{bmatrix}.$$

    The second row of \(R\) has only the corner entry:

    $$\frac{3}{\sqrt{6}}x_2=\frac{3}{\sqrt{6}}\quad\Rightarrow\quad x_2=1.$$

    The first row is \(\sqrt{2}\,x_1+(1/\sqrt{2})x_2=3/\sqrt{2}\). Put in \(x_2=1\):

    $$\sqrt{2}\,x_1=\frac{3}{\sqrt{2}}-\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}=\sqrt{2}\quad\Rightarrow\quad x_1=1.$$

    That is back-substitution. Start at the bottom pivot, then clear the known term from the row above. For a larger \(R\) you walk up the diagonal the same way. No row swaps.

    from math import sqrt
A = [[1, 0], [1, 1], [0, -1]]
Q = [[1/sqrt(2), -1/sqrt(6)], [1/sqrt(2), 1/sqrt(6)], [0, -sqrt(2/3)]]
R = [[sqrt(2), 1/sqrt(2)], [0, 3/sqrt(6)]]
# QR should return A
got = [[Q[i][0]*R[0][j] + Q[i][1]*R[1][j] for j in range(2)] for i in range(3)]
assert all(abs(got[i][j] - A[i][j]) < 1e-9 for i in range(3) for j in range(2))

    Checks

    Why is the identity \(Q^{\mathsf T}Q=I\) only 2 by 2 here?

    \(Q\) has two orthonormal columns in \(\mathbb{R}^3\). The product \(Q^{\mathsf T}Q\) compares those columns with each other. It does not say the two columns span all of \(\mathbb{R}^3\).

    Where does the \(-1/2\) in \(w_2\) come from?

    \(v_2\cdot w_1=1\) and \(w_1\cdot w_1=2\), so the projection is half of \(w_1\), which is \((1/2,1/2,0)\). Subtract that from \((0,1,-1)\).

    Why is \(R_{21}=0\) without multiplying the matrices?

    \(q_2\) was built perpendicular to \(v_1\). The entry \(R_{21}\) is that dot product.

    Can you left-multiply \(QRx=b\) by \(Q^{-1}\)?

    Not in this example. \(Q\) is 3 by 2. The legal left factor is \(Q^{\mathsf T}\), and it produces a 2 by 2 system.
