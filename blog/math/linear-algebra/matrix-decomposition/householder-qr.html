    Householder QR: the reflector, then Q and R

    October 2026 · 22 min read

    Before you start: the dot product, the transpose rule \((AB)^{\mathsf T}=B^{\mathsf T}A^{\mathsf T}\), and why \(I\) appears when a scalar multiplies a vector. This page is two lectures. The definition is what the QR calculation recalls.

    Playlist videos 2 and 3 are one factorization. Video 3 is QR factorization with Householder Matrices Pt 1 (27 July 2025, 5 minutes 23 seconds). It defines \(H\) and proves it is its own inverse. Video 2 is QR Factorization with Householder Matrix Pt 2 (21 August 2025, 7 minutes 19 seconds). It chooses \(u\) and builds \(Q\) and \(R\). Video 3 was uploaded first. In the playlist it is the later of the two. The slides derive the unit vector, sketch how later columns are reduced, and finish a 2 by 2. The description links a 3 by 3 writeup. Both are worked below. One slide writes a denominator of \(5\) where the later matrix needs \(\sqrt{5}\).

    The formula, and the unit-vector mark

    The definition slide highlights \(u\) and adds one condition: \(u\) is a unit vector. \(H\) is square, the same size as the space \(u\) lives in. If \(u\) is \(n\times 1\), then \(uu^{\mathsf T}\) is \(n\times n\), and so is \(H\).

    $$H=I-2uu^{\mathsf T},\qquad u^{\mathsf T}u=1.$$

    \(uu^{\mathsf T}\) is an outer product, not a dot product. The dot product is the scalar \(u^{\mathsf T}u\). Swapping those two is the usual way to break the formula. Replacing \(u\) by \(-u\) does not change \(H\), because \((-u)(-u)^{\mathsf T}=uu^{\mathsf T}\). The length condition does. If \(u^{\mathsf T}u\neq 1\), the cancellation in the orthogonality proof below leaves a leftover multiple of \(uu^{\mathsf T}\), and \(H\) is not a reflection.

    Symmetry, in the order on the slide

    Symmetric means \(H^{\mathsf T}=H\). Transpose the definition. The transpose passes through the sum, and \(I^{\mathsf T}=I\):

    $$H^{\mathsf T}=I^{\mathsf T}-2(uu^{\mathsf T})^{\mathsf T}=I-2(uu^{\mathsf T})^{\mathsf T}.$$

    The yellow rule on the slide is \((AB)^{\mathsf T}=B^{\mathsf T}A^{\mathsf T}\). Apply it to the product \(u\,u^{\mathsf T}\):

    $$(uu^{\mathsf T})^{\mathsf T}=(u^{\mathsf T})^{\mathsf T}u^{\mathsf T}=uu^{\mathsf T}.$$

    The outer product is unchanged, so the transpose of \(H\) is \(H\) again.

    $$H^{\mathsf T}=I-2uu^{\mathsf T}=H.$$

    That is the last line of the symmetry proof. No entry of \(u\) was needed, only the transpose rule and \(I^{\mathsf T}=I\).

    Orthogonal means square, with \(Q^{\mathsf T}Q=I\)

    The lecture's definition of an orthogonal matrix is the implication on the slide:

    $$Q^{\mathsf T}Q=I\quad\Rightarrow\quad Q^{-1}=Q^{\mathsf T}.$$

    \(Q\) has to be square for that inverse to exist. The lecture calls the rectangular version semi-orthogonal: a tall matrix can still satisfy \(Q^{\mathsf T}Q=I\), which means its columns are orthonormal, but it has no inverse, and \(QQ^{\mathsf T}\) is not \(I\). A Householder matrix is the square case. Once \(H^{\mathsf T}H=I\), the slide's implication gives \(H^{-1}=H^{\mathsf T}\). Combined with symmetry, that inverse is \(H\) itself.

    The cancellation that uses \(u^{\mathsf T}u=1\)

    Because \(H^{\mathsf T}=H\), the product \(H^{\mathsf T}H\) is the square of \(I-2uu^{\mathsf T}\). Expand it the way the slide does, before combining like terms:

    $$H^{\mathsf T}H=(I-2uu^{\mathsf T})(I-2uu^{\mathsf T})=I-2uu^{\mathsf T}-2uu^{\mathsf T}+4uu^{\mathsf T}uu^{\mathsf T}.$$
    $$H^{\mathsf T}H=I-4uu^{\mathsf T}+4uu^{\mathsf T}uu^{\mathsf T}.$$

    The yellow mark is the middle factor of the last term. Associate \(uu^{\mathsf T}uu^{\mathsf T}\) as \(u(u^{\mathsf T}u)u^{\mathsf T}\). The unit-vector hypothesis replaces \(u^{\mathsf T}u\) by \(1\):

    $$uu^{\mathsf T}uu^{\mathsf T}=u(u^{\mathsf T}u)u^{\mathsf T}=uu^{\mathsf T}.$$

    Then \(4uu^{\mathsf T}uu^{\mathsf T}=4uu^{\mathsf T}\), and those two terms cancel.

    $$H^{\mathsf T}H=I-4uu^{\mathsf T}+4uu^{\mathsf T}=I.$$

    So \(H\) is orthogonal. With the symmetry line, \(H^{-1}=H^{\mathsf T}=H\). If the length were \(c\neq 1\) instead, the same association would give \(uu^{\mathsf T}uu^{\mathsf T}=c\,uu^{\mathsf T}\), and the remainder would be \(4(c-1)uu^{\mathsf T}\), not zero. The unit-vector mark is the whole proof.

    What the picture is doing

    The geometry slide draws \(u\), a dotted line labeled \(u^{\perp}\), and a right angle where they meet. \(u\) is the normal. The dotted line is the mirror: every direction perpendicular to \(u\). A red vector \(v\) is sent to \(Hv\), the reflection of \(v\) across that mirror.

    Split \(v\) into the part along the normal and the part in the mirror.

    $$v_{\parallel}=(u\cdot v)\,u,\qquad v_{\perp}=v-v_{\parallel}.$$

    Reflection keeps \(v_{\perp}\) and reverses \(v_{\parallel}\):

    $$Hv=v_{\perp}-v_{\parallel}=v-2(u\cdot v)\,u=(I-2uu^{\mathsf T})v.$$

    Two cases check the picture without a drawing. If \(v\) already lies in the mirror, \(u\cdot v=0\), so \(Hv=v\). The mirror is fixed. If \(v\) is parallel to \(u\), say \(v=cu\), then \(Hv=cu-2cu=-cu\). The normal is flipped. Every other vector is those two pieces added, so it lands on the other side of the mirror at the same distance. Length is preserved because \(H\) is orthogonal: \(\lVert Hv\rVert^2=v^{\mathsf T}H^{\mathsf T}Hv=v^{\mathsf T}v\).

    One unit vector, checked by hand

    The lecture does not pick numbers. This check uses \(u=(3/5,4/5)\), which has length \(1\) because \(9+16=25\).

    $$uu^{\mathsf T}=\frac{1}{25}\begin{bmatrix}9&12\\12&16\end{bmatrix},\qquad H=I-2uu^{\mathsf T}=\begin{bmatrix}7/25&-24/25\\-24/25&-7/25\end{bmatrix}.$$

    \(H\) is visibly symmetric. Its first column is \(He_1=(7/25,-24/25)\). The parallel test: \(Hu=u-2u=-u\), so

    $$H\begin{bmatrix}3/5\\4/5\end{bmatrix}=\begin{bmatrix}-3/5\\-4/5\end{bmatrix}.$$

    And \(H^{\mathsf T}H=I\) on this matrix: the two columns each have squared length \((49+576)/625=1\), and their dot product is \((-168+168)/625=0\).

    u = [3/5, 4/5]
H = [[7/25, -24/25], [-24/25, -7/25]]
# Hu should be -u
got = [H[0][0]*u[0] + H[0][1]*u[1], H[1][0]*u[0] + H[1][1]*u[1]]
assert all(abs(got[i] + u[i]) < 1e-12 for i in range(2))

    A product of orthogonal matrices is orthogonal

    The last slide of the definition lecture is the fact the QR lecture assumes. If each \(Q_i\) is orthogonal and

    $$Q=Q_1 Q_2\cdots Q_n,$$

    the transpose reverses the product, which is the same \((AB)^{\mathsf T}=B^{\mathsf T}A^{\mathsf T}\) rule used above:

    $$Q^{\mathsf T}=Q_n^{\mathsf T}\cdots Q_2^{\mathsf T}Q_1^{\mathsf T}.$$

    Multiply. The inner pair is \(Q_1^{\mathsf T}Q_1=I\), then the next pair, until nothing is left but \(I\). For two factors the cancellation is short enough to write in full:

    $$(Q_1 Q_2)^{\mathsf T}(Q_1 Q_2)=Q_2^{\mathsf T}Q_1^{\mathsf T}Q_1 Q_2=Q_2^{\mathsf T}Q_2=I.$$

    The slide's picture of the general case is that same collapse, with \(I\) sitting in the middle: \(Q^{\mathsf T}Q=Q_n^{\mathsf T}\cdots I\cdots Q_n\), and then that \(I\) eats the neighbors. The conclusion on the last frame is \(Q^{\mathsf T}Q=I\).

    Householder QR uses this with each \(Q_i\) equal to a reflector. The channel's next video sets

    $$A=QR,\qquad Q=H_1 H_2\cdots H_n.$$

    Each \(H_i\) is orthogonal by the cancellation above, so the product \(Q\) is orthogonal by this last proof. The QR lecture writes \(k\) factors in the reduction \(H_k\cdots H_1 A=R\). Undoing that reduction puts \(H_1\cdots H_k\) in front of \(R\). Choosing each \(u\), and the 2 by 2 where \(Q\) is a single \(H\), is below.

    Row-reduce with matrices you can invert

    Video 2 starts from the formula above. The target is a factorization \(A=QR\). \(Q\) is orthogonal, so \(Q^{\mathsf T}Q=I\). \(R\) has zeros strictly below the diagonal. The lecture calls that row echelon form. In the examples the pivots stay as lengths, not as leading ones, so this is not reduced row echelon form. Nobody divides a row to force a pivot of \(1\).

    Ordinary elimination would also make those zeros, but the elimination matrix is not orthogonal, and inverting it is a separate calculation. A Householder reflector is orthogonal and symmetric, so its inverse is itself. That is the yellow line on the last slide of this lecture, \(H^{-1}=H^{\mathsf T}=H\), which is the cancellation above. It is why the lecture can invert a reflector without Gauss-Jordan. The plan is to left-multiply \(A\) by a string of those reflectors until the product is \(R\), then undo the reflectors to recover \(A\).

    $$H_k\cdots H_2 H_1 A=R,\qquad A=H_1 H_2\cdots H_k R.$$

    The second line is the first line multiplied on the left by \(H_1\), then \(H_2\), and so on, using \(H_i^2=I\). So \(Q=H_1 H_2\cdots H_k\), in that order. For the 2 by 2 there is only one reflector, and \(Q=H\).

    On the grid, reflecting the first column \(a_1\) across the mirror lands it on the positive horizontal axis. Call that image \(Ha_1\). Any orthogonal matrix preserves length: \(\lVert Qx\rVert^2=x^{\mathsf T}Q^{\mathsf T}Qx=x^{\mathsf T}x\). The lecture uses that once. The reflected column has the same length as \(a_1\), so the vector on the axis is not an arbitrary \((1,0,\ldots)\). It is

    $$Ha_1=\lVert a_1\rVert e_1.$$

    \(e_1=(1,0,\ldots,0)\). The lecture fixes the positive axis. Reflecting onto \(-\lVert a_1\rVert e_1\) is another legal reflector. It flips the sign of that pivot in \(R\) and the sign of a column of \(Q\). This video does not use that choice.

    Where \(u\) comes from

    Ignore the rest of \(A\) for a moment and ask only that \(H\) send \(a_1\) to \(\lVert a_1\rVert e_1\).

    $$(I-2uu^{\mathsf T})a_1=\lVert a_1\rVert e_1.$$

    \(u^{\mathsf T}a_1\) is the dot product \(u\cdot a_1\), a scalar, so it can slide past \(u\):

    $$a_1-2(u\cdot a_1)u=\lVert a_1\rVert e_1,$$
    $$a_1-\lVert a_1\rVert e_1=2(u\cdot a_1)\,u.$$

    The left side is known. The right side is a scalar times \(u\). The two sides are parallel, which is the "same direction" mark on the slide. \(u\) is a unit vector, so take the left side and divide by its length. That is the boxed formula:

    $$u=\frac{a_1-\lVert a_1\rVert e_1}{\lVert a_1-\lVert a_1\rVert e_1\rVert}.$$

    This choice points the same way as \(a_1-\lVert a_1\rVert e_1\), so the scalar \(2(u\cdot a_1)\) comes out positive and the parallel-vector step does not flip \(u\). If that difference is the zero vector, \(a_1\) is already on the positive axis and no reflector is needed.

    The 2 by 2, with the denominator corrected

    The example matrix and its first column are

    $$A=\begin{bmatrix}3&2\\4&1\end{bmatrix},\qquad a_1=\begin{bmatrix}3\\4\end{bmatrix}.$$

    \(\lVert a_1\rVert=\sqrt{9+16}=5\), so \(\lVert a_1\rVert e_1=(5,0)\) and

    $$a_1-\lVert a_1\rVert e_1=\begin{bmatrix}-2\\4\end{bmatrix},\qquad \lVert a_1-\lVert a_1\rVert e_1\rVert=\sqrt{4+16}=\sqrt{20}=2\sqrt{5}.$$
    $$u=\frac{1}{2\sqrt{5}}\begin{bmatrix}-2\\4\end{bmatrix}=\begin{bmatrix}-1/\sqrt{5}\\2/\sqrt{5}\end{bmatrix}.$$

    The unit-vector slide writes \(u=(-1/5,2/5)\). That vector has length \(1/\sqrt{5}\), not \(1\). The \(H\) multiplied on the next slide is the reflector for \((-1/\sqrt{5},2/\sqrt{5})\), not for the printed fifths. Use the square root.

    Now the outer product. \(uu^{\mathsf T}=(1/5)\begin{bmatrix}1&-2\\-2&4\end{bmatrix}\), so

    $$2uu^{\mathsf T}=\begin{bmatrix}2/5&-4/5\\-4/5&8/5\end{bmatrix},\qquad H=I-2uu^{\mathsf T}=\begin{bmatrix}3/5&4/5\\4/5&-3/5\end{bmatrix}.$$

    That is the matrix in the product on the "multiply" slide. Check the first column before trusting it:

    $$Ha_1=\begin{bmatrix}3/5&4/5\\4/5&-3/5\end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix}3\\4\end{bmatrix}=\begin{bmatrix}5\\0\end{bmatrix}.$$

    The second entry is zero, as the lecture says, and the first entry is the length \(5\). The whole product is

    $$HA=\begin{bmatrix}3/5&4/5\\4/5&-3/5\end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix}3&2\\4&1\end{bmatrix}=\begin{bmatrix}5&2\\0&1\end{bmatrix}.$$

    The second column was not asked to become a multiple of \(e_2\). One reflector only clears below the first pivot. For a 2 by 2, that is the whole job. \(R=HA\).

    Undo \(H\) to name \(Q\)

    From \(HA=R\), multiply on the left by \(H\). The yellow identity \(H^{-1}=H^{\mathsf T}=H\) turns that into \(A=HR\). With one reflector, the lecture sets \(Q=H\):

    $$A=QR,\qquad Q=\begin{bmatrix}3/5&4/5\\4/5&-3/5\end{bmatrix},\qquad R=\begin{bmatrix}5&2\\0&1\end{bmatrix}.$$

    Two checks. The columns of \(Q\) are unit length and perpendicular: \((3/5)^2+(4/5)^2=1\), and \((3/5)(4/5)+(4/5)(-3/5)=0\). And \(QR\) returns \(A\): the first column is \(5\) times column 1 of \(Q\), which is \((3,4)\). The second column is \(2(3/5,4/5)+1(4/5,-3/5)=(2,1)\).

    def matmul(a, b):
    n = len(a)
    return [[sum(a[i][k] * b[k][j] for k in range(n)) for j in range(n)] for i in range(n)]

Q = [[3/5, 4/5], [4/5, -3/5]]
R = [[5, 2], [0, 1]]
A = [[3, 2], [4, 1]]
got = matmul(Q, R)
assert all(abs(got[i][j] - A[i][j]) < 1e-9 for i in range(2) for j in range(2))

    A larger square matrix needs more reflectors, then \(Q\) is their product in the order above, not the single \(H\). A rectangular matrix uses the same reflectors. They are square of the row dimension. \(R\) is the same shape as \(A\), with zeros below the diagonal. The lecture does not form a thin \(Q\).

    Later columns use a smaller reflector

    After \(H_1\), the first column is \(\lVert a_1\rVert e_1\). The trailing block, rows and columns \(2\) through the end, is treated as a new matrix. Build its reflector from its first column by the same formula, then embed it:

    $$\begin{bmatrix}1&0\\0&H\end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix}\lVert a_1\rVert&\tilde a_{12}&\cdots\\0&\tilde a_{22}&\cdots\\0&\tilde a_{32}&\cdots\end{bmatrix}.$$

    The \(1\) and the zeros around it are the point of the embedding. They leave the first row and the first column alone, so the zero already made in position \((2,1)\) is not filled back in. The small \(H\) only mixes the trailing entries. Repeat until nothing below the diagonal remains. Some frames label the first pivot \(\lVert a_2\rVert\) after the second reflector. That label does not match the construction: the first pivot stays \(\lVert a_1\rVert\). The new length appears on the next diagonal.

    The linked 3 by 3

    The description's writeup uses the same positive-axis formula, on

    $$A=\begin{bmatrix}12&-51&4\\6&167&-68\\-4&24&-41\end{bmatrix}.$$

    First column \(x=(12,6,-4)\), and \(\lVert x\rVert=\sqrt{144+36+16}=14\). Then \(x-14e_1=(-2,6,-4)\), whose length is \(2\sqrt{14}\), so

    $$u_1=\frac{(-1,3,-2)}{\sqrt{14}},\qquad H_1=\begin{bmatrix}6/7&3/7&-2/7\\3/7&-2/7&6/7\\-2/7&6/7&3/7\end{bmatrix}.$$

    The first column check is \(H_1 x=(14,0,0)\). The full product, matching the writeup, is

    $$A_1=H_1 A=\begin{bmatrix}14&21&-14\\0&-49&-14\\0&168&-77\end{bmatrix}.$$

    The trailing column is \((-49,168)\). Its length is \(\sqrt{49^2+168^2}=175\). The same formula gives a unit vector this time with no leftover square root:

    $$u_2=\begin{bmatrix}-4/5\\3/5\end{bmatrix},\qquad \tilde H_2=\begin{bmatrix}-7/25&24/25\\24/25&7/25\end{bmatrix}.$$

    Embed it, as on the subsequent-columns slide:

    $$H_2=\begin{bmatrix}1&0&0\\0&-7/25&24/25\\0&24/25&7/25\end{bmatrix}.$$

    \(H_2\) does not touch the first row. Applied to \(A_1\), it replaces \((-49,168)\) with \((175,0)\) and leaves the \(14\) alone. The result is upper triangular:

    $$R=H_2 H_1 A=\begin{bmatrix}14&21&-14\\0&175&-70\\0&0&-35\end{bmatrix}.$$

    The last diagonal entry is \(-35\). Nothing is wrong with that. No third reflector was built for a 1 by 1 block, so that entry is whatever the second reflector did to the third column. It does not have to be a positive length.

    Undo in reverse order: \(A=H_1 H_2 R\), so \(Q=H_1 H_2\), not \(H_2 H_1\).

    $$Q=\begin{bmatrix}6/7&-69/175&58/175\\3/7&158/175&-6/175\\-2/7&6/35&33/35\end{bmatrix}.$$

    \(Q^{\mathsf T}Q=I\), and \(QR=A\). Those two products were checked entry by entry against the matrices above. The writeup's closing line is the same check: \(Q^{\mathsf T}Q=I\) and \(Q^{\mathsf T}A=R\).

    Checks

    Which step of the symmetry proof uses \((AB)^{\mathsf T}=B^{\mathsf T}A^{\mathsf T}\)?

    The outer product. \((uu^{\mathsf T})^{\mathsf T}=(u^{\mathsf T})^{\mathsf T}u^{\mathsf T}=uu^{\mathsf T}\). After that, \(H^{\mathsf T}\) is the same expression as \(H\).

    Where does \(u^{\mathsf T}u=1\) enter the orthogonality proof?

    Only in \(uu^{\mathsf T}uu^{\mathsf T}=u(u^{\mathsf T}u)u^{\mathsf T}\). That replaces the quartic term by \(uu^{\mathsf T}\), which cancels \(-4uu^{\mathsf T}\). If the length is not 1, those terms do not cancel.

    What happens to a vector that is already perpendicular to \(u\)?

    It lies in the mirror, so \(u\cdot v=0\) and \(Hv=v\). The formula does not move the mirror.

    Why does \(Q=H_1 H_2\) satisfy \(Q^{\mathsf T}Q=I\) without multiplying the reflectors out?

    \(Q^{\mathsf T}=H_2^{\mathsf T}H_1^{\mathsf T}=H_2 H_1\), and \(H_1^{\mathsf T}H_1=I\), so the product collapses to \(H_2^{\mathsf T}H_2=I\). Each factor only needs to be orthogonal on its own.

    Why is \(Ha_1\) equal to \(\lVert a_1\rVert e_1\), not just \(e_1\)?

    \(H\) is orthogonal, so it cannot change length. The reflected column has to keep length \(\lVert a_1\rVert\). Putting a \(1\) in the pivot would be a different, non-orthogonal, row operation.

    The slide prints \(u=(-1/5,2/5)\) for \(a_1=(3,4)\). What should the denominator be?

    \(\sqrt{5}\), not \(5\). The difference \(a_1-5e_1=(-2,4)\) has length \(2\sqrt{5}\), so the unit vector is \((-1/\sqrt{5},2/\sqrt{5})\). That is the \(u\) whose reflector is the \(H\) on the next slide.

    For two reflectors, why is \(Q=H_1 H_2\) rather than \(H_2 H_1\)?

    The reduction is \(H_2 H_1 A=R\). Each reflector is its own inverse, so clearing them means multiplying on the left by \(H_1\) and then by \(H_2\). The product in front of \(R\) is \(H_1 H_2\).

    What does the \(1\) in the corner of the embedded reflector protect?

    The first column has already been reduced to a multiple of \(e_1\). A full-size reflector built from a later column would mix that column again. The identity block leaves those zeros in place.
