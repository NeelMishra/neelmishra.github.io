    Householder QR: reflect columns, then read off Q and R

    October 2026 · 15 min read

    Before you start: the dot product, a matrix transpose, and why \(I\) appears when a scalar multiplies a vector. The definition and the two proofs are in playlist video 3. This lecture recalls the formula and uses it.

    This is video 2 of the Matrix Decompositions playlist, QR Factorization with Householder Matrix Pt 2 by FiveMinuteLectures (21 August 2025, 7 minutes 19 seconds). The slides derive the unit vector, sketch how later columns are reduced, and finish a 2 by 2. The description links a 3 by 3 writeup. Both are worked below. One slide writes a denominator of \(5\) where the later matrix needs \(\sqrt{5}\). The note uses the vector that actually produces that matrix.

    Row-reduce with matrices you can invert

    The target is a factorization \(A=QR\). \(Q\) is orthogonal, so \(Q^{\mathsf T}Q=I\). \(R\) has zeros strictly below the diagonal. The lecture calls that row echelon form. In the examples the pivots stay as lengths, not as leading ones, so this is not reduced row echelon form. Nobody divides a row to force a pivot of \(1\).

    Ordinary elimination would also make those zeros, but the elimination matrix is not orthogonal, and inverting it is a separate calculation. A Householder reflector is orthogonal and symmetric, so its inverse is itself. The lecture's plan is to left-multiply \(A\) by a string of those reflectors until the product is \(R\), then undo the reflectors to recover \(A\).

    $$H_k\cdots H_2 H_1 A=R,\qquad A=H_1 H_2\cdots H_k R.$$

    The second line is the first line multiplied on the left by \(H_1\), then \(H_2\), and so on, using \(H_i^2=I\). So \(Q=H_1 H_2\cdots H_k\), in that order. For the 2 by 2 there is only one reflector, and \(Q=H\).

    The reflector on the slide

    The recalled formula, with the unit vector highlighted, is

    $$H=I-2uu^{\mathsf T},\qquad \lVert u\rVert=1.$$

    \(uu^{\mathsf T}\) is the outer product. If \(u\) is \(m\times 1\), then \(uu^{\mathsf T}\) and \(H\) are \(m\times m\). The geometry on the grid: \(u\) is the normal to a mirror line, drawn as the dotted \(u^{\perp}\). Reflecting the first column \(a_1\) across that mirror lands it on the positive horizontal axis. Call that image \(Ha_1\).

    \(H\) is symmetric because \((uu^{\mathsf T})^{\mathsf T}=uu^{\mathsf T}\). It is also orthogonal. Expand the square, and use \(u^{\mathsf T}u=1\), so \(uu^{\mathsf T}uu^{\mathsf T}=uu^{\mathsf T}\):

    $$H^2=(I-2uu^{\mathsf T})^2=I-4uu^{\mathsf T}+4uu^{\mathsf T}=I.$$

    Therefore \(H^{-1}=H^{\mathsf T}=H\). That is the yellow line on the last slide. It is why the lecture can invert a reflector without Gauss-Jordan.

    Any orthogonal matrix preserves length: \(\lVert Qx\rVert^2=x^{\mathsf T}Q^{\mathsf T}Qx=x^{\mathsf T}x\). The lecture uses that once. The reflected column has the same length as \(a_1\), so the vector on the axis is not an arbitrary \((1,0,\ldots)\). It is

    $$Ha_1=\lVert a_1\rVert e_1.$$

    \(e_1=(1,0,\ldots,0)\). The lecture fixes the positive axis. Reflecting onto \(-\lVert a_1\rVert e_1\) is another legal reflector. It flips the sign of that pivot in \(R\) and the sign of a column of \(Q\). This video does not use that choice.

    Where \(u\) comes from

    Ignore the rest of \(A\) for a moment and ask only that \(H\) send \(a_1\) to \(\lVert a_1\rVert e_1\).

    $$(I-2uu^{\mathsf T})a_1=\lVert a_1\rVert e_1.$$

    \(u^{\mathsf T}a_1\) is the dot product \(u\cdot a_1\), a scalar, so it can slide past \(u\):

    $$a_1-2(u\cdot a_1)u=\lVert a_1\rVert e_1,$$
    $$a_1-\lVert a_1\rVert e_1=2(u\cdot a_1)\,u.$$

    The left side is known. The right side is a scalar times \(u\). The two sides are parallel, which is the "same direction" mark on the slide. \(u\) is a unit vector, so take the left side and divide by its length. That is the boxed formula:

    $$u=\frac{a_1-\lVert a_1\rVert e_1}{\lVert a_1-\lVert a_1\rVert e_1\rVert}.$$

    This choice points the same way as \(a_1-\lVert a_1\rVert e_1\), so the scalar \(2(u\cdot a_1)\) comes out positive and the parallel-vector step does not flip \(u\). If that difference is the zero vector, \(a_1\) is already on the positive axis and no reflector is needed.

    The 2 by 2, with the denominator corrected

    The example matrix and its first column are

    $$A=\begin{bmatrix}3&2\\4&1\end{bmatrix},\qquad a_1=\begin{bmatrix}3\\4\end{bmatrix}.$$

    \(\lVert a_1\rVert=\sqrt{9+16}=5\), so \(\lVert a_1\rVert e_1=(5,0)\) and

    $$a_1-\lVert a_1\rVert e_1=\begin{bmatrix}-2\\4\end{bmatrix},\qquad \lVert a_1-\lVert a_1\rVert e_1\rVert=\sqrt{4+16}=\sqrt{20}=2\sqrt{5}.$$
    $$u=\frac{1}{2\sqrt{5}}\begin{bmatrix}-2\\4\end{bmatrix}=\begin{bmatrix}-1/\sqrt{5}\\2/\sqrt{5}\end{bmatrix}.$$

    The unit-vector slide writes \(u=(-1/5,2/5)\). That vector has length \(1/\sqrt{5}\), not \(1\). The \(H\) multiplied on the next slide is the reflector for \((-1/\sqrt{5},2/\sqrt{5})\), not for the printed fifths. Use the square root.

    Now the outer product. \(uu^{\mathsf T}=(1/5)\begin{bmatrix}1&-2\\-2&4\end{bmatrix}\), so

    $$2uu^{\mathsf T}=\begin{bmatrix}2/5&-4/5\\-4/5&8/5\end{bmatrix},\qquad H=I-2uu^{\mathsf T}=\begin{bmatrix}3/5&4/5\\4/5&-3/5\end{bmatrix}.$$

    That is the matrix in the product on the "multiply" slide. Check the first column before trusting it:

    $$Ha_1=\begin{bmatrix}3/5&4/5\\4/5&-3/5\end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix}3\\4\end{bmatrix}=\begin{bmatrix}5\\0\end{bmatrix}.$$

    The second entry is zero, as the lecture says, and the first entry is the length \(5\). The whole product is

    $$HA=\begin{bmatrix}3/5&4/5\\4/5&-3/5\end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix}3&2\\4&1\end{bmatrix}=\begin{bmatrix}5&2\\0&1\end{bmatrix}.$$

    The second column was not asked to become a multiple of \(e_2\). One reflector only clears below the first pivot. For a 2 by 2, that is the whole job. \(R=HA\).

    Undo \(H\) to name \(Q\)

    From \(HA=R\), multiply on the left by \(H\). The yellow identity \(H^{-1}=H^{\mathsf T}=H\) turns that into \(A=HR\). With one reflector, the lecture sets \(Q=H\):

    $$A=QR,\qquad Q=\begin{bmatrix}3/5&4/5\\4/5&-3/5\end{bmatrix},\qquad R=\begin{bmatrix}5&2\\0&1\end{bmatrix}.$$

    Two checks. The columns of \(Q\) are unit length and perpendicular: \((3/5)^2+(4/5)^2=1\), and \((3/5)(4/5)+(4/5)(-3/5)=0\). And \(QR\) returns \(A\): the first column is \(5\) times column 1 of \(Q\), which is \((3,4)\). The second column is \(2(3/5,4/5)+1(4/5,-3/5)=(2,1)\).

    def matmul(a, b):
    n = len(a)
    return [[sum(a[i][k] * b[k][j] for k in range(n)) for j in range(n)] for i in range(n)]

Q = [[3/5, 4/5], [4/5, -3/5]]
R = [[5, 2], [0, 1]]
A = [[3, 2], [4, 1]]
got = matmul(Q, R)
assert all(abs(got[i][j] - A[i][j]) < 1e-9 for i in range(2) for j in range(2))

    A larger square matrix needs more reflectors, then \(Q\) is their product in the order above, not the single \(H\). A rectangular matrix uses the same reflectors. They are square of the row dimension. \(R\) is the same shape as \(A\), with zeros below the diagonal. The lecture does not form a thin \(Q\).

    Later columns use a smaller reflector

    After \(H_1\), the first column is \(\lVert a_1\rVert e_1\). The trailing block, rows and columns \(2\) through the end, is treated as a new matrix. Build its reflector from its first column by the same formula, then embed it:

    $$\begin{bmatrix}1&0\\0&H\end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix}\lVert a_1\rVert&\tilde a_{12}&\cdots\\0&\tilde a_{22}&\cdots\\0&\tilde a_{32}&\cdots\end{bmatrix}.$$

    The \(1\) and the zeros around it are the point of the embedding. They leave the first row and the first column alone, so the zero already made in position \((2,1)\) is not filled back in. The small \(H\) only mixes the trailing entries. Repeat until nothing below the diagonal remains. Some frames label the first pivot \(\lVert a_2\rVert\) after the second reflector. That label does not match the construction: the first pivot stays \(\lVert a_1\rVert\). The new length appears on the next diagonal.

    The linked 3 by 3

    The description's writeup uses the same positive-axis formula, on

    $$A=\begin{bmatrix}12&-51&4\\6&167&-68\\-4&24&-41\end{bmatrix}.$$

    First column \(x=(12,6,-4)\), and \(\lVert x\rVert=\sqrt{144+36+16}=14\). Then \(x-14e_1=(-2,6,-4)\), whose length is \(2\sqrt{14}\), so

    $$u_1=\frac{(-1,3,-2)}{\sqrt{14}},\qquad H_1=\begin{bmatrix}6/7&3/7&-2/7\\3/7&-2/7&6/7\\-2/7&6/7&3/7\end{bmatrix}.$$

    The first column check is \(H_1 x=(14,0,0)\). The full product, matching the writeup, is

    $$A_1=H_1 A=\begin{bmatrix}14&21&-14\\0&-49&-14\\0&168&-77\end{bmatrix}.$$

    The trailing column is \((-49,168)\). Its length is \(\sqrt{49^2+168^2}=175\). The same formula gives a unit vector this time with no leftover square root:

    $$u_2=\begin{bmatrix}-4/5\\3/5\end{bmatrix},\qquad \tilde H_2=\begin{bmatrix}-7/25&24/25\\24/25&7/25\end{bmatrix}.$$

    Embed it, as on the subsequent-columns slide:

    $$H_2=\begin{bmatrix}1&0&0\\0&-7/25&24/25\\0&24/25&7/25\end{bmatrix}.$$

    \(H_2\) does not touch the first row. Applied to \(A_1\), it replaces \((-49,168)\) with \((175,0)\) and leaves the \(14\) alone. The result is upper triangular:

    $$R=H_2 H_1 A=\begin{bmatrix}14&21&-14\\0&175&-70\\0&0&-35\end{bmatrix}.$$

    The last diagonal entry is \(-35\). Nothing is wrong with that. No third reflector was built for a 1 by 1 block, so that entry is whatever the second reflector did to the third column. It does not have to be a positive length.

    Undo in reverse order: \(A=H_1 H_2 R\), so \(Q=H_1 H_2\), not \(H_2 H_1\).

    $$Q=\begin{bmatrix}6/7&-69/175&58/175\\3/7&158/175&-6/175\\-2/7&6/35&33/35\end{bmatrix}.$$

    \(Q^{\mathsf T}Q=I\), and \(QR=A\). Those two products were checked entry by entry against the matrices above. The writeup's closing line is the same check: \(Q^{\mathsf T}Q=I\) and \(Q^{\mathsf T}A=R\).

    Checks

    Why is \(Ha_1\) equal to \(\lVert a_1\rVert e_1\), not just \(e_1\)?

    \(H\) is orthogonal, so it cannot change length. The reflected column has to keep length \(\lVert a_1\rVert\). Putting a \(1\) in the pivot would be a different, non-orthogonal, row operation.

    The slide prints \(u=(-1/5,2/5)\) for \(a_1=(3,4)\). What should the denominator be?

    \(\sqrt{5}\), not \(5\). The difference \(a_1-5e_1=(-2,4)\) has length \(2\sqrt{5}\), so the unit vector is \((-1/\sqrt{5},2/\sqrt{5})\). That is the \(u\) whose reflector is the \(H\) on the next slide.

    For two reflectors, why is \(Q=H_1 H_2\) rather than \(H_2 H_1\)?

    The reduction is \(H_2 H_1 A=R\). Each reflector is its own inverse, so clearing them means multiplying on the left by \(H_1\) and then by \(H_2\). The product in front of \(R\) is \(H_1 H_2\).

    What does the \(1\) in the corner of the embedded reflector protect?

    The first column has already been reduced to a multiple of \(e_1\). A full-size reflector built from a later column would mix that column again. The identity block leaves those zeros in place.
