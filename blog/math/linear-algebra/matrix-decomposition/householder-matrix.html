    Householder matrices: a reflection that is its own inverse

    October 2026 · 12 min read

    Before you start: the transpose rule \((AB)^{\mathsf T}=B^{\mathsf T}A^{\mathsf T}\), and the dot product. The QR calculation that uses these matrices is playlist video 2.

    This is video 3 of the Matrix Decompositions playlist, QR factorization with Householder Matrices Pt 1 by FiveMinuteLectures (27 July 2025, 5 minutes 23 seconds). It does not factor a matrix. It defines a Householder matrix, proves the two properties on the slide, and shows why a product of orthogonal matrices is still orthogonal. That last fact is what lets the next lecture, in the channel's order, build \(Q\) out of reflectors instead of Gram-Schmidt.

    The formula, and the unit-vector mark

    The definition slide highlights \(u\) and adds one condition: \(u\) is a unit vector. \(H\) is square, the same size as the space \(u\) lives in. If \(u\) is \(n\times 1\), then \(uu^{\mathsf T}\) is \(n\times n\), and so is \(H\).

    $$H=I-2uu^{\mathsf T},\qquad u^{\mathsf T}u=1.$$

    \(uu^{\mathsf T}\) is an outer product, not a dot product. The dot product is the scalar \(u^{\mathsf T}u\). Swapping those two is the usual way to break the formula. Replacing \(u\) by \(-u\) does not change \(H\), because \((-u)(-u)^{\mathsf T}=uu^{\mathsf T}\). The length condition does. If \(u^{\mathsf T}u\neq 1\), the cancellation in the orthogonality proof below leaves a leftover multiple of \(uu^{\mathsf T}\), and \(H\) is not a reflection.

    Symmetry, in the order on the slide

    Symmetric means \(H^{\mathsf T}=H\). Transpose the definition. The transpose passes through the sum, and \(I^{\mathsf T}=I\):

    $$H^{\mathsf T}=I^{\mathsf T}-2(uu^{\mathsf T})^{\mathsf T}=I-2(uu^{\mathsf T})^{\mathsf T}.$$

    The yellow rule on the slide is \((AB)^{\mathsf T}=B^{\mathsf T}A^{\mathsf T}\). Apply it to the product \(u\,u^{\mathsf T}\):

    $$(uu^{\mathsf T})^{\mathsf T}=(u^{\mathsf T})^{\mathsf T}u^{\mathsf T}=uu^{\mathsf T}.$$

    The outer product is unchanged, so the transpose of \(H\) is \(H\) again.

    $$H^{\mathsf T}=I-2uu^{\mathsf T}=H.$$

    That is the last line of the symmetry proof. No entry of \(u\) was needed, only the transpose rule and \(I^{\mathsf T}=I\).

    Orthogonal means square, with \(Q^{\mathsf T}Q=I\)

    The lecture's definition of an orthogonal matrix is the implication on the slide:

    $$Q^{\mathsf T}Q=I\quad\Rightarrow\quad Q^{-1}=Q^{\mathsf T}.$$

    \(Q\) has to be square for that inverse to exist. The lecture calls the rectangular version semi-orthogonal: a tall matrix can still satisfy \(Q^{\mathsf T}Q=I\), which means its columns are orthonormal, but it has no inverse, and \(QQ^{\mathsf T}\) is not \(I\). A Householder matrix is the square case. Once \(H^{\mathsf T}H=I\), the slide's implication gives \(H^{-1}=H^{\mathsf T}\). Combined with symmetry, that inverse is \(H\) itself.

    The cancellation that uses \(u^{\mathsf T}u=1\)

    Because \(H^{\mathsf T}=H\), the product \(H^{\mathsf T}H\) is the square of \(I-2uu^{\mathsf T}\). Expand it the way the slide does, before combining like terms:

    $$H^{\mathsf T}H=(I-2uu^{\mathsf T})(I-2uu^{\mathsf T})=I-2uu^{\mathsf T}-2uu^{\mathsf T}+4uu^{\mathsf T}uu^{\mathsf T}.$$
    $$H^{\mathsf T}H=I-4uu^{\mathsf T}+4uu^{\mathsf T}uu^{\mathsf T}.$$

    The yellow mark is the middle factor of the last term. Associate \(uu^{\mathsf T}uu^{\mathsf T}\) as \(u(u^{\mathsf T}u)u^{\mathsf T}\). The unit-vector hypothesis replaces \(u^{\mathsf T}u\) by \(1\):

    $$uu^{\mathsf T}uu^{\mathsf T}=u(u^{\mathsf T}u)u^{\mathsf T}=uu^{\mathsf T}.$$

    Then \(4uu^{\mathsf T}uu^{\mathsf T}=4uu^{\mathsf T}\), and those two terms cancel.

    $$H^{\mathsf T}H=I-4uu^{\mathsf T}+4uu^{\mathsf T}=I.$$

    So \(H\) is orthogonal. With the symmetry line, \(H^{-1}=H^{\mathsf T}=H\). If the length were \(c\neq 1\) instead, the same association would give \(uu^{\mathsf T}uu^{\mathsf T}=c\,uu^{\mathsf T}\), and the remainder would be \(4(c-1)uu^{\mathsf T}\), not zero. The unit-vector mark is the whole proof.

    What the picture is doing

    The geometry slide draws \(u\), a dotted line labeled \(u^{\perp}\), and a right angle where they meet. \(u\) is the normal. The dotted line is the mirror: every direction perpendicular to \(u\). A red vector \(v\) is sent to \(Hv\), the reflection of \(v\) across that mirror.

    Split \(v\) into the part along the normal and the part in the mirror.

    $$v_{\parallel}=(u\cdot v)\,u,\qquad v_{\perp}=v-v_{\parallel}.$$

    Reflection keeps \(v_{\perp}\) and reverses \(v_{\parallel}\):

    $$Hv=v_{\perp}-v_{\parallel}=v-2(u\cdot v)\,u=(I-2uu^{\mathsf T})v.$$

    Two cases check the picture without a drawing. If \(v\) already lies in the mirror, \(u\cdot v=0\), so \(Hv=v\). The mirror is fixed. If \(v\) is parallel to \(u\), say \(v=cu\), then \(Hv=cu-2cu=-cu\). The normal is flipped. Every other vector is those two pieces added, so it lands on the other side of the mirror at the same distance. Length is preserved because \(H\) is orthogonal: \(\lVert Hv\rVert^2=v^{\mathsf T}H^{\mathsf T}Hv=v^{\mathsf T}v\).

    One unit vector, checked by hand

    The lecture does not pick numbers. This check uses \(u=(3/5,4/5)\), which has length \(1\) because \(9+16=25\).

    $$uu^{\mathsf T}=\frac{1}{25}\begin{bmatrix}9&12\\12&16\end{bmatrix},\qquad H=I-2uu^{\mathsf T}=\begin{bmatrix}7/25&-24/25\\-24/25&-7/25\end{bmatrix}.$$

    \(H\) is visibly symmetric. Its first column is \(He_1=(7/25,-24/25)\). The parallel test: \(Hu=u-2u=-u\), so

    $$H\begin{bmatrix}3/5\\4/5\end{bmatrix}=\begin{bmatrix}-3/5\\-4/5\end{bmatrix}.$$

    And \(H^{\mathsf T}H=I\) on this matrix: the two columns each have squared length \((49+576)/625=1\), and their dot product is \((-168+168)/625=0\).

    u = [3/5, 4/5]
H = [[7/25, -24/25], [-24/25, -7/25]]
# Hu should be -u
got = [H[0][0]*u[0] + H[0][1]*u[1], H[1][0]*u[0] + H[1][1]*u[1]]
assert all(abs(got[i] + u[i]) < 1e-12 for i in range(2))

    A product of orthogonal matrices is orthogonal

    The last slide is the fact the QR lecture assumes. If each \(Q_i\) is orthogonal and

    $$Q=Q_1 Q_2\cdots Q_n,$$

    the transpose reverses the product, which is the same \((AB)^{\mathsf T}=B^{\mathsf T}A^{\mathsf T}\) rule used above:

    $$Q^{\mathsf T}=Q_n^{\mathsf T}\cdots Q_2^{\mathsf T}Q_1^{\mathsf T}.$$

    Multiply. The inner pair is \(Q_1^{\mathsf T}Q_1=I\), then the next pair, until nothing is left but \(I\). For two factors the cancellation is short enough to write in full:

    $$(Q_1 Q_2)^{\mathsf T}(Q_1 Q_2)=Q_2^{\mathsf T}Q_1^{\mathsf T}Q_1 Q_2=Q_2^{\mathsf T}Q_2=I.$$

    The slide's picture of the general case is that same collapse, with \(I\) sitting in the middle: \(Q^{\mathsf T}Q=Q_n^{\mathsf T}\cdots I\cdots Q_n\), and then that \(I\) eats the neighbors. The conclusion on the last frame is \(Q^{\mathsf T}Q=I\).

    Householder QR uses this with each \(Q_i\) equal to a reflector. The channel's next video sets

    $$A=QR,\qquad Q=H_1 H_2\cdots H_n.$$

    Each \(H_i\) is orthogonal by the cancellation above, so the product \(Q\) is orthogonal by this last proof. The arithmetic of choosing each \(u\), and the 2 by 2 where \(Q\) is a single \(H\), is in the Householder QR note.

    Checks

    Which step of the symmetry proof uses \((AB)^{\mathsf T}=B^{\mathsf T}A^{\mathsf T}\)?

    The outer product. \((uu^{\mathsf T})^{\mathsf T}=(u^{\mathsf T})^{\mathsf T}u^{\mathsf T}=uu^{\mathsf T}\). After that, \(H^{\mathsf T}\) is the same expression as \(H\).

    Where does \(u^{\mathsf T}u=1\) enter the orthogonality proof?

    Only in \(uu^{\mathsf T}uu^{\mathsf T}=u(u^{\mathsf T}u)u^{\mathsf T}\). That replaces the quartic term by \(uu^{\mathsf T}\), which cancels \(-4uu^{\mathsf T}\). If the length is not 1, those terms do not cancel.

    What happens to a vector that is already perpendicular to \(u\)?

    It lies in the mirror, so \(u\cdot v=0\) and \(Hv=v\). The formula does not move the mirror.

    Why does \(Q=H_1 H_2\) satisfy \(Q^{\mathsf T}Q=I\) without multiplying the reflectors out?

    \(Q^{\mathsf T}=H_2^{\mathsf T}H_1^{\mathsf T}=H_2 H_1\), and \(H_1^{\mathsf T}H_1=I\), so the product collapses to \(H_2^{\mathsf T}H_2=I\). Each factor only needs to be orthogonal on its own.
