    LU: no swap, then one row swap

    October 2026 · 14 min read

    Before you start: Gaussian elimination. This page is two lectures. The no-swap solve is the one the row-swap example tells you to look up.

    Playlist videos 7 and 9 are the same method with and without a row exchange. Video 9 is LU Decomposition Example (11 May 2025, 4 minutes 21 seconds). Video 7 is Permuted LU Decomposition Example (7 June 2025, 4 minutes 47 seconds). It is the upload after video 9, and it comes earlier in the playlist. The short clip between them is not written here. Both lectures store the negative of the constant used in "add a multiple of the pivot row." Both then solve a lower system and an upper system. They disagree about the right-hand side once a row has moved.

    Regular, as this lecture uses the word

    The opening picture is \(A=LU\), with \(1\)s on the diagonal of \(L\) and zeros below the diagonal of \(U\). The footnote says this is only for regular square matrices. The lecture defines regular at the end: no row was permuted while reducing the system. Square is a separate requirement. A square matrix can still have a zero pivot. That matrix is not regular in this sense, and \(A=LU\) by this elimination is not available. The version that records the swap is below.

    The no-swap system

    The equations on the slide use \(x\), \(y\), and \(z\). The matrix form renames them \(x_1\), \(x_2\), and \(x_3\). The intermediate vector in the solve is also called \(y\). That \(y\) is not the unknown \(y\). From here the unknowns are \(x_1,x_2,x_3\), and \(y\) is only the intermediate vector.

    $$x+y+z=1,\qquad 4x+3y-z=6,\qquad 3x+5y+3z=4.$$
    $$A=\begin{bmatrix}1&1&1\\4&3&-1\\3&5&3\end{bmatrix},\qquad b=\begin{bmatrix}1\\6\\4\end{bmatrix}.$$

    The step list is: eliminate \(A\), store the negatives of the row-operation constants in \(L\), replace \(A\) by \(LU\), solve \(Ly=b\), then solve \(Ux=y\). On this system those steps are consistent, because no row of \(b\) has to move. The same step list is copied into the permutation lecture, where it is not.

    Elimination

    \(U\) starts as \(A\). \(L\) starts with \(1\)s on the diagonal. The lecture says you should not need a permutation. If a pivot is zero, this factorization stops.

    Multiply row 1 by \(-4\) and add it to row 2. The constant is \(-4\), so \(L_{21}\) stores \(4\):

    $$\begin{bmatrix}4&3&-1\end{bmatrix}+(-4)\begin{bmatrix}1&1&1\end{bmatrix}=\begin{bmatrix}0&-1&-5\end{bmatrix}.$$

    Multiply row 1 by \(-3\) and add it to row 3. Store \(3\):

    $$\begin{bmatrix}3&5&3\end{bmatrix}+(-3)\begin{bmatrix}1&1&1\end{bmatrix}=\begin{bmatrix}0&2&0\end{bmatrix}.$$

    The second pivot is \(-1\). The entry under it is \(2\). The lecture multiplies that pivot row by \(2\) and adds. The side numbers \(0,-2,-10\) are that multiple, \(2(0,-1,-5)\). Store the opposite, \(-2\), at \(L_{32}\):

    $$\begin{bmatrix}0&2&0\end{bmatrix}+2\begin{bmatrix}0&-1&-5\end{bmatrix}=\begin{bmatrix}0&0&-10\end{bmatrix}.$$

    The factors on the settled slide are

    $$L=\begin{bmatrix}1&0&0\\4&1&0\\3&-2&1\end{bmatrix},\qquad U=\begin{bmatrix}1&1&1\\0&-1&-5\\0&0&-10\end{bmatrix}.$$

    Row 3 of \(LU\) is \(3\) times row 1 of \(U\), minus \(2\) times row 2, plus row 3: \((3,3,3)+(0,2,10)+(0,0,-10)=(3,5,3)\). That is row 3 of \(A\). The other two rows match the same way. \(\det U=1\cdot(-1)\cdot(-10)=10\), and \(\det L=1\), so \(\det A=10\).

    Forward substitution

    Set \(y=Ux\), so \(Ly=b\). The first row says \(y_1=1\). The second row is \(4y_1+y_2=6\). The slide states \(y_2=2\) without writing that line: \(4+y_2=6\). The third row is \(3y_1-2y_2+y_3=4\), which the slide compresses to

    $$3-4+y_3=4,\qquad y_3=5,\qquad y=\begin{bmatrix}1\\2\\5\end{bmatrix}.$$

    The \(3\) is \(3y_1\). The \(-4\) is \(-2y_2\). Those are the numbers the lecture says out loud: \(y_1=1\), \(y_2=2\), \(y_3=5\).

    Back substitution, and the reversed column

    Now \(Ux=y\):

    $$\begin{bmatrix}1&1&1\\0&-1&-5\\0&0&-10\end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix}x_1\\x_2\\x_3\end{bmatrix}=\begin{bmatrix}1\\2\\5\end{bmatrix}.$$

    The last row is \(-10x_3=5\), so \(x_3=-1/2\). The middle row is \(-x_2-5x_3=2\):

    $$-x_2-5\left(-\frac12\right)=2,\qquad -x_2+\frac52=2,\qquad x_2=\frac12.$$

    The first row is \(x_1+x_2+x_3=1\), so \(x_1+1/2-1/2=1\) and \(x_1=1\). The animation writes those three lines, \(x_3\), then \(x_2\), then \(x_1\), in that order.

    The final slide stacks them into a column as \((-1/2,1/2,1)\). That is computation order, from the bottom unknown up, read as if it were \((x_1,x_2,x_3)\). The vector that matches the component lines is \((1,1/2,-1/2)\). In the equation names, \(x=1\), \(y=1/2\), \(z=-1/2\).

    The printed column does not solve the system. Its first and third equations happen to hold, and the middle one does not:

    $$4\left(-\frac12\right)+3\left(\frac12\right)-1=-\frac32,\qquad \text{not }6.$$

    The corrected vector satisfies all three: \(1+1/2-1/2=1\), \(4+3/2+1/2=6\), and \(3+5/2-3/2=4\).

    A = [[1, 1, 1], [4, 3, -1], [3, 5, 3]]
x = [1, 1/2, -1/2]
Ax = [sum(A[i][k] * x[k] for k in range(3)) for i in range(3)]
assert Ax == [1, 6, 4]
printed = [-1/2, 1/2, 1]
bad = [sum(A[i][k] * printed[k] for k in range(3)) for i in range(3)]
assert bad == [1, -3/2, 4]

    When the pivot is zero

    The permutation lecture starts from a matrix this elimination cannot accept. The \((1,1)\) entry is \(0\), so the multiplier for the row below is undefined. The first nonzero row under it is row 2, and the picture swaps those two rows. It does not pick the largest entry in the column, and it does not finish \(L\) and \(U\) for this matrix.

    $$\begin{bmatrix}0&0&-4\\1&2&3\\1&0&2\end{bmatrix}\longrightarrow\begin{bmatrix}1&2&3\\0&0&-4\\1&0&2\end{bmatrix}.$$

    The footnote there says the method is only for square nonsingular matrices. That is a different restriction from "regular." This matrix is nonsingular, with determinant \((-4)(0-2)=8\), and it is not regular: the first pivot was zero. Nonsingular is what makes some swap available. If the whole active column is zero, no exchange creates a pivot.

    The system they actually factor is

    $$x_1+2x_2+x_3=2,\qquad 2x_1+4x_2+x_3=7,\qquad x_2=2.$$
    $$A=\begin{bmatrix}1&2&1\\2&4&1\\0&1&0\end{bmatrix},\qquad b=\begin{bmatrix}2\\7\\2\end{bmatrix}.$$

    Column 1 does not need a swap. Multiply row 1 by \(-2\), add it to row 2, and store the opposite, \(2\). Row 3 already has a \(0\) in column 1, so \(L_{31}=0\):

    $$\begin{bmatrix}2&4&1\end{bmatrix}+(-2)\begin{bmatrix}1&2&1\end{bmatrix}=\begin{bmatrix}0&0&-1\end{bmatrix}.$$

    The \((2,2)\) entry is now \(0\). Swap row 2 with row 3, the only row below the current pivot. Do not swap with row 1: that row already cleared column 1, and bringing it back down would put a nonzero under the first pivot. The same two rows of \(P\), which starts as \(I\), are swapped. In \(L\), swap only the stored multipliers \(L_{21}\) and \(L_{31}\), not the whole rows. A whole-row swap would move a diagonal \(1\). The entry under the new pivot is already \(0\), so the last multiplier is \(0\).

    $$L=\begin{bmatrix}1&0&0\\0&1&0\\2&0&1\end{bmatrix},\quad U=\begin{bmatrix}1&2&1\\0&1&0\\0&0&-1\end{bmatrix},\quad P=\begin{bmatrix}1&0&0\\0&0&1\\0&1&0\end{bmatrix}.$$

    \(P\) swaps rows 2 and 3, so

    $$PA=\begin{bmatrix}1&2&1\\0&1&0\\2&4&1\end{bmatrix}.$$

    Row 3 of \(LU\) is \(2(1,2,1)+(0,0,-1)=(2,4,1)\). The product is \(PA\). This \(P\) is one transposition, so \(P^2=I\). The lecture writes \(PA=LU\), then \(PAx=Pb\), then \(LUx=Pb\).

    The step list still says to begin with \(Ly=b\). That line was copied from the no-swap lecture, where it is right. Here the boxed equation uses \(Pb\). Solving \(Ly=b\) does not solve \(Ax=b\).

    Apply the same row swap to \(b\): \(Pb=(2,2,7)\). Forward substitution gives \(y_1=2\), \(y_2=2\), and \(2y_1+y_3=7\), so \(y_3=3\). Back substitution: \(-x_3=3\), so \(x_3=-3\); \(x_2=2\); \(x_1+4-3=2\), so \(x_1=1\).

    $$x=\begin{bmatrix}1\\2\\-3\end{bmatrix}.$$

    Check: \(1+4-3=2\), \(2+8-3=7\), and \(x_2=2\). If the right-hand side is left as \(b=(2,7,2)\), the same \(L\) and \(U\) produce \(x=(-14,7,2)\). Then \(Ax=(2,2,7)\), which is \(Pb\). The rows of \(b\) were solved as if they had not been swapped.

    A = [[1, 2, 1], [2, 4, 1], [0, 1, 0]]
x = [1, 2, -3]
assert [sum(A[i][k] * x[k] for k in range(3)) for i in range(3)] == [2, 7, 2]

    Checks

    What does regular mean on the no-swap slide?

    The elimination did not permute a row. Square is listed separately. The second example is square and nonsingular, and it is not regular: a pivot was zero.

    Why does \(L\) store \(4\), \(3\), and \(-2\), rather than \(-4\), \(-3\), and \(2\)?

    Those second numbers are the constants in "add a multiple of the pivot row." \(L\) stores their negatives. Adding \(-4\) times row 1 is the same row as subtracting \(4\) times row 1.

    The final column is \((-1/2,1/2,1)\). Which unknown is \(-1/2\)?

    \(x_3\), also called \(z\) on the equation slide. The component lines are \(x_3=-1/2\), \(x_2=1/2\), \(x_1=1\). Read top to bottom, the printed column has those values in reverse.

    Why is \(Ly=b\) right in one lecture and wrong in the other?

    With no swap, \(P=I\) and \(Pb=b\). After rows 2 and 3 move, the first triangular system is \(Ly=Pb=(2,2,7)\). Using \(b\) solves \(Ax=Pb\).

    Why swap only \(L_{21}\) with \(L_{31}\)?

    Those are the multipliers already stored for the rows that moved. The diagonal \(1\)s stay. Swapping the whole rows of \(L\) would move one of them.
