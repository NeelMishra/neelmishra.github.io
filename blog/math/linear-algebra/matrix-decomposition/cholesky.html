    Cholesky: square-root the pivots, then multiply

    October 2026 · 12 min read

    Before you start: the transpose, and one pass of Gaussian elimination. This lecture builds the factor out of an LU decomposition and plays that elimination as an animation. The arithmetic is written out below.

    This is video 6 of the Matrix Decompositions playlist, Cholesky Factorization Example by FiveMinuteLectures (14 June 2025, 5 minutes 17 seconds). A symmetric positive definite matrix is factored as \(A=GG^{\mathsf T}\). The route is LU, then a diagonal of pivots, then the positive square roots of those pivots. The upload points ahead to Gram-Schmidt. In this playlist that process is already videos 4 and 5. The videos after this one are LU.

    Positive definite, as the slide states it

    The slide says a matrix \(A\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times n}\) is positive definite if it is symmetric, \(A^{\mathsf T}=A\), and if every nonzero \(x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}\) satisfies \(x^{\mathsf T}Ax>0\). The narration says "if and only if", and adds that the product is zero only for the zero vector. That last sentence is the same condition: at \(x=0\) the product is 0, and the inequality says it is positive for every other \(x\).

    The inequality does not force symmetry. \(x^{\mathsf T}Ax\) only sees the symmetric part of \(A\). For

    $$B=\begin{bmatrix}1&100\\-100&1\end{bmatrix},\qquad x^{\mathsf T}Bx=x_1^2+x_2^2,$$

    the product is positive for every nonzero \(x\), and \(B\) is not symmetric. \(GG^{\mathsf T}\) is always symmetric, so a factor of that shape cannot exist unless \(A\) is. Symmetry has to be required on its own. In this note \(L\) is the unit lower factor from LU. \(G\) is the Cholesky factor. Some texts use \(L\) for what this lecture calls \(G\).

    Four steps, and a picture that is one step early

    The procedure on the slide is:

    $$A=LU,\qquad U=DV,\qquad A=LDL^{\mathsf T},\qquad D=SS,\qquad A=LSSL^{\mathsf T}=(LS)(LS)^{\mathsf T}=GG^{\mathsf T}.$$

    \(D\) is the diagonal of pivots. \(V\) is upper triangular with 1s on the diagonal. For a symmetric matrix the lecture sets \(V=L^{\mathsf T}\). \(S\) is the diagonal matrix of positive square roots, so \(S^{\mathsf T}=S\) and \((LS)^{\mathsf T}=S^{\mathsf T}L^{\mathsf T}=SL^{\mathsf T}\). That is why the right factor is \(G^{\mathsf T}\), not a second copy of \(G\). \(G\) is not symmetric in the example below, so \(G^2\) is a different product. Its first row is \((1,0,0)\), which is not the first row of \(A\).

    The opening picture draws both factors with 1s on the diagonal, and the lecture calls \(G\) lower unit triangular. Those 1s belong to \(L\) and to \(V\), before \(S\) is multiplied in. The \(G\) from step 4 has the square roots of the pivots on its diagonal. In this example that diagonal is \(1,1,2\).

    The matrix

    The example is

    $$A=\begin{bmatrix}1&1&1\\1&2&-2\\1&-2&14\end{bmatrix}.$$

    It is symmetric: the pairs across the diagonal are \(1\) with \(1\), \(1\) with \(1\), and \(-2\) with \(-2\). The positive-definite test the lecture uses is the sign of the pivots, after the elimination. Two smaller checks use the same numbers. The top-left entry is \(1\). The leading \(2\) by \(2\) has determinant \(2-1=1\). Expanding the full determinant,

    $$\det A=1\cdot(28-4)-1\cdot(14+2)+1\cdot(-2-2)=24-16-4=4.$$

    Those three numbers, \(1\), \(1\), and \(4\), are the running products of the pivots computed next.

    Elimination, written out

    \(L\) starts as \(I\). \(U\) starts as \(A\). Each multiplier is the entry under the pivot, divided by the pivot, and it is stored in \(L\). The diagonal of \(L\) stays \(1\). This pass never swaps rows. A zero pivot would stop it. The later playlist videos are the ones that permute.

    The first pivot is \(1\). Subtract row 1 from row 2, and subtract row 1 from row 3. The multipliers are \(1/1=1\) and \(1/1=1\):

    $$\begin{bmatrix}1&2&-2\end{bmatrix}-\begin{bmatrix}1&1&1\end{bmatrix}=\begin{bmatrix}0&1&-3\end{bmatrix},\qquad\begin{bmatrix}1&-2&14\end{bmatrix}-\begin{bmatrix}1&1&1\end{bmatrix}=\begin{bmatrix}0&-3&13\end{bmatrix}.$$

    The animation writes \(-1,-1,-1\) beside the row being cleared. That is row 1 with the minus sign already attached. After this column,

    $$U=\begin{bmatrix}1&1&1\\0&1&-3\\0&-3&13\end{bmatrix},\qquad L=\begin{bmatrix}1&0&0\\1&1&0\\1&0&1\end{bmatrix}.$$

    The active block is \(\begin{bmatrix}1&-3\\-3&13\end{bmatrix}\). It is still symmetric. That is why the next multiplier will equal the entry that stays in \(U\) above the pivot.

    The second pivot is \(1\). The entry under it is \(-3\), so the multiplier is \(-3/1=-3\). Subtract \(-3\) times row 2 from row 3, which is the same as adding \(3\) times row 2:

    $$\begin{bmatrix}0&-3&13\end{bmatrix}-(-3)\begin{bmatrix}0&1&-3\end{bmatrix}=\begin{bmatrix}0&-3&13\end{bmatrix}+\begin{bmatrix}0&3&-9\end{bmatrix}=\begin{bmatrix}0&0&4\end{bmatrix}.$$

    Store \(-3\) at \(L_{32}\). The factors on the settled slide are

    $$L=\begin{bmatrix}1&0&0\\1&1&0\\1&-3&1\end{bmatrix},\qquad U=\begin{bmatrix}1&1&1\\0&1&-3\\0&0&4\end{bmatrix}.$$

    Row 3 of \(LU\) is row 1 of \(U\), minus \(3\) times row 2, plus row 3: \((1,1,1)-3(0,1,-3)+(0,0,4)=(1,-2,14)\). That is row 3 of \(A\). The other two rows match the same way. \(\det L=1\), so \(\det A=\det U=1\cdot 1\cdot 4=4\).

    Split \(U\) into \(D\) and \(V\)

    \(D\) is the diagonal of \(U\):

    $$D=\begin{bmatrix}1&0&0\\0&1&0\\0&0&4\end{bmatrix}.$$

    All three entries are positive. The lecture treats that as the positive-definite check and continues. \(V\) is \(U\) with each row divided by its pivot: row 1 by \(1\), row 2 by \(1\), row 3 by \(4\).

    $$V=\begin{bmatrix}1&1&1\\0&1&-3\\0&0&1\end{bmatrix}.$$

    Scaling those rows by \(1\), \(1\), and \(4\) puts the pivots back, so \(U=DV\). And \(V=L^{\mathsf T}\). The lecture uses that match as a check that the elimination was consistent. It had to match on this matrix: the active block stayed symmetric, so the \(-3\) above the second pivot is the same \(-3\) stored in \(L\). After the row scaling, the unit upper factor is the transpose of the unit lower factor. Therefore \(A=LDL^{\mathsf T}\).

    Why a positive diagonal of \(D\) is enough

    Set \(y=L^{\mathsf T}x\). Then

    $$x^{\mathsf T}Ax=x^{\mathsf T}LDL^{\mathsf T}x=y^{\mathsf T}Dy=y_1^2+y_2^2+4y_3^2.$$

    \(L^{\mathsf T}\) is invertible, so \(y=0\) only when \(x=0\). For any other \(y\) the sum is positive, because each pivot is positive. That is the lecture's claim: positive diagonal entries of \(D\) mean \(A\) is positive definite.

    The vector that isolates the last pivot solves \(L^{\mathsf T}x=(0,0,1)^{\mathsf T}\). From the bottom row, \(x_3=1\). The middle row is \(x_2-3x_3=0\), so \(x_2=3\). The top row is \(x_1+x_2+x_3=0\), so \(x_1=-4\). Then

    $$Ax=\begin{bmatrix}-4+3+1\\-4+6-2\\-4-6+14\end{bmatrix}=\begin{bmatrix}0\\0\\4\end{bmatrix},\qquad x^{\mathsf T}Ax=4.$$

    The value is the last pivot. If that pivot had been negative, the same vector would make the quadratic form negative, and the square root in the next step would not be real.

    Square root of \(D\)

    \(S\) is the diagonal of positive square roots. The slide writes the diagonal of \(D\) as \(1\cdot 1\), \(1\cdot 1\), and \(2\cdot 2\):

    $$S=\begin{bmatrix}1&0&0\\0&1&0\\0&0&2\end{bmatrix},\qquad SS=D.$$

    \(S\) is diagonal, so \(S^{\mathsf T}=S\), and

    $$A=LSSL^{\mathsf T}=(LS)(SL^{\mathsf T})=(LS)(LS)^{\mathsf T}.$$

    \(G\) is \(L\) times \(S\)

    Right-multiplication by a diagonal matrix scales columns. Columns 1 and 2 of \(L\) are unchanged. Column 3 is multiplied by \(2\), and its only nonzero entry is the \(1\) at the bottom, which becomes \(2\). Entrywise, \(G_{ij}=L_{ij}s_j\):

    $$G=LS=\begin{bmatrix}1&0&0\\1&1&0\\1&-3&1\end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix}1&0&0\\0&1&0\\0&0&2\end{bmatrix}=\begin{bmatrix}1&0&0\\1&1&0\\1&-3&2\end{bmatrix}.$$

    That is the printed \(G\). The boxed pair on the last slide is this \(G\) and

    $$G^{\mathsf T}=\begin{bmatrix}1&1&1\\0&1&-3\\0&0&2\end{bmatrix}.$$

    The diagonal is \(1,1,2\), not \(1,1,1\).

    The product the lecture leaves as a check

    The lecture says to multiply \(GG^{\mathsf T}\) and compare with \(A\). It does not write the product. Row 1 of \(G\) is \((1,0,0)\), so row 1 of the product is row 1 of \(G^{\mathsf T}\): \((1,1,1)\).

    Row 2 is \((1,1,0)\). Its dots with the rows of \(G\) are \(1\), then \(1+1=2\), then \(1-3=-2\). That is \((1,2,-2)\).

    Row 3 is \((1,-3,2)\). Its dots are \(1\), then \(1-3=-2\), then \(1+9+4=14\). That is \((1,-2,14)\). The product is \(A\).

    A = [[1, 1, 1], [1, 2, -2], [1, -2, 14]]
G = [[1, 0, 0], [1, 1, 0], [1, -3, 2]]
GT = [list(col) for col in zip(*G)]
product = [[sum(G[i][k] * GT[k][j] for k in range(3)) for j in range(3)] for i in range(3)]
assert product == A

    The same \(G\) from \(A=GG^{\mathsf T}\)

    Requiring a positive diagonal determines \(G\) from \(A\) without going back through \(L\). The \((1,1)\) entry says \(g_{11}^2=1\), so \(g_{11}=1\). The first column of \(A\) is \(g_{11}\) times the first column of \(G\), so that column is \((1,1,1)\).

    The \((2,2)\) entry is \(g_{21}^2+g_{22}^2=2\). With \(g_{21}=1\), this says \(g_{22}^2=1\), so \(g_{22}=1\). The \((3,2)\) entry is \(g_{31}g_{21}+g_{32}g_{22}=-2\), so \(1+g_{32}=-2\) and \(g_{32}=-3\).

    The \((3,3)\) entry is \(g_{31}^2+g_{32}^2+g_{33}^2=14\), so \(1+9+g_{33}^2=14\) and \(g_{33}^2=4\). The positive root is \(2\). That \(4\) is the last pivot from elimination: \(14-1-9\). The two routes are the same arithmetic. Elimination keeps the \(4\) in \(D\). The square-root step moves \(2\) onto \(G\).

    Checks

    Why does \(x^{\mathsf T}Ax>0\) not replace the symmetry requirement?

    The product ignores the skew part of \(A\). \(GG^{\mathsf T}\) is symmetric, so \(A\) has to be symmetric before that factorization can hold.

    Why is row 3 of \(U\) divided by \(4\)?

    \(V\) has 1s on the diagonal. The third pivot is \(4\), so that row is divided by \(4\). Multiplying back by \(D\) recovers \(U\).

    Why is \((LS)^{\mathsf T}\) equal to \(SL^{\mathsf T}\)?

    \(S\) is diagonal, so \(S^{\mathsf T}=S\). Transposing reverses the product and leaves \(S\) in place. That is why the right factor is \(G^{\mathsf T}\).

    The opening picture has 1s on the diagonal of \(G\). What is the computed diagonal?

    \(1,1,2\). The 1s belong to \(L\). Column 3 of \(L\) is scaled by \(\sqrt{4}=2\).

    What fails if the last pivot is negative?

    The real square root in \(S\) does not exist, and \(x=(-4,3,1)^{\mathsf T}\) makes \(x^{\mathsf T}Ax\) equal to that pivot, so the quadratic form is no longer always positive.
