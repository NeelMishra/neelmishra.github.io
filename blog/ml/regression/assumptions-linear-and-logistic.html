    Assumptions of Linear and Logistic Regression

    July 2026 · 18 min read

    Statistical assumptions are often taught like an all-or-nothing checklist: if residuals are skewed or have unequal spread, someone declares the regression "invalid." That view blurs together very different mathematical claims. An optimization solver can compute ordinary least squares or penalized logistic regression on almost any finite matrix; an assumption is simply the condition required for a specific interpretation—such as unbiased slopes, minimum-variance efficiency, trustworthy standard errors, or calibrated probabilities.

    Think of regression assumptions as a ladder. Climbing each rung buys a stronger guarantee, and slipping off one rung invalidates only the claims above it while leaving lower rungs intact. This chapter builds the assumption ladders for linear and logistic regression side by side, proves what breaks on two concrete hand-checkable datasets, and gives a diagnostic playbook for deciding what to repair.

    Pipeline Foundations Shared by Both Models

    Before comparing error distributions or link functions, both models depend on data-collection and pipeline rules that no matrix formula can rescue after the fact. Let $n$ be the number of observed rows and let $X \in \mathbb{R}^{n \times (p+1)}$ be the augmented design matrix containing a leading column of ones and $p$ prepared predictors.

    Foundational pipeline conditions required before interpreting either linear or logistic regression.
    FoundationPlain-English meaningWhat fails when violated
    Representative sampling / supportTraining rows cover the same population and feature region where predictions will be used.Selection bias or covariate/concept shift degrades out-of-sample predictions and calibration.
    No target or split leakageEvery column in $X$ (and every learned scaler/imputer) uses only information available prior to prediction time.Validation metrics look artificially strong while live deployment fails immediately.
    Full column rank ($\operatorname{rank}(X) = p + 1$)No predictor column is an exact linear combination of the intercept and other columns.Individual parameters $\beta_j$ are unidentifiable (infinitely many $\beta$ produce identical $X\beta$).
    Accounted row dependenceRows treated as independent do not share hidden clusters, repeated users, or serial time shocks.Effective sample size is smaller than $n$, making naive standard errors dangerously optimistic.
    Train-serve parityServing applies the exact unit conversions, category encodings, and missingness rules learned in training.Correct coefficients multiply mismatched features, causing silent bias in production.

    The Linear Regression Ladder: What Each Assumption Actually Buys

    Write the population data-generating model for $n$ observations as

    $$y = X\beta + \varepsilon,$$

    where $y \in \mathbb{R}^n$ is the continuous response vector, $\beta = (\beta_0, \beta_1, \dots, \beta_p)^\top \in \mathbb{R}^{p+1}$ is the unknown parameter vector, and $\varepsilon \in \mathbb{R}^n$ is the unobservable population disturbance vector (distinct from the observable sample residual $e = y - X\hat\beta$). Notice how each successive condition unlocks a distinct theorem:

    The four-rung assumption ladder for Ordinary Least Squares, $\hat\beta = (X^\top X)^{-1} X^\top y$.
    Rung & mathematical conditionExact guarantee unlockedWhat breaks if this rung fails (and remedy)
    1. Identification & moments
    $\operatorname{rank}(X)=p+1$, i.i.d. draws with $E[\|X\|_2^2] < \infty$, $E[Y^2] < \infty$, $E[XX^\top] \succ 0$    		 Unique sample fit & Best Linear Predictor (BLP): $\hat\beta$ is unique in sample and converges in probability to $\beta^* = (E[XX^\top])^{-1}E[XY]$, the $L_2$-closest linear approximation to $E[Y\mid X]$. Exact collinearity makes $X^\top X$ singular (drop redundant dummy/column, use SVD pseudoinverse, or add Ridge penalty). Heavy infinite-variance tails destabilize OLS (use Huber/LAD).
    2. Correct mean & strict exogeneity
    $E[Y \mid X] = X\beta$, equivalently $E[\varepsilon \mid X] = 0$    		 Finite-sample unbiasedness: $E[\hat\beta \mid X] = \beta$. (Weaker contemporaneous orthogonality $E[x_i \varepsilon_i] = 0$ suffices for large-sample consistency $\hat\beta \xrightarrow{p} \beta$.) Omitted confounders, simultaneity, or classical measurement error bias $\hat\beta$ itself, even as $n \to \infty$. Remedy requires study design (controls, IV, RCT) or basis terms for missing curvature—never robust SEs.
    3. Spherical conditional errors
    $\operatorname{Var}(\varepsilon \mid X) = \sigma^2 I_n$ (constant variance $\sigma^2$ and zero cross-row correlation)    		 Classical SE validity & Gauss-Markov efficiency (BLUE): $\operatorname{Var}(\hat\beta \mid X) = \sigma^2(X^\top X)^{-1}$ holds, and OLS achieves the smallest covariance matrix among all linear unbiased estimators. Under heteroscedasticity or clustering, $\hat\beta$ stays unbiased (if Rung 2 holds!) but $\sigma^2(X^\top X)^{-1}$ is wrong and WLS/GLS can beat OLS in variance. Use HC2/HC3 sandwich or cluster-robust SEs, or model weights via WLS/GLS.
    4. Conditional normality
    $\varepsilon \mid X \sim \mathcal{N}(0, \sigma^2 I_n)$    		 Exact finite-sample inference & BUE/MLE optimality: $t$-ratios follow $t_{n-p-1}$ and nested tests follow $F$ exactly at any $n > p + 1$; OLS equals Gaussian MLE and achieves the Cramér-Rao bound among all unbiased estimators. Non-normal errors invalidate exact small-sample $p$-values and Gaussian prediction intervals, though asymptotic CLT inference for $\hat\beta$ still holds for large $n$ under mild leverage and moment bounds.
    Crucial separation: Rung 2 versus Rung 3. Unequal error variance ($\operatorname{Var}(\varepsilon_i \mid X) = \sigma_i^2$) breaks Rung 3, leaving $E[\hat\beta \mid X] = \beta$ completely intact while invalidating textbook standard errors. By contrast, an omitted confounder correlated with $X$ breaks Rung 2 ($E[\varepsilon \mid X] \ne 0$), biasing the point estimate $\hat\beta$ itself—a failure that larger $n$ and sandwich standard errors only measure more precisely around the wrong value.

    The Logistic Regression Ladder: Log-Odds, Overlap, and Calibration

    For binary outcomes $Y_i \in \{0, 1\}$, logistic regression dispenses with additive Gaussian errors altogether. Instead, conditional on $X_i = x_i$, each outcome is modeled as $Y_i \mid X_i = x_i \sim \operatorname{Bernoulli}\big(p(x_i)\big)$ with linear log-odds:

    $$\operatorname{logit}\big(p(x_i)\big) = \log\frac{p(x_i)}{1 - p(x_i)} = x_i^\top \beta \quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad p(x_i) = \sigma(x_i^\top \beta) = \frac{1}{1 + e^{-x_i^\top \beta}}.$$

    Notice that heteroscedasticity is built into the Bernoulli family: $\operatorname{Var}(Y_i \mid X_i = x_i) = p(x_i)\big(1 - p(x_i)\big)$ automatically peaks at $0.25$ when $p = 0.5$ and approaches $0$ in the tails. Logistic regression's assumption ladder therefore looks structurally different from OLS:

    The four-rung assumption ladder for Binary Logistic Regression (Maximum Likelihood Estimation).
    Rung & mathematical conditionExact guarantee unlockedWhat breaks if this rung fails (and remedy)
    1. Full rank & class overlap
    $\operatorname{rank}(X) = p + 1$ and no hyperplane perfectly separates $y_i=1$ from $y_i=0$    		 Unique finite unregularized MLE: Negative log-likelihood is strictly convex and coercive, guaranteeing a unique finite minimizer $\hat\beta_{\text{MLE}} \in \mathbb{R}^{p+1}$ (Albert & Anderson, 1984). Under complete or quasi-complete separation, $\|\hat\beta_{\text{MLE}}\| \to \infty$ and Fisher-information SEs explode even though in-sample ranking looks perfect. Use $L_2$/$L_1$ regularization or Firth's penalized likelihood.
    2. Linear log-odds specification
    $\log\frac{P(Y=1\mid X)}{1-P(Y=1\mid X)} = X\beta$ in the chosen feature basis    		 Consistent conditional probabilities & in-population calibration: $\hat p(x) = \sigma(x^\top \hat\beta) \xrightarrow{p} P(Y = 1 \mid X = x)$, and predicted risk matches empirical event rates across risk deciles. Omitted nonlinearities (e.g., U-shaped risk in age) or missing interactions cause systematic miscalibration and biased odds ratios. Expand $X$ with splines, polynomials, or interactions, and check calibration curves.
    3. Independent Bernoulli trials & sufficient events
    Conditionally independent rows and adequate minority-class count relative to $p+1$    		 Asymptotic normality & valid model SEs: $\hat\beta \approx \mathcal{N}\big(\beta, (X^\top W X)^{-1}\big)$ with diagonal weight matrix $W_{ii} = p_i(1 - p_i)$, validating Wald and likelihood-ratio intervals. (Note: MLE is generally not finite-sample unbiased!) Clustered/repeated rows or overdispersed grouped counts make $(X^\top \hat W X)^{-1}$ too narrow (use cluster/sandwich covariance). Rare events ($n_{\min} \ll 10(p+1)$) cause severe small-sample MLE bias away from zero (penalize or simplify).
    4. Deployment prior & concept stability
    Serving shares training $P(Y \mid X)$ (or known prior shift under fixed $P(X \mid Y)$)    		 Out-of-sample probability calibration & optimal thresholding: Cost-based decision thresholds derived on validation data remain optimal in production. Case-control sampling or prevalence shift changes base rates, shifting log-odds by $\log\frac{\pi_{\text{new}}(1-\pi_{\text{old}})}{\pi_{\text{old}}(1-\pi_{\text{new}})}$ while leaving ROC-AUC deceptive; recalibrate intercept or refit.

    Two Hand-Verifiable Numerical Demonstrations

    1. Linear Regression Under Growing Error Spread (Heteroscedasticity)

    Consider six rows $x = (1, 2, 3, 4, 5, 6)$ with observed outcomes $y = (2.9, 5.2, 6.6, 9.8, 9.4, 15.1)$. Fitting OLS gives $\hat\beta_0 = 0.4867$ and $\hat\beta_1 = 2.1943$, with residual degrees of freedom $n - 2 = 4$ and $\text{SSE} = 6.9937$ ($\hat\sigma = 1.3223$). Look closely at the residuals $e_i$ and row leverages $h_{ii} = \frac{1}{6} + \frac{(x_i - 3.5)^2}{17.5}$:

    1$1$$2.9$$2.6810$$+0.2190$$0.5238$$+0.4600$
    2$2$$5.2$$4.8752$$+0.3248$$0.2952$$+0.4608$
    3$3$$6.6$$7.0695$$-0.4695$$0.1810$$-0.5732$
    4$4$$9.8$$9.2638$$+0.5362$$0.1810$$+0.6547$
    5$5$$9.4$$11.4581$$-2.0581$$0.2952$$-2.9203$
    6$6$$15.1$$13.6524$$+1.4476$$0.5238$$+3.0400$

    Because the largest errors sit at $x = 5$ and high-leverage $x = 6$, the homoscedastic formula $\operatorname{se}_{\text{OLS}}(\hat\beta_1) = \hat\sigma / \sqrt{17.5} = 0.3161$ averages the quiet left-hand residuals with the noisy right-hand ones. Replacing $\sigma^2 I$ in $(X^\top X)^{-1} X^\top \Omega X (X^\top X)^{-1}$ with $\operatorname{diag}(e_i^2) \cdot \frac{n}{n-2}$ gives $\operatorname{se}_{\text{HC1}}(\hat\beta_1) = 0.3378$, and dividing each residual by $1 - h_{ii}$ to counter finite-sample high-leverage shrinkage gives $\operatorname{se}_{\text{HC3}}(\hat\beta_1) = 0.5077$—a $60.6\%$ larger standard error for the exact same slope $\hat\beta_1 = 2.1943$.

    2. Logistic Regression: Class Overlap Versus Complete Separation

    Now take five centered inputs $x = (-2, -1, 0, 1, 2)$ and compare two binary label vectors that differ in only one entry at $x = 0$:

    Flipping one label at $x = 0$ destroys overlap and sends the unregularized logistic MLE to infinity.
    RegimeLabels at $x = (-2,-1,0,1,2)$Unregularized MLE $(\hat\beta_0, \hat\beta_1)$Unpenalized NLL$L_2$-penalized fit ($\frac{1}{2}\beta_1^2$)
    Overlapping classes$(0, 0, 1, 0, 1)$$(-0.6227,\; 1.0904)$ (unique, finite)$2.4220$$(-0.4648,\; 0.7204)$
    Complete separation$(0, 0, 0, 1, 1)$Does not exist ($\|\hat\beta\| \to \infty$)$\inf = 0$ ($0.00009$ at $\beta_1 = 20$)$(-0.6071,\; 1.0470)$ (finite!)

    In the separated dataset $(0, 0, 0, 1, 1)$, every negative label has $x_i \le 0$ and every positive label has $x_i \ge 1$. Along the ray $\beta(k) = (-0.5k, k)^\top$, the linear score $k(x_i - 0.5)$ is negative on all zeros and positive on all ones; increasing $k \to \infty$ pushes every predicted probability strictly closer to its observed $0/1$ label and drives negative log-likelihood toward $0$ without ever reaching a stationary minimum. Adding an $L_2$ penalty $\frac{1}{2}\lambda\beta_1^2$ ($\lambda = 1$) makes the objective coercive again and restores a well-behaved finite solution.

    Six Persistent Interview Misconceptions

    • "Predictors $X$ must be normally distributed." False in both linear and logistic regression. Predictors can be binary indicators, skewed dollar amounts, or deterministic grid points; normality in Rung 4 applies only to conditional disturbances $\varepsilon \mid X$ in small-sample linear inference.
    • "Non-normal residuals make OLS coefficients biased." False. Unbiasedness requires only linear conditional mean and $E[\varepsilon \mid X] = 0$ (Rung 2), with zero distributional shape assumptions.
    • "Logistic regression requires 50/50 balanced classes." False. Random undersampling or synthetic oversampling distorts the intercept and ruins probability calibration! Under true rare-event prevalence, logistic MLE estimates calibrated probabilities directly—choose an asymmetric cost threshold instead of corrupting the base rate.
    • "Heteroscedasticity-robust standard errors fix omitted-variable bias or functional-form error." False. Sandwich estimators estimate the sampling variance around whatever pseudo-true parameter the model converges to; they never move $\hat\beta$.
    • "Logistic MLE is finite-sample unbiased just like OLS." False. Because the logit link is nonlinear, maximum likelihood for logistic regression is asymptotically consistent and efficient but exhibits finite-sample bias away from zero (amplified in small or sparse samples, motivating Firth's 1993 penalty).
    • "High $R^2$ or ROC-AUC proves the assumptions hold." False. High $R^2$ routinely coexists with curvature, serial correlation, or omitted confounders, and ROC-AUC is completely invariant to monotone probability miscalibration.

    Reproduce Both Numerical Demonstrations

    The Python check below verifies the six-row heteroscedastic OLS, HC1, and HC3 standard errors and the five-row logistic overlap versus complete-separation results.

    Show the complete numerical check 
    import numpy as np
from scipy import optimize

# 1. Six-row heteroscedastic linear regression: OLS vs HC1 vs HC3 SEs
x = np.array([1., 2., 3., 4., 5., 6.])
y = np.array([2.9, 5.2, 6.6, 9.8, 9.4, 15.1])
X = np.column_stack([np.ones_like(x), x])
n, k = X.shape

beta = np.linalg.lstsq(X, y, rcond=None)[0]
e = y - X @ beta
XtX_inv = np.linalg.inv(X.T @ X)
h = np.sum((X @ XtX_inv) * X, axis=1)
s2 = (e @ e) / (n - k)

se_ols = np.sqrt(np.diag(s2 * XtX_inv))
se_hc1 = np.sqrt(np.diag((n / (n - k)) * XtX_inv @ (X.T * (e ** 2)) @ X @ XtX_inv))
se_hc3 = np.sqrt(np.diag(XtX_inv @ (X.T * ((e / (1.0 - h)) ** 2)) @ X @ XtX_inv))

assert np.allclose(beta, [0.48666667, 2.19428571], atol=1e-6)
assert np.isclose(se_ols[1], 0.31606423, atol=1e-6)
assert np.isclose(se_hc1[1], 0.33776316, atol=1e-6)
assert np.isclose(se_hc3[1], 0.50769597, atol=1e-6)
print("Slope:", beta[1], "SE(OLS/HC1/HC3):", se_ols[1], se_hc1[1], se_hc3[1])

# 2. Five-row logistic regression: class overlap vs complete separation
x5 = np.array([-2., -1., 0., 1., 2.])
X5 = np.column_stack([np.ones_like(x5), x5])
y_overlap = np.array([0., 0., 1., 0., 1.])
y_sep = np.array([0., 0., 0., 1., 1.])

def pen_nll(b, target, lam=0.0):
    s = X5 @ b
    return np.sum(np.logaddexp(0.0, s) - target * s) + 0.5 * lam * (b[1] ** 2)

b_ov = optimize.minimize(pen_nll, np.zeros(2), args=(y_overlap, 0.0), method="BFGS").x
b_sep_l2 = optimize.minimize(pen_nll, np.zeros(2), args=(y_sep, 1.0), method="BFGS").x
assert np.allclose(b_ov, [-0.62269131, 1.09042703], atol=1e-6)
assert np.allclose(b_sep_l2, [-0.60713419, 1.04696530], atol=1e-6)
print("Overlap MLE:", b_ov, "| Separated L2:", b_sep_l2)
