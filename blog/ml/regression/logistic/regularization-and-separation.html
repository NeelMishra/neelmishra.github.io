    Regularization, Identifiability, and Perfect Separation

    July 2026 · 21 min read

    How can a training dataset that is easiest to classify—where a clean hyperplane separates every positive example from every negative example—break maximum-likelihood logistic regression? Think about what happens to log-loss once a boundary classifies every training row on the right side: doubling the coefficient vector leaves the geometric boundary untouched, yet steepens the sigmoid toward a step function, pushing every positive probability closer to $1$ and every negative probability closer to $0$. Because $\sigma(z)$ approaches $0$ and $1$ only as $|z|\to\infty$, the unpenalized solver chases an infinite slope forever.

    Regularization restores a finite, well-conditioned estimator by charging an explicit budget for slope magnitude—and simultaneously stabilizes variance under high-dimensional or collinear features.

    Albert & Anderson's Taxonomy: Complete Separation, Quasi-Complete Separation, and Overlap

    Let $\tilde x_i = (1, x_{i1}, \dots, x_{id})^\top \in \mathbb{R}^{d+1}$ be the augmented predictor for row $i \in \{1,\dots,n\}$ (stacked into full-rank $X \in \mathbb{R}^{n\times(d+1)}$), and write the signed label as $y_i^{\pm} = 2y_i - 1 \in \{-1, +1\}$ ($+1$ when $y_i=1$, $-1$ when $y_i=0$). Albert & Anderson (1984) proved that every binary dataset falls into exactly one of three mutually exclusive geometric configurations:

    1. Complete separation: there exists a nonzero vector $v \in \mathbb{R}^{d+1}\setminus\{0\}$ that places every observation strictly on its correct side of the hyperplane: $$y_i^{\pm}\big(\tilde x_i^\top v\big) > 0 \qquad \text{for all } i = 1,\dots,n.$$ Along the ray $\beta(c) = c\,v$ ($c > 0$), every per-row loss $\operatorname{softplus}(-c\,y_i^{\pm}\tilde x_i^\top v) = \ln(1 + e^{-c\,|v^\top\tilde x_i|})$ decreases strictly toward $0$: $\lim_{c\to\infty} J(c\,v) = 0$. Since $J(\beta) > 0$ for every finite $\beta$, no finite MLE exists.
    2. Quasi-complete separation: no vector achieves complete separation, and there exists $v \in \mathbb{R}^{d+1}\setminus\{0\}$ such that $$y_i^{\pm}\big(\tilde x_i^\top v\big) \ge 0 \quad \text{for all } i = 1,\dots,n,$$ with boundary equality $\tilde x_i^\top v = 0$ holding for at least one positive ($y_i=1$) and at least one negative ($y_j=0$) observation (if only one class touched $\tilde x^\top v = 0$, nudging the intercept $v_0$ slightly would turn it into complete separation!). Let $\mathcal{B} = \{i : \tilde x_i^\top v = 0\}$ be the tied boundary subset ($|\mathcal{B}| \ge 2$). Along $\beta(c) = c\,v$, non-boundary rows satisfy $\hat p_i(c) \to y_i$, while boundary rows remain pinned at $z_i = 0$ ($\hat p_i = 0.5$), driving $J(c\,v)$ strictly downward toward the nonzero unattained infimum $\frac{|\mathcal{B}|\ln 2}{n}$ as $c \to \infty$. Again, no finite MLE exists!
    3. Overlap: for every $v \in \mathbb{R}^{d+1}\setminus\{0\}$, at least one observation lies strictly on the wrong side ($y_i^{\pm}(\tilde x_i^\top v) < 0$). Then $J(c\,v) \ge \frac{c}{n}|v^\top \tilde x_i| \to +\infty$ as $c\to\infty$ in every direction ($J$ is coercive), so a unique finite MLE $\hat\beta$ exists whenever $\operatorname{rank}(X) = d+1$.

    In small clinical or epidemiological studies where an unshrunk odds ratio is desired under separation, Firth's (1993) penalized log-likelihood $\ell_{\mathrm{Firth}}(\beta) = \ell(\beta) + \frac{1}{2}\ln\det\!\big(X^\top W(\beta) X\big)$ (equivalent to Jeffreys' invariant prior; Heinze & Schemper, 2002) both removes $O(n^{-1})$ small-sample bias and guarantees finite estimates because $\det(X^\top W X) \to 0 \implies \frac{1}{2}\ln\det(X^\top W X) \to -\infty$ whenever $\|\beta\|_2 \to \infty$.

    Ridge ($L_2$), Lasso ($L_1$), and Why the Intercept Is Unpenalized

    Partition $\beta = (\beta_0, \beta_{1:d}^\top)^\top$ into the scalar intercept $\beta_0 \in \mathbb{R}$ and the $d$ feature slopes $\beta_{1:d} = (\beta_1,\dots,\beta_d)^\top \in \mathbb{R}^d$. For tuning parameter $\lambda > 0$ (note that scikit-learn parameterizes the summed objective by $C = \frac{1}{n\lambda}$), the $L_2$ (ridge) and $L_1$ (lasso) objectives penalize only $\beta_{1:d}$:

    $$J_{L_2}(\beta) = \frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\big[\operatorname{softplus}(z_i) - y_i z_i\big] + \frac{\lambda}{2}\sum_{j=1}^{d}\beta_j^2,$$ $$J_{L_1}(\beta) = \frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\big[\operatorname{softplus}(z_i) - y_i z_i\big] + \lambda\sum_{j=1}^{d}|\beta_j|.$$
    • $L_2$ regularization (smooth, $\lambda$-strongly convex on $\beta_{1:d}$): adds $\lambda\beta_{1:d}$ to the slope gradient and $\lambda I_d$ to the slope Hessian block $\frac{1}{n}X_{1:d}^\top W X_{1:d} + \lambda I_d \succeq \lambda I_d$. Even under exact collinearity ($x_2 = x_1$) or separation, the slope solution is unique and shares weight equally across identical standardized predictors.
    • $L_1$ regularization (sparse via KKT subdifferential optimality): $|\beta_j|$ is non-differentiable at $0$ with subdifferential $\partial|\beta_j|\big|_0 = [-1, +1]$. Coordinate $j \ge 1$ is optimal at $\hat\beta$ if and only if $$\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}(\hat p_i - y_i)x_{ij} = -\lambda\operatorname{sign}(\hat\beta_j)\;\;\text{if }\hat\beta_j \ne 0, \qquad \left|\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}(\hat p_i - y_i)x_{ij}\right| \le \lambda\;\;\text{if }\hat\beta_j = 0.$$ Features whose correlation with residuals is smaller than $\lambda$ are set identically to $0$ (solved via proximal coordinate descent / SAGA). Elastic Net $\lambda\big[\alpha\|\beta_{1:d}\|_1 + \frac{1-\alpha}{2}\|\beta_{1:d}\|_2^2\big]$ combines sparsity with strict convexity on correlated feature groups (Zou & Hastie, 2005).
    Always exclude $\beta_0$ from the penalty—and standardize $x_{1:d}$ inside each CV fold! Because $\beta_0$ is unpenalized, $\frac{\partial J_{\mathrm{pen}}}{\partial \beta_0} = \frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^n(\hat p_i - y_i) = 0$ still enforces $\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^n \hat p_i = \bar y$ (exact mean calibration); penalizing $\beta_0$ artificially drags rare-event probabilities toward $0.5$. (One degenerate edge case: if all labels belong to one class, $y_i \equiv 0$, an unpenalized $\beta_0$ still diverges to $-\infty$.) Because penalties are not scale invariant, standardize every non-intercept feature using training-fold mean and standard deviation before fitting.

    Bayesian MAP Equivalence—With Explicit Prior Normalizing Constants

    Penalized MLE coincides with maximum a posteriori (MAP) estimation under independent zero-mean priors on $\beta_{1:d}\in\mathbb{R}^d$ and a flat improper prior $p(\beta_0)\propto 1$ on the intercept. Writing out the normalized prior densities explicitly clarifies both fixed-$\lambda$ optimization and hyperparameter learning:

    Gaussian prior $\beta_j \stackrel{\mathrm{i.i.d.}}{\sim} \mathcal{N}(0, \tau^2)$ ($L_2$ penalty):

    $$p(\beta_{1:d}\mid \tau^2) = (2\pi\tau^2)^{-d/2}\exp\!\left(-\frac{\|\beta_{1:d}\|_2^2}{2\tau^2}\right), \qquad \ln p(\beta_{1:d}\mid \tau^2) = -\frac{d}{2}\ln(2\pi\tau^2) - \frac{1}{2\tau^2}\sum_{j=1}^{d}\beta_j^2.$$

    Laplace prior $\beta_j \stackrel{\mathrm{i.i.d.}}{\sim} \mathrm{Laplace}(0, b)$ ($L_1$ penalty):

    $$p(\beta_{1:d}\mid b) = (2b)^{-d}\exp\!\left(-\frac{\|\beta_{1:d}\|_1}{b}\right), \qquad \ln p(\beta_{1:d}\mid b) = -d\ln(2b) - \frac{1}{b}\sum_{j=1}^{d}|\beta_j|.$$

    Maximizing $\ell(\beta) + \ln p(\beta_{1:d})$ and dividing by $-n$ yields $J_{L_2}(\beta)$ with $\lambda = \frac{1}{n\tau^2}$ and $J_{L_1}(\beta)$ with $\lambda = \frac{1}{nb}$. At a fixed $(\tau^2, b)$, the log-normalizing constants $-\frac{d}{2}\ln(2\pi\tau^2)$ and $-d\ln(2b)$ do not depend on $\beta$ and drop out of $\arg\max_\beta$. However, if $(\tau^2, b)$ are estimated from data via Empirical Bayes (maximizing the marginal likelihood $\int L(\beta)p(\beta_{1:d}\mid\tau^2)\,d\beta_{1:d}$, e.g., via Laplace approximation), those normalizing constants are essential—without $-\frac{d}{2}\ln(2\pi\tau^2)$, maximizing over $\tau^2$ would trivially send $\tau^2 \to \infty$ ($\lambda \to 0$)!

    Regularization Is Not Class Weighting

    Practitioners sometimes confuse penalty strength $\lambda$ with class weights $w_c$. They act on orthogonal parts of the optimization problem: class weighting reweights rows inside the empirical risk $\sum_i \omega_{y_i}\ell_i(z_i,y_i)$ to reflect unequal misclassification costs or case-control sampling—and cannot stop a separating ray $c\,v$ from driving every weighted loss toward zero! Regularization bounds $\|\beta_{1:d}\|$ in parameter space so the objective stays coercive:

    Structural distinction between class weighting and coefficient regularization.
    PropertyClass weighting ($\omega_0, \omega_1$)Coefficient regularization ($\lambda\|\beta_{1:d}\|$)

    Verify Complete vs. Quasi-Complete Separation and MAP Identities in Python

    Show the complete numerical check 
    import numpy as np
from scipy.special import expit

def newton_trace(x, y, lam, steps=8):
    X = np.column_stack([np.ones_like(x), x])
    n = len(x)
    b = np.zeros(2)
    b1_hist, J_hist = [], []
    for s in range(steps + 1):
        z = X @ b
        p = expit(z)
        J = np.mean(np.maximum(z, 0) - y * z + np.log1p(np.exp(-np.abs(z)))) + 0.5 * lam * b[1] ** 2
        b1_hist.append(b[1])
        J_hist.append(J)
        if s < steps:
            g = (X.T @ (p - y)) / n + np.array([0.0, lam * b[1]])
            H = (X.T @ np.diag(p * (1 - p)) @ X) / n + np.diag([0.0, lam])
            b = b - np.linalg.solve(H, g)
    return np.array(b1_hist), np.array(J_hist), b

xc = np.array([-3.0, -2.0, -1.0, 1.0, 2.0, 3.0])
yc = np.array([0.0, 0.0, 0.0, 1.0, 1.0, 1.0])
xq = np.array([-2.0, -1.0, 0.0, 0.0, 1.0, 2.0])
yq = np.array([0.0, 0.0, 0.0, 1.0, 1.0, 1.0])

bc, Jc, _ = newton_trace(xc, yc, lam=0.0, steps=10)
bq, Jq, _ = newton_trace(xq, yq, lam=0.0, steps=10)
bl2, Jl2, b_hat = newton_trace(xc, yc, lam=0.5, steps=10)

assert Jc[-1] < 1e-4  # Approaches 0 under complete separation
assert np.isclose(Jq[-1], 2 * np.log(2) / 6, atol=1e-4)  # Approaches (2 ln 2)/6 under quasi-separation!
assert np.isclose(b_hat[1], 0.70149, atol=1e-4)
assert np.isclose(np.mean(expit(b_hat[0] + b_hat[1] * xc)), np.mean(yc))
print(f"Complete J_10={Jc[-1]:.5f}, Quasi J_10={Jq[-1]:.5f} (bound={2*np.log(2)/6:.5f}), L2 b1={b_hat[1]:.4f}")
