    Maximum Likelihood and the Log-Loss Derivation

    July 2026 · 20 min read

    Once the sigmoid $\hat p_i = \sigma(\tilde x_i^\top \beta) = (1 + e^{-\tilde x_i^\top \beta})^{-1}$ maps linear scores into $(0,1)$, how should we measure whether a candidate parameter vector $\beta$ fits observed binary labels $y_i \in \{0,1\}$? A natural first instinct from linear regression is to minimize squared probability residuals $\sum_{i=1}^n (y_i - \sigma(\tilde x_i^\top \beta))^2$. Yet composing squared error with the saturating sigmoid destroys convexity and stalls gradient-based learning precisely when a prediction is most wrong.

    Maximum likelihood estimation (MLE) under a conditional Bernoulli model leads instead to log-loss (binary cross-entropy). In one stroke, log-loss cancels the sigmoid's flat tail derivative so that gradients stay proportional to the raw probability error $\hat p_i - y_i$, yields a globally convex objective, and acts as a strictly proper scoring rule whose population minimum recovers true conditional probabilities.

    Why Squared Error on Sigmoid Probabilities Fails

    Consider a single negative observation ($y = 0$) scored at logit $z = \tilde x^\top \beta \in \mathbb{R}$ with predicted probability $\hat p = \sigma(z)$. Under scaled squared error $L_{\mathrm{SE}}(z) = 2(\sigma(z) - 0)^2 = 2\sigma(z)^2$ (scaled by $2$ so both losses have curvature $1/4$ near $z \to -\infty$? More directly, at $z=0$, $2\sigma(0)^2 = 0.50$ vs $\ln 2 \approx 0.693$), differentiating with $\sigma'(z) = \sigma(z)(1-\sigma(z))$ gives:

    $$\frac{d}{dz}\big[2\sigma(z)^2\big] = 4\sigma(z)^2\big(1-\sigma(z)\big), \qquad \frac{d^2}{dz^2}\big[2\sigma(z)^2\big] = 4\sigma(z)^2\big(1-\sigma(z)\big)\big(2 - 3\sigma(z)\big).$$

    Two pathologies appear immediately when the model is confidently wrong ($z \gg 0$, so $\sigma(z) \to 1$ while $y = 0$):

    • Vanishing gradient on glaring errors: as $z \to +\infty$, $1 - \sigma(z) \approx e^{-z} \to 0$, so the gradient $4\sigma(z)^2(1-\sigma(z))$ decays exponentially to zero. The more confidently wrong the prediction, the weaker the corrective gradient!
    • Non-convexity: whenever $\sigma(z) > \frac{2}{3}$ (equivalently $z > \ln 2 \approx 0.6931$), the factor $(2 - 3\sigma(z))$ is strictly negative, turning $2\sigma(z)^2$ concave. Summing non-convex per-row plateaus across features can create flat saddles and spurious local minima in $\beta$.

    By contrast, Bernoulli log-loss for $y = 0$ is $-\ln(1 - \sigma(z)) = \ln(1 + e^z)$, whose first derivative is $\sigma(z) \to 1$ as $z \to +\infty$ (full unit pushback on confident mistakes) and whose second derivative is $\sigma(z)(1-\sigma(z)) > 0$ for every finite $z$.

    Bernoulli Likelihood, Cross-Entropy, and KL Divergence

    Let row $i \in \{1,\dots,n\}$ have observed binary outcome $y_i \in \{0,1\}$, augmented predictor vector $\tilde x_i = (1, x_{i1}, \dots, x_{id})^\top \in \mathbb{R}^{d+1}$ (stacked as rows of $X \in \mathbb{R}^{n \times (d+1)}$), coefficient vector $\beta = (\beta_0, \beta_1, \dots, \beta_d)^\top \in \mathbb{R}^{d+1}$, linear logit $z_i = \tilde x_i^\top \beta$, and model probability $\hat p_i = \sigma(z_i) \in (0,1)$. A single compact Bernoulli probability mass function covers both outcomes $y_i=1$ and $y_i=0$:

    $$\Pr(Y_i = y_i \mid x_i; \beta) = \hat p_i^{\,y_i}(1 - \hat p_i)^{1 - y_i}.$$

    Assuming the labels $Y_1,\dots,Y_n$ are conditionally independent given $X$, the joint likelihood $L(\beta)$ multiplies across rows, and taking the natural logarithm converts the underflow-prone product into an additive log-likelihood $\ell(\beta)$:

    $$L(\beta) = \prod_{i=1}^{n} \hat p_i^{\,y_i}(1 - \hat p_i)^{1 - y_i}, \qquad \ell(\beta) = \ln L(\beta) = \sum_{i=1}^{n}\Big[y_i \ln \hat p_i + (1 - y_i)\ln(1 - \hat p_i)\Big].$$

    Optimization frameworks minimize rather than maximize and divide by $n$ so step sizes stay comparable across sample sizes, defining log-loss (mean negative log-likelihood, or empirical binary cross-entropy):

    $$J(\beta) = -\frac{1}{n}\ell(\beta) = -\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\Big[y_i \ln \hat p_i + (1 - y_i)\ln(1 - \hat p_i)\Big].$$

    Why does minimizing log-loss incentivize honest probabilities? Suppose at feature vector $x$ the true data-generating probability is $q = \Pr(Y=1 \mid X=x) \in (0,1)$ and a model reports $p \in (0,1)$. The expected pointwise log-loss decomposes into the irreducible Shannon entropy $H(q)$ plus a Kullback–Leibler divergence:

    $$\mathbb{E}_{Y\sim\mathrm{Bern}(q)}\big[-\!\{Y\ln p + (1-Y)\ln(1-p)\}\big] = \underbrace{-q\ln q - (1-q)\ln(1-q)}_{H(q)\text{ (irreducible noise)}} + \underbrace{q\ln\frac{q}{p} + (1-q)\ln\frac{1-q}{1-p}}_{D_{\mathrm{KL}}(\mathrm{Bern}(q)\,\|\,\mathrm{Bern}(p))\,\ge\,0}.$$

    Because $D_{\mathrm{KL}}(\mathrm{Bern}(q)\,\|\,\mathrm{Bern}(p)) \ge 0$ with equality if and only if $p = q$, log-loss is a strictly proper scoring rule (Gneiting & Raftery, 2007): in the population limit, no forecast can beat reporting the true conditional probability $q$.

    Overflow-Safe Logit Form and Sign-Aware Softplus

    Evaluating $J(\beta)$ in two stages—first rounding $\hat p_i = \sigma(z_i)$ in IEEE-754 double precision and then calling $\ln(\hat p_i)$ and $\ln(1-\hat p_i)$—is numerically fragile. Substituting $\hat p_i = (1+e^{-z_i})^{-1} = e^{z_i}(1+e^{z_i})^{-1}$ and $1-\hat p_i = (1+e^{z_i})^{-1}$ directly into the single-row loss $\ell_i(z_i, y_i) = -[y_i\ln\hat p_i + (1-y_i)\ln(1-\hat p_i)]$ cancels the intermediate probability:

    $$\ell_i(z_i, y_i) = -y_i\ln\frac{e^{z_i}}{1+e^{z_i}} - (1-y_i)\ln\frac{1}{1+e^{z_i}} = \ln(1 + e^{z_i}) - y_i z_i = \operatorname{softplus}(z_i) - y_i z_i.$$

    Even $\ln(1 + e^z)$ overflows 64-bit floating point once $z > 709.78$ ($e^{800} = +\infty$). Factoring out $e^{\max(z,0)}$ gives the sign-aware softplus identity, whose exponential argument $-|z| \le 0$ always lies in $(0,1]$:

    $$\operatorname{softplus}(z) = \max(z, 0) + \ln\!\big(1 + e^{-|z|}\big), \qquad \ell_i(z_i, y_i) = \max(z_i, 0) - y_i z_i + \ln\!\big(1 + e^{-|z_i|}\big).$$

    Implemented with log1p(exp(-abs(z))), every term stays finite and exact across the entire IEEE-754 double range:

    Score Vector, Batch Gradient, and Fisher Information Hessian

    Differentiating $\ell_i(z_i, y_i) = \ln(1 + e^{z_i}) - y_i z_i$ with respect to $z_i$ yields $\frac{\partial \ell_i}{\partial z_i} = \frac{e^{z_i}}{1+e^{z_i}} - y_i = \hat p_i - y_i$. Applying the chain rule with $\nabla_\beta z_i = \tilde x_i$ gives both the log-likelihood score vector $g(\beta) = \nabla\ell(\beta)$ and the mean log-loss gradient $\nabla J(\beta)$:

    $$g(\beta) = \nabla\ell(\beta) = \sum_{i=1}^{n}(y_i - \hat p_i)\tilde x_i = X^\top(y - \hat p), \qquad \nabla J(\beta) = -\frac{1}{n}g(\beta) = \frac{1}{n}X^\top(\hat p - y),$$

    where $y = (y_1,\dots,y_n)^\top \in \{0,1\}^n$ and $\hat p = (\hat p_1,\dots,\hat p_n)^\top \in (0,1)^n$. Setting the first coordinate of $g(\hat\beta)=0$ (corresponding to the intercept column $\mathbf{1}_n$) immediately proves the calibration-in-the-large identity on the training sample: $\sum_{i=1}^n \hat p_i = \sum_{i=1}^n y_i$, so $\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^n \hat p_i = \bar y$.

    Differentiating a second time using $\nabla_\beta \hat p_i = \sigma'(z_i)\tilde x_i = \hat p_i(1-\hat p_i)\tilde x_i$ produces the Hessian matrices of $\ell(\beta)$ and $J(\beta)$:

    $$\nabla^2\ell(\beta) = -X^\top W(\beta) X, \qquad \nabla^2 J(\beta) = \frac{1}{n}X^\top W(\beta) X, \qquad W(\beta) = \operatorname{diag}\!\big(\hat p_1(1-\hat p_1),\dots,\hat p_n(1-\hat p_n)\big).$$

    Notice that the observed labels $y_i$ cancel completely out of $\nabla^2\ell(\beta)$ because the logit is the canonical link of the Bernoulli exponential family (McCullagh & Nelder, 1989). Consequently, the observed Fisher information $-\nabla^2\ell(\beta) = X^\top W X$ and expected Fisher information $\mathbb{E}[-\nabla^2\ell(\beta)\mid X]$ coincide identically, making Newton–Raphson and Fisher scoring the exact same algorithm.

    Convexity vs. Strict Convexity: Separating Uniqueness from Existence

    For any vector $v \in \mathbb{R}^{d+1}$, the quadratic form of the Hessian is a weighted sum of squares:

    $$v^\top \nabla^2 J(\beta)\,v = \frac{1}{n}v^\top X^\top W(\beta) X v = \frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n} \underbrace{\hat p_i(1-\hat p_i)}_{w_i \in (0,\,0.25]}\,(\tilde x_i^\top v)^2 = \frac{1}{n}\big\|W(\beta)^{1/2} X v\big\|_2^2 \ge 0.$$

    Therefore $\nabla^2 J(\beta) \succeq 0$ everywhere on $\mathbb{R}^{d+1}$: $J(\beta)$ is globally convex (and $\ell(\beta)$ is globally concave) for every design matrix $X$, ruling out spurious non-global local minima. However, rigorous theory separates two distinct questions that are frequently conflated:

    Uniqueness vs. existence of the unregularized MLE (Albert & Anderson, 1984).
    • Uniqueness of any finite minimizer (governed by $\operatorname{rank}(X)$): at every finite $\beta \in \mathbb{R}^{d+1}$, each logit $z_i = \tilde x_i^\top \beta$ is finite, so $\hat p_i \in (0,1)$ and $w_i = \hat p_i(1-\hat p_i) > 0$ strictly. If $\operatorname{rank}(X) = d+1$ (no collinear columns) and $v \ne 0$, then $Xv \ne 0$, so at least one $(\tilde x_i^\top v)^2 > 0$ and $v^\top \nabla^2 J(\beta) v > 0$. Hence $J(\beta)$ is strictly convex on $\mathbb{R}^{d+1}$: if a finite minimizer $\hat\beta$ exists, it is guaranteed to be unique. (If $\operatorname{rank}(X) < d+1$, nullspace vectors $Xv = 0$ create flat affine ridges of equivalent parameters.)
    • Existence of a finite minimizer (governed by class overlap): strict convexity on $\mathbb{R}^{d+1}$ does not guarantee that $\inf_\beta J(\beta)$ is attained at a finite $\beta$—just as $f(t) = e^{-t}$ satisfies $f''(t) = e^{-t} > 0$ everywhere on $\mathbb{R}$ yet has no finite minimizer because $f(t) \downarrow 0$ as $t \to +\infty$. Albert & Anderson (1984) proved that a finite unregularized MLE $\hat\beta$ exists if and only if the data exhibits class overlap (neither complete nor quasi-complete separation). Under separation, a ray $\beta = c\,v$ pushes $\hat p_i \to y_i$ and $w_i \to 0$ as $c \to +\infty$, driving $\|\beta^{(t)}\|_2 \to \infty$ unless a regularizer (such as $\frac{\lambda}{2}\|\beta_{1:d}\|_2^2$, which makes $J_\lambda$ $\lambda$-strongly convex and coercive) is added.

    Worked Five-Row Dataset: Hand-Checked Score, Hessian, and Newton Step

    To check every matrix formula by hand, take $n = 5$ observations with centered predictor $x = (-2, -1, 0, 1, 2)^\top$ and overlapping labels $y = (0, 1, 0, 1, 1)^\top$ ($y_2=1$ at $x_2=-1$ overlaps with $y_3=0$ at $x_3=0$, and $\operatorname{rank}(X)=2$, so a unique finite MLE exists). Start from $\beta^{(0)} = (0, 0)^\top$:

    • At $\beta^{(0)}=(0,0)^\top$: every $z_i^{(0)}=0$, $\hat p_i^{(0)}=0.5$, $w_i^{(0)}=0.25$, $\ell(\beta^{(0)}) = 5\ln(0.5) = -3.465736$, and $J(\beta^{(0)}) = \ln 2 = 0.693147$.
    • Score vector: $\sum_{i=1}^5 (y_i - 0.5) = 3 - 2.5 = 0.5$ and $\sum_{i=1}^5 x_i(y_i - 0.5) = (-2)(-0.5) + (-1)(0.5) + 0(-0.5) + 1(0.5) + 2(0.5) = 1.0 - 0.5 + 0 + 0.5 + 1.0 = 2.0$, so $g(\beta^{(0)}) = (0.5, 2.0)^\top$ and $\nabla J(\beta^{(0)}) = (-0.1, -0.4)^\top$.
    • Fisher information: since $\sum x_i = 0$ and $\sum x_i^2 = 4+1+0+1+4 = 10$, $X^\top W^{(0)} X = 0.25\begin{bmatrix}5 & 0 \\ 0 & 10\end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix}1.25 & 0 \\ 0 & 2.50\end{bmatrix}$.
    • Exact first Newton step: $\beta^{(1)} = \beta^{(0)} + (X^\top W^{(0)} X)^{-1} g(\beta^{(0)}) = (0.5/1.25,\; 2.0/2.50)^\top = (0.4000, 0.8000)^\top$, slashing $J(\beta)$ from $0.693147$ to $0.495905$ in one step!

    Assumptions, Failure Modes, and Numerical Verification

    • Conditional independence across rows: repeated measurements per patient, user, or cluster violate $L(\beta)=\prod_{i=1}^n \Pr(y_i\mid x_i;\beta)$; while the point estimate solves generalized estimating equations under an independence working correlation, naive Fisher-information standard errors $(X^\top W X)^{-1}$ become invalid without cluster-robust (sandwich) variance or mixed effects.
    • No closed-form normal equation: because $\hat p = \sigma(X\beta)$ wraps the parameters inside a nonlinear sigmoid, $X^\top(y - \sigma(X\beta)) = 0$ is transcendental and requires iterative optimization.
    • Overlap vs. separation: verify both $\operatorname{rank}(X)=d+1$ (uniqueness) and bounded Newton steps (no complete or quasi-complete separation).
    Show the complete numerical check 
    import numpy as np
from scipy.special import expit

# 1. Verify IEEE-754 float64 softplus stability at z = 40 and z = 800
for z, y in [(0.0, 1), (10.0, 0), (40.0, 0), (40.0, 1), (800.0, 1)]:
    with np.errstate(all="ignore"):
        p = 1.0 / (1.0 + np.exp(-z))
        naive_prob = -(y * np.log(p) + (1 - y) * np.log(1 - p))
        naive_sp = np.log(1.0 + np.exp(z)) - y * z
    stable_sp = max(z, 0.0) - y * z + np.log1p(np.exp(-abs(z)))
    print(f"z={z:5.1f}, y={y}: naive_prob={naive_prob}, naive_sp={naive_sp}, stable={stable_sp:.6f}")

# 2. Verify 5-row score, Fisher information, Newton Step 1, and converged MLE
x = np.array([-2.0, -1.0, 0.0, 1.0, 2.0])
y = np.array([0.0, 1.0, 0.0, 1.0, 1.0])
X = np.column_stack([np.ones_like(x), x])

beta = np.zeros(2)
for k in range(6):
    z = X @ beta
    p = expit(z)
    J = np.mean(np.maximum(z, 0) - y * z + np.log1p(np.exp(-np.abs(z))))
    g = X.T @ (y - p)
    W = np.diag(p * (1.0 - p))
    F = X.T @ W @ X
    if k == 0:
        assert np.allclose(g, [0.5, 2.0])
        assert np.allclose(F, [[1.25, 0.0], [0.0, 2.50]])
    if k == 1:
        assert np.allclose(beta, [0.4, 0.8])
    print(f"k={k}: beta=({beta[0]:.6f}, {beta[1]:.6f}), J={J:.6f}, ||score||={np.linalg.norm(g):.2e}")
    beta = beta + np.linalg.solve(F, g)

assert np.isclose(np.sum(expit(X @ beta)), np.sum(y))
