    Linear Regression: Overview and Roadmap

    July 2026 · 18 min read

    Linear regression is the workhorse of quantitative modeling because every moving part—its geometry, numerical conditioning, sampling uncertainty, and failure modes—can be inspected transparently. Given $n$ observations of a continuous response $y_i$ and $p$ predictors $(x_{i1}, \ldots, x_{ip})$, the model decomposes each outcome into an explained linear combination plus an unexplained disturbance $\varepsilon_i$:

    $$y_i = \beta_0 + \beta_1 x_{i1} + \cdots + \beta_p x_{ip} + \varepsilon_i \qquad (i = 1, \ldots, n).$$

    Stacking all $n$ equations into vectors and matrices yields

    $$y = X\beta + \varepsilon.$$

    Here the word linear means linear in the parameter vector $\beta$, not restricted to straight lines in raw inputs: the columns of $X$ can hold $\log x_1$, $x_1^2$, spline bases, or interaction products $x_1 x_2$, and every theorem of linear regression still applies.

    Core notation used consistently throughout the twelve-part Linear Regression track.

    $\beta$$(p + 1) \times 1$Unknown population parameter vector $(\beta_0, \beta_1, \ldots, \beta_p)^\top$
    $\hat\beta$$(p + 1) \times 1$Sample estimator computed from the observed $(X, y)$
    $\varepsilon = y - X\beta$$n \times 1$Unobservable population disturbances around $X\beta$
    $\hat y = X\hat\beta = Hy$$n \times 1$In-sample fitted values (orthogonal projection of $y$ onto $\mathcal{C}(X)$ under OLS)
    $e = y - \hat y$$n \times 1$Observable sample residuals ($X^\top e = 0$ under OLS)
    Never conflate the intercept $\beta_0$, disturbance $\varepsilon_i$, and residual $e_i$. The constant $\beta_0$ shifts the entire hyperplane vertically so disturbances can average to zero. The disturbance $\varepsilon_i = y_i - x_i^\top \beta$ is an unobservable population random variable. The residual $e_i = y_i - x_i^\top \hat\beta$ is a known sample number computed after fitting, forced by OLS algebra to satisfy $\sum_{i=1}^n e_i = 0$ even when $E[\varepsilon \mid X] \ne 0$.

    The Four-Stage Linear Regression Lifecycle

    1. Target and feature design: Decide whether you need an out-of-sample prediction, a conditional descriptive slope, or an identified causal effect, and construct $X$ using only prediction-time information.
    2. Estimation and numerical factorization: Ordinary least squares minimizes $\text{RSS}(b) = \|y - Xb\|_2^2$, leading to normal equations $X^\top X \hat\beta = X^\top y$. When $\operatorname{rank}(X) = p + 1$, the minimizer is unique; in floating-point code, Householder QR or SVD avoids squaring the condition number of $X$.
    3. Out-of-sample evaluation: Compare training and held-out RMSE, MAE, and out-of-sample $R^2$ (measured against the training mean $\bar y_{\text{train}}$) to detect overfitting or shift.
    4. Residual and influence diagnostics: Inspect $e_i$ versus $\hat y_i$ for curvature or fan spread, check leverage $h_{ii}$ and Cook's distance $D_i$ for influential rows, check VIF/condition numbers for collinearity, and match standard errors (homoscedastic, HC3, or cluster-robust) to the error structure.

    Why Loss Functions Choose Different Population Targets

    Before fitting slopes, ask why least squares targets the conditional mean in the first place. At any fixed input $x$, choosing a prediction $a(x)$ to minimize expected loss $E[L(Y, a) \mid X = x]$ selects a specific functional of the conditional distribution $Y \mid X = x$:

    Population Bayes-optimal prediction $a^*(x) = \arg\min_a E[L(Y, a) \mid X = x]$ for common regression and probability losses.

    Complete Six-Row Worked Walkthrough: OLS, Sum-of-Squares Decomposition, and $R^2$

    The simplest constant baseline predicts $\bar y$ for every row, incurring Total Sum of Squares $\text{SST} = \sum_{i=1}^n (y_i - \bar y)^2$. When OLS includes an intercept, residual orthogonality ($\sum_{i=1}^n e_i = 0$ and $\sum_{i=1}^n \hat y_i e_i = 0$) splits $\text{SST}$ cleanly into explained Regression Sum of Squares (SSR) and unexplained Error Sum of Squares (SSE, also called RSS):

    $$\text{SST} = \text{SSR} + \text{SSE}, \qquad \text{SSR} = \sum_{i=1}^n (\hat y_i - \bar y)^2, \qquad \text{SSE} = \sum_{i=1}^n (y_i - \hat y_i)^2.$$

    The coefficient of determination is $R^2 = \frac{\text{SSR}}{\text{SST}} = 1 - \frac{\text{SSE}}{\text{SST}}$ (requiring non-constant $y$ so $\text{SST} > 0$). Let us verify every single quantity on six rows $x = (1, 2, 3, 4, 5, 6)$ and $y = (2.2, 3.8, 5.1, 5.6, 7.4, 8.3)$, where $\bar x = 3.5$, $\bar y = 5.4$, $S_{xx} = 17.5$, $S_{xy} = 20.9$, $\hat\beta_1 = \frac{20.9}{17.5} \approx 1.1943$, and $\hat\beta_0 = 5.4 - 3.5\hat\beta_1 = 1.2200$:

    Notice one special property of simple regression ($p = 1$ with an intercept): the sample correlation is $r = S_{xy} / \sqrt{S_{xx} S_{yy}} = 20.9 / \sqrt{17.5 \times 25.34} = 0.992485$, whose square $r^2 = 0.985026$ equals $R^2$ identically. With $p \ge 2$ predictors, $R^2$ equals the squared correlation between $y$ and the fitted vector $\hat y$, rather than any single predictor.

    Interview trap: High $R^2$ does not validate functional form or exogeneity. A straight line fitted to mildly curved data over a wide $x$ range routinely achieves $R^2 > 0.95$ while systematically underpredicting or overpredicting both ends. Always inspect residual plots alongside summary metrics.

    Reproduce Every Number on This Page

    Show the complete numerical check 
    import numpy as np

# 1. Six-row walkthrough: OLS, SST = SSR + SSE, R2, and slope SE
x = np.array([1., 2., 3., 4., 5., 6.])
y = np.array([2.2, 3.8, 5.1, 5.6, 7.4, 8.3])
X = np.column_stack([np.ones_like(x), x])

beta = np.linalg.lstsq(X, y, rcond=None)[0]
yhat = X @ beta
e = y - yhat
sst = np.sum((y - y.mean()) ** 2)
ssr = np.sum((yhat - y.mean()) ** 2)
sse = np.sum(e ** 2)
r2 = ssr / sst
sigma_hat = np.sqrt(sse / (len(y) - 2))
se_b1 = sigma_hat / np.sqrt(np.sum((x - x.mean()) ** 2))

assert np.allclose(beta, [1.22, 20.9 / 17.5])
assert np.isclose(sst, 25.34)
assert np.isclose(ssr + sse, sst)
assert np.isclose(r2, np.corrcoef(x, y)[0, 1] ** 2)
assert np.isclose(sigma_hat, 0.30798887, atol=1e-6)
assert np.isclose(se_b1, 0.07362342, atol=1e-6)
print(f"beta={beta}, SST={sst:.4f}, SSR={ssr:.4f}, SSE={sse:.4f}, R2={r2:.4f}, se={se_b1:.4f}")

# 2. Five-value optimal constant summaries on (1, 2, 3, 8, 9)
s = np.array([1., 2., 3., 8., 9.])
assert np.isclose(s.mean(), 4.6) and np.isclose(np.mean((s - 4.6) ** 2), 10.64)
assert np.isclose(np.median(s), 3.0) and np.isclose(np.mean(np.abs(s - 3.0)), 2.80)
# Pinball tau = 0.8 is flat at 0.88 across all a in [8, 9]
for a in [8.0, 8.5, 9.0]:
    pin = np.mean(np.where(s >= a, 0.8 * (s - a), 0.2 * (a - s)))
    assert np.isclose(pin, 0.88)
# Huber delta = 2 minimized at a = 3.5 (clipped residuals: -2, -1.5, -0.5, +2, +2 sum to 0)
r_h = s - 3.5
assert np.isclose(np.sum(np.clip(r_h, -2.0, 2.0)), 0.0)
huber_mean = np.mean(np.where(np.abs(r_h) <= 2.0, 0.5 * r_h ** 2, 2.0 * (np.abs(r_h) - 1.0)))
assert np.isclose(huber_mean, 4.05)
print("All loss-target summaries verified.")
