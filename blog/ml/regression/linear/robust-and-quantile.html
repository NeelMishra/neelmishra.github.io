    Robust and Quantile Regression

    July 2026 · 18 min read

    Ordinary least squares minimizes $\frac{1}{2}\sum_{i=1}^n r_i^2$, where $r_i = y_i - x_i^\top \beta$. Differentiating gives $\sum_{i=1}^n r_i x_i = 0$: every observation pulls on the fitted hyperplane with force proportional to its raw residual $r_i$. One corrupted sensor reading or heavy-tailed shock ($r_i = +20$) exerts twenty times the pull of a typical point ($r_i = +1$). Robust M-estimation (Huber, 1964) caps that pull so vertical outliers cannot hijack the center, while quantile regression (Koenker & Bassett, 1978) tilts the positive and negative slopes of absolute loss to model any conditional percentile $Q_\tau(Y \mid X = x)$ directly.

    Loss Functions, Score Functions $\psi(r)$, and Population Targets

    Consider M-estimators minimizing $\sum_{i=1}^n \rho(y_i - x_i^\top \beta)$ for convex residual loss $\rho(r)$:

    $$\rho_\delta^{\text{Huber}}(r) = \begin{cases} \frac{1}{2}r^2, & |r| \le \delta, \\ \delta\big(|r| - \frac{1}{2}\delta\big), & |r| > \delta, \end{cases} \qquad \rho_\tau^{\text{pinball}}(r) = r\big(\tau - \mathbf{1}\{r < 0\}\big) = \begin{cases} \tau r, & r \ge 0, \\ (1 - \tau)|r|, & r < 0, \end{cases}$$

    where $\delta > 0$ is Huber's clipping threshold and $\tau \in (0, 1)$ is the target quantile level (note that $\rho_{0.5}^{\text{pinball}}(r) = \frac{1}{2}|r|$, so $\tau = 0.5$ is identical to Least Absolute Deviations, LAD).

    Why Empirical Quantiles Have a Flat Interval When $n\tau$ Is an Integer

    Students are often surprised when linear-programming solvers and numpy.quantile(..., method='inverted_cdf') return slightly different constants on small samples. Let $y_{(1)} < y_{(2)} < \cdots < y_{(n)}$ be sorted observations and minimize empirical pinball risk $R_n(a) := \frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^n \rho_\tau(y_i - a)$. For any $a$ strictly between $y_{(k)}$ and $y_{(k+1)}$, exactly $k$ points lie below $a$ ($y_i - a < 0$) and $n - k$ lie above $a$ ($y_i - a > 0$), so

    $$\frac{d}{da} R_n(a) = \frac{1}{n}\Big[(1 - \tau)k - \tau(n - k)\Big] = \frac{k}{n} - \tau \qquad \forall a \in (y_{(k)}, y_{(k+1)}).$$

    Look at what happens in the two possible arithmetic cases:

    • $n\tau \notin \mathbb{Z}$: The derivative jumps strictly from negative ($\frac{\lfloor n\tau \rfloor}{n} - \tau < 0$) to positive ($\frac{\lfloor n\tau \rfloor + 1}{n} - \tau > 0$) right at the single order statistic $y_{(\lceil n\tau \rceil)}$, which is the unique minimizer.
    • $n\tau = k \in \{1, \ldots, n - 1\}$ is an exact integer: On the entire open interval $(y_{(k)}, y_{(k+1)})$, $\frac{d}{da}R_n(a) = \frac{k}{n} - \tau = 0$! Every value $a \in [y_{(k)}, y_{(k+1)}]$ achieves the exact same global minimum pinball loss:
      • On the even-sized sample $(2, 5, 8, 9, 11, 16)$ ($n = 6$, median $\tau = 0.5 \implies n\tau = 3$), every $a \in [8, 9]$ yields identical $\text{MAE} = 3.50$; the left-continuous inverse empirical CDF $F_n^{-1}(\tau) := \inf\{y : F_n(y) \ge \tau\}$ selects the left endpoint $8.0$, whereas the sample median convention averages endpoints to $8.5$.
      • On $(1, 2, 3, 8, 9)$ ($n = 5$, $\tau = 0.8 \implies n\tau = 4$), every $a \in [8, 9]$ achieves minimum total pinball loss $4.40$ (mean $0.88$), with $F_n^{-1}(0.8) = 8.0$.

    Influence Functions, Kink-Point Regularity, and Leverage Blindness

    For an M-functional $\beta(F)$ solving $m(b, F) := E_F[\psi(Y - X^\top b)X] = 0$, contaminating $F$ by $\varepsilon$-mass at $(x_0, y_0)$ ($F_\varepsilon = (1 - \varepsilon)F + \varepsilon \delta_{(x_0, y_0)}$) and differentiating at $\varepsilon = 0$ yields

    $$\operatorname{IF}(x_0, y_0; \beta, F) = M^{-1} \psi\big(y_0 - x_0^\top \beta(F)\big)\,x_0, \qquad M := -\frac{\partial}{\partial b^\top} E_F\big[\psi(Y - X^\top b)X\big]\Big|_{b = \beta(F)}.$$

    State the smoothness condition honestly! For smooth $\rho$ (or piecewise-$C^1$ Huber when $P(|Y - X^\top\beta| = \delta) = 0$), differentiating inside the expectation gives $M = E_F[\psi'(Y - X^\top \beta) X X^\top]$. For LAD and pinball loss, however, $\psi_\tau(r) = \tau - \mathbf{1}\{r < 0\}$ is a step function whose pointwise derivative $\psi_\tau'(r)$ is zero for $r \ne 0$ and undefined at the kink $r = 0$! Instead, one differentiates the conditional CDF directly: $E_F[\psi_\tau(Y - X^\top b)X] = E_X\big[X\big(\tau - F_{Y\mid X}(X^\top b \mid X)\big)\big]$. Assuming the conditional error $\varepsilon_\tau = Y - X^\top \beta_\tau$ has a continuous density $f_{\varepsilon_\tau \mid X}(0 \mid X) > 0$ strictly positive at zero, the Jacobian exists and equals

    $$M_\tau = E\big[f_{\varepsilon_\tau \mid X}(0 \mid X)\,X X^\top\big], \qquad \sqrt{n}(\hat\beta_\tau - \beta_\tau) \xrightarrow{d} \mathcal{N}\!\left(0,\; \tau(1 - \tau)\,M_\tau^{-1} E[X X^\top] M_\tau^{-1}\right).$$

    When errors are i.i.d. with density $f_\varepsilon(0) > 0$ at the median ($\tau = 0.5$), this simplifies to $\frac{1}{4 f_\varepsilon(0)^2}(E[X X^\top])^{-1}$ (explaining why sample medians have asymptotic variance $\frac{\pi}{2}\sigma^2 \approx 1.571\sigma^2$ under Gaussian noise where $f(0) = \frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi}\sigma}$, yet beat OLS decisively under Laplace or $t_3$ tails!).

    Crucial limitation: Bounded $\psi(r)$ does NOT protect against high-leverage $X$-outliers! In $\operatorname{IF}(x_0, y_0) = M^{-1}\psi(r_0)x_0$, Huber, LAD, and quantile regression bound $\psi(r_0)$, making them robust to vertical $y$-contamination, but the factor $x_0$ remains completely unbounded! A single corrupted row with $x_0 = 1000$ and bad $y_0$ can tilt OLS, Huber, and LAD alike (requiring high-breakdown estimators like MM-regression or LTS when $X$ itself can be corrupted).

    Algorithms (Scale-Equivariant Huber IRLS & Quantile LP) and Outlier Benchmark

    • Huber Iteratively Reweighted Least Squares (IRLS) & scale equivariance: Writing $\psi_\delta(r) = w(r) r$ with $w(r) := \min(1, \delta / |r|)$ turns $\sum_i \psi_\delta(r_i) x_i = 0$ into weighted least squares $\hat\beta^{(t+1)} = (X^\top W^{(t)} X)^{-1} X^\top W^{(t)} y$. Because fixed $\delta$ is not scale-invariant (multiplying $y$ by $1000$ changes which points exceed $\delta$), always set $\delta = 1.345\,\hat\sigma_{\text{MAD}}$ using a robust scale such as $\hat\sigma_{\text{MAD}} = 1.4826\,\operatorname{median}_i |r_i - \operatorname{median}(r)|$ ($1.345\sigma$ achieves $95\%$ asymptotic efficiency at the normal distribution).
    • Koenker-Bassett Linear Program (LP): Split each residual into nonnegative slack variables $r_i = u_i - v_i$ ($u_i \ge 0, v_i \ge 0$). At optimality $u_i v_i = 0$, so $u_i = \max(r_i, 0)$ and $v_i = \max(-r_i, 0)$, turning quantile regression into a standard LP solved exactly by simplex or interior-point (HiGHS) methods: $$\min_{\beta \in \mathbb{R}^k,\,u \ge 0,\,v \ge 0} \;\tau \mathbf{1}_n^\top u + (1 - \tau)\mathbf{1}_n^\top v \qquad \text{subject to} \qquad X\beta + u - v = y.$$
    • Quantile prediction bands vs. OLS intervals: Recall from Part 3 that $\hat\sigma\sqrt{1 + x_0^\top(X^\top X)^{-1}x_0}$ applies only to homoscedastic Gaussian OLS. Fitting $\hat\beta_{0.05}$ and $\hat\beta_{0.95}$ directly builds an asymmetric, heteroscedasticity-adaptive $90\%$ prediction band $[x_0^\top \hat\beta_{0.05},\, x_0^\top \hat\beta_{0.95}]$ (calibratable in finite samples via split conformalized quantile regression; check that separate quantile lines do not cross inside your feature domain).

    Show the complete numerical check 
    import numpy as np
from scipy import optimize

# 1. Flat empirical pinball plateaus when n * tau is an integer
even_s = np.array([2., 5., 8., 9., 11., 16.])
for a in [8.0, 8.5, 9.0]:
    assert np.isclose(np.mean(np.abs(even_s - a)), 3.50)

s5 = np.array([1., 2., 3., 8., 9.])
for a in [8.0, 8.4, 9.0]:
    pin = np.mean(np.where(s5 >= a, 0.8 * (s5 - a), 0.2 * (a - s5)))
    assert np.isclose(pin, 0.88)

# 2. Seven-row vertical-outlier benchmark: OLS, Huber IRLS (delta=1.5), LAD, and Q(0.9)
x = np.arange(1., 8.)
y = np.array([3.1, 4.9, 7.2, 8.8, 11.1, 12.9, 30.0])
X = np.column_stack([np.ones_like(x), x])

b_ols = np.linalg.lstsq(X, y, rcond=None)[0]
assert np.allclose(b_ols, [-3.22857143, 3.59285714])

# Huber IRLS with delta = 1.5
b_hub = b_ols.copy()
delta = 1.5
for _ in range(200):
    r = y - X @ b_hub
    w = np.where(np.abs(r) <= delta, 1.0, delta / np.abs(r))
    b_hub = np.linalg.solve(X.T @ (w[:, None] * X), X.T @ (w * y))
assert np.allclose(b_hub, [0.28, 2.27714286])
assert np.allclose(X.T @ np.clip(y - X @ b_hub, -delta, delta), [0.0, 0.0], atol=1e-11)

# Exact LP solver for quantile regression
def fit_quantile_lp(X_mat, y_vec, tau):
    n_rows, k_cols = X_mat.shape
    c = np.concatenate([np.zeros(k_cols), np.full(n_rows, tau), np.full(n_rows, 1.0 - tau)])
    A_eq = np.hstack([X_mat, np.eye(n_rows), -np.eye(n_rows)])
    bounds = [(None, None)] * k_cols + [(0.0, None)] * (2 * n_rows)
    res = optimize.linprog(c, A_eq=A_eq, b_eq=y_vec, bounds=bounds, method="highs")
    return res.x[:k_cols]

b_lad = fit_quantile_lp(X, y, 0.5)
b_q90 = fit_quantile_lp(X, y, 0.9)
assert np.allclose(b_lad, [1.1, 2.0])
assert np.allclose(b_q90, [-1.38333333, 4.48333333])
# Confirm LAD invariance when outlier y_7 increases from 30 to 3000!
y_extreme = y.copy(); y_extreme[-1] = 3000.0
assert np.allclose(fit_quantile_lp(X, y_extreme, 0.5), b_lad)
print("All flat-plateau, Huber IRLS, and Koenker-Bassett LP checks verified.")
