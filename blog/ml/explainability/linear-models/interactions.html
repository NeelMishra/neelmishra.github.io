    When One Feature Changes Another Feature’s Effect

    October 2026 · 9 min read

    Our additive linear model from Part 1 added 5 dollars per monthly visit for every customer regardless of membership tier. What if an extra visit generates more spending for Premium members than for Basic members? Adding a product term lets a linear model capture that synergy directly—and gives us the cleanest possible laboratory for understanding main-effect coefficients, centering, and how Shapley values divide joint credit.

    Let the visit rate depend on the plan

    Before writing the interaction equation, define every symbol explicitly:

    • $\hat{y} \in \mathbb{R}$: predicted monthly spend in USD.
    • $v \ge 0$: number of monthly store visits.
    • $p \in \{0, 1\}$: binary membership indicator ($p = 0$ for Basic, $p = 1$ for Premium).
    • $\beta_0 = 20$: baseline spend (USD) at $v = 0$ visits on the Basic plan ($p = 0$).
    • $\beta_v = 5$: main-effect visit slope (USD per visit) when $p = 0$.
    • $\beta_p = 10$: main-effect plan gap (USD) when $v = 0$.
    • $\beta_{vp} = 2$: bilinear interaction coefficient (extra USD per visit added specifically when $p = 1$).

    With those quantities defined, our illustrative interaction model is:

    $$\hat{y} = \beta_0 + \beta_v v + \beta_p p + \beta_{vp} v p = 20 + 5v + 10p + 2vp.$$

    Notice that this model is still linear in the parameters $(\beta_0, \beta_v, \beta_p, \beta_{vp})$—ordinary least squares fits it once we supply the product column $x_{vp} = v \cdot p$—even though $\hat{y}$ is no longer additive across $v$ and $p$. Substituting $p = 0$ and $p = 1$ collapses the single equation into two plan-specific lines:

    Basic ($p = 0$): $\hat{y} = 20 + 5v$ (slope $= 5$ USD/visit).
    Premium ($p = 1$): $\hat{y} = (20 + 10) + (5 + 2)v = 30 + 7v$ (slope $= 7$ USD/visit).

    In plain English: one extra visit adds 5 USD to a Basic prediction and 7 USD to a Premium prediction.

    Read the interaction symmetrically in either direction

    Differentiating (or taking unit finite differences of) $\hat{y}(v, p)$ shows why an interaction is always two-sided. Let $\Delta_v \hat{y}(v, p) = \hat{y}(v+1, p) - \hat{y}(v, p)$ denote the effect of one additional visit at plan $p$, and let $\Delta_p \hat{y}(v) = \hat{y}(v, 1) - \hat{y}(v, 0)$ denote the effect of switching from Basic to Premium at $v$ visits:

    $$\begin{aligned}\Delta_v \hat{y}(v, p) &= \beta_v + \beta_{vp}p = 5 + 2p,\\\Delta_p \hat{y}(v) &= \beta_p + \beta_{vp}v = 10 + 2v.\end{aligned}$$

    The mixed second difference $\Delta_p \Delta_v \hat{y} = \beta_{vp} = 2$ USD per visit is identical in both directions: the visit slope increases by $2$ on Premium, and the Premium gap widens by $2$ with every extra visit.

    Rebuild Maya’s prediction—and see how Shapley splits the interaction

    Maya has $v = 4$ visits and Premium membership ($p = 1$). Evaluating the four algebraic terms directly gives:

    Algebraic term-by-term decomposition of Maya’s prediction ($v=4, p=1$).
    TermFormulaCalculationAmount (USD)
    Intercept$\beta_0$$20$$20$
    Visits main effect$\beta_v v$$5 \times 4$$20$
    Premium main effect$\beta_p p$$10 \times 1$$10$
    Joint interaction$\beta_{vp} v p$$2 \times 4 \times 1$$8$
    Total $\hat{y}$—$20 + 20 + 10 + 8$$58$

    Keeping the $8$ USD interaction on its own row is the most transparent decomposition of the formula. What happens, though, when a feature-level attribution method like SHAP must assign one credit $\phi_v$ to visits and one credit $\phi_p$ to plan relative to a reference customer $(v_0, p_0)$? For any bilinear term $\beta_{vp} v p$, the two feature orderings $(v \text{ first}, p \text{ second})$ and $(p \text{ first}, v \text{ second})$ split the pure synergy $\beta_{vp}(v - v_0)(p - p_0)$ exactly 50/50:

    $$\begin{aligned}\phi_v &= \beta_v(v - v_0) + \beta_{vp}(v - v_0)p_0 + \tfrac{1}{2}\beta_{vp}(v - v_0)(p - p_0),\\\phi_p &= \beta_p(p - p_0) + \beta_{vp}v_0(p - p_0) + \tfrac{1}{2}\beta_{vp}(v - v_0)(p - p_0).\end{aligned}$$

    Reference customer $(v_0, p_0)$Baseline $\phi_0 = \hat{y}(v_0, p_0)$Visits SHAP $\phi_v$Plan SHAP $\phi_p$Check $\phi_0 + \phi_v + \phi_p$
    Zero-visit Basic $(0, 0)$$20$ USD$20 + \tfrac{1}{2}(8) = +24$ USD$10 + \tfrac{1}{2}(8) = +14$ USD$20 + 24 + 14 = 58$ USD

    Against $(0, 0)$, the $+8$ USD joint interaction divides evenly into $+4$ USD for visits ($\phi_v = 24$) and $+4$ USD for plan ($\phi_p = 14$). Against $(2, 0)$, the baseline already includes 2 visits ($\phi_0 = 30$) and the plan gap at 2 visits is already $14$ USD, leaving only $2(4-2)(1-0) = 4$ USD of incremental synergy to split $+2 / +2$.

    Read main-effect coefficients at their reference values (centering)

    In $\hat{y} = 20 + 5v + 10p + 2vp$, $\beta_v = 5$ is the visit slope specifically where $p = 0$, and $\beta_p = 10$ is the Premium lift specifically where $v = 0$. Once a product term enters a regression, a main-effect coefficient is no longer a global average effect—it is a conditional slope evaluated where the interacting partner equals zero.

    If $v = 0$ visits is rare or uninteresting, center visits around the sample mean $\bar{v} = 2$ by defining $u = v - 2$ (so $v = u + 2$) and substituting directly:

    $$\begin{aligned}\hat{y} &= 20 + 5(u + 2) + 10p + 2(u + 2)p\\&= 30 + 5u + 14p + 2up.\end{aligned}$$

    Now the intercept $30$ is Basic spend at $2$ visits ($u = 0$), and the Premium coefficient $14$ is the plan difference at $2$ visits! Maya has $u = 4 - 2 = 2$, so her prediction remains $30 + 5(2) + 14(1) + 2(2)(1) = 58$ USD. Centering re-anchors main-effect coefficients at a meaningful reference point without changing a single prediction or the interaction coefficient $\beta_{vp} = 2$.

    Explore both plans interactively

    Slide monthly visits under Basic, repeat under Premium, and toggle the plan checkbox at fixed visit counts to watch both conditional differences update simultaneously.

    Recompute the product column whenever inputs change

    def predict_interaction(visits, premium):
    return 20 + 5 * visits + 10 * premium + 2 * visits * premium

for visits in [1, 4]:
    basic = predict_interaction(visits, 0)
    premium = predict_interaction(visits, 1)
    print(visits, basic, premium, premium - basic)
# 1 25 37 12
# 4 40 58 18

    In production feature pipelines (and perturbation explainers), always derive visits * premium inside the model pipeline from raw $(v, p)$ rather than passing a precomputed column that could remain frozen when $p$ is toggled.

    Write the explanation around an explicit counterfactual comparison

    For Maya, the clearest stakeholder sentence avoids jargon altogether: “At four monthly visits, the model predicts 58 USD on Premium versus 40 USD on Basic—an 18 USD difference.” Next, we step back from equations to ask how raw measurements should be engineered so every feature in the model has a clear human meaning.
