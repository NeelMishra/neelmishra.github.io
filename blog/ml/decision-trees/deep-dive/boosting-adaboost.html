    Boosting & AdaBoost

    September 2026 · 7 min read

    A single split may miss useful structure. AdaBoost keeps that small learner, then trains another with more attention on the rows the previous learner missed. The final classifier adds their weighted votes.

    What makes a stump useful?

    A decision stump is a tree with one split. It asks one feature question and predicts a class on each side. In binary AdaBoost, a useful learner must have error below 50% under the current example weights. Being better than chance on the unweighted data is not enough.

    Follow one round before reading the formula

    Imagine five training rows, initially carrying weight 0.2 each. A stump misses one row, so its weighted error is 0.2. AdaBoost gives it a vote of about 0.693, increases the missed row’s weight, and decreases the others:

    RowsOld weight eachNew normalized weight each
    The one missed row0.20.5
    Each of the four correct rows0.20.125

    One missed row now accounts for half the training objective. Nothing was relabeled; the next stump is judged with different priorities.

    Here is the rule that produces those numbers. At round $t$, weights $w_i$ sum to 1, the stump is $h_t$, and both its predictions and labels $y_i$ are −1 or +1. Its weighted error $\varepsilon_t$ is the sum of weights on rows it misses. The vote is $\alpha_t$:

    $$\alpha_t=\frac12\ln\frac{1-\varepsilon_t}{\varepsilon_t},\qquad w_i\leftarrow w_i\exp\!\left(-\alpha_t y_i h_t(x_i)\right).$$

    Renormalize the updated weights to sum to 1. For error 0.2, the incorrect row is multiplied by 2 and each correct row by 0.5. Their unnormalized weights total 0.8, giving the table above.

    Repeat the procedure. To predict a new row, add each stump’s vote times its ±1 prediction. Choose the sign of that total, with a fixed rule for an exact tie.

    The updated weights become the entering weights of the next round. For example, round 1 starts with 1/16 per row and misses four rows: ε = 4/16 = 0.25. After normalization, each missed row has weight 1/8, while each of the other twelve has weight 1/24. The two groups now carry half the total weight each. The labels and coordinates have not changed.

    A stump’s error is not the ensemble’s error

    At round 3, the stump misses rows 5, 6, 13, and 16. Each enters with weight 0.025, so its weighted error is 0.10, even though it misses 4 of 16 rows: an unweighted error of 25%. Meanwhile, the combined classifier has zero training errors. These quantities answer different questions.

    Follow row 5, at (2, 6), whose true class is B / −1. The third stump predicts A because x₂ = 6 exceeds 4. Its weight therefore rises from 0.025 to 0.125. But the first two stumps both predicted B for this row, and their votes still win:

    $$F_3(x_5)=-0.549-0.805+1.099\approx-0.255.$$

    The combined score is negative, so the ensemble correctly predicts B. Reweighting responds to the new stump’s mistake even though the accumulated classifier gets this row right. The update can continue improving margins after training accuracy reaches 100%; validation performance determines whether more rounds help on new data.

    Why those particular weights?

    AdaBoost minimizes exponential loss: a confidently wrong combined vote is penalized much more than a weakly wrong one. The example weights are proportional to the current per-row losses. Adding a stump multiplies those losses by exactly the update factor above.

    The AdaBoost derivation shows how minimizing the loss over the new vote produces the log-ratio formula. It also develops a second round and the training-error bound, so we do not repeat that proof here.

    Handle boundary cases explicitly: a perfect stump requires a stopping rule instead of an infinite vote; an error of 0.5 gives a zero vote. Repeatedly missed noisy labels can receive excessive weight. Choose learner capacity and the number of rounds using validation data.

    Continue from weighted mistakes to loss gradients

    AdaBoost’s loss-based view connects to gradient boosting, which fits each new learner to a correction derived from the chosen loss. For squared error, that correction is the residual: target minus current prediction.
