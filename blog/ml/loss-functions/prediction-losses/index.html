    Prediction Losses and Their Best Constants

    September 2026 · 5 min read + loss explorer

    Before a model learns anything about the input features, what should it predict for every row? The answer depends on the loss: the same observations can favor a mean, a median, a probability, or a balance of clipped residuals.

    What “best constant” means

    Take $n>0$ training observations and make the raw score the same number $c$ for all of them. We choose that number by minimizing the total training loss:

    $$J(c)=\sum_{i=1}^n\ell(y_i,c),\qquad c^*\in\arg\min_c J(c).$$

    This is a one-parameter optimization problem. There are no feature weights or splits yet. In gradient boosting, $F_0(x)=c^*$ is the starting score; later trees add corrections that depend on $x$. The reported prediction may be $c$ itself, a probability $\sigma(c)$, or a mean count $e^c$.

    Every derivation below uses equally weighted training rows and no penalty on the constant. Dividing $J$ by $n$ changes its scale, but not its minimizers.

    The results, with the derivation one click away

    LossReported constant predictionBest raw score $c^*$
    Half squared errorMean target$\bar y$
    Absolute errorAny empirical medianA median of the targets
    Binary log lossPositive-label fraction $\bar y$$\log\!\frac{\bar y}{1-\bar y}$ when $0<\bar y<1$
    Poisson loss, equal exposureMean count $\bar y$$\log\bar y$ when $\bar y>0$
    Huber loss, fixed $\delta$A balance of capped errorsA root of $\sum_i\operatorname{clip}(c-y_i,-\delta,\delta)=0$

    Binary data containing only one class and Poisson data containing only zeros have boundary optima: the best reported prediction exists, but no finite log-odds or log-mean score represents it. The individual pages explain those cases.

    Move the constant and inspect the actual objective

    Choose a loss, then move $c$. The curve is the sum of that loss over the displayed targets. Its minimum is marked separately from your chosen score. Absolute error has a whole interval of equally good answers in this example; that flat segment is part of the result.

    Read the five derivations

    1. Squared error: summing residuals gives the mean.
    2. Absolute error: counting observations on each side gives a median.
    3. Binary log loss: match the class fraction, then convert probability to log odds.
    4. Poisson loss: match the mean count, then convert it to a log mean.
    5. Huber loss: balance residuals after clipping their influence.

    Starting a model versus fitting its next tree

    The starting constant uses the original targets. A later tree fits corrections to the current model. The StatQuest regression example shows how feature thresholds group rows whose pseudo-residuals can share a correction.

    The other branches of the loss-functions series study entropy, distribution divergences, transport, and triplet objectives. Those do not all optimize a scalar prediction against an observed target, so the constant-score formulas here should not be applied to them automatically.
