    2. Regression: The Derivation

    September 2026 · Lecture 2 / 4 · 10 min read

    Notes on StatQuest with Josh Starmer · Gradient Boost Part 2 · 26:46 video. Explanations and redrawn figures follow the lecture’s worked example.

    Lecture 1 showed what boosting does. This lecture derives the same steps from squared-error loss: start at a mean, calculate residuals, fit a tree, average within its leaves, and update predictions.

    1. Three rows and one loss

    Video 0:56 ↗
    The smaller training set used in Lecture 2.
    RowHeight (m)ColorGenderWeight $y_i$
    11.6BlueMale88
    21.6GreenFemale76
    31.5BlueFemale56

    $x_i$ means the input features of row $i$; $y_i$ is its measured weight. $F(x_i)$ is the current prediction. The loss of one row is

    $$L(y_i,F(x_i))=\frac12\bigl(y_i-F(x_i)\bigr)^2.$$

    The factor $1/2$ makes derivatives simpler. It halves every squared-error total, so it does not change which prediction has the smallest loss.

    2. Why the first prediction is the mean

    Video 6:30 ↗

    Before building a tree, choose one constant $c$ for every row. The notation $\arg\min_c$ means “find the value of $c$ that makes this total smallest.”

    $$F_0=\arg\min_c\sum_{i=1}^{n}\frac12(y_i-c)^2.$$

    Differentiate with respect to $c$, and set the result to zero:

    $$\sum_i(c-y_i)=0\quad\Longrightarrow\quad nc=\sum_i y_i.$$
    $$F_0=\frac{88+76+56}{3}=\frac{220}{3}\approx73.33.$$

    At the mean, the signed residuals sum to zero. That does not mean every prediction is correct. It means a single change applied to everybody cannot improve squared error: the upward and downward requests balance. A tree can improve the fit by giving different feature regions different corrections.

    For these rows, the model starts too low for weights 88 and 76, and too high for weight 56. Keeping one constant cannot solve both problems. Separating the 56 kg row from the other two can.

    3. Why the negative gradient becomes a residual

    Video 10:01 ↗

    At round $m$, $F_{m-1}$ is the model containing all trees built so far. Differentiate the loss with respect to its prediction, not an input feature:

    $$\frac{\partial L(y_i,F)}{\partial F}=F-y_i.$$

    A positive derivative means increasing the prediction increases loss. Reverse its sign to get the direction we want the next tree to learn:

    $$r_{im}=-\left.\frac{\partial L(y_i,F)}{\partial F}\right|_{F=F_{m-1}(x_i)}=y_i-F_{m-1}(x_i).$$

    For this loss, the pseudo-residual is exactly the ordinary residual. “Pseudo-residual” is the general name because other losses produce different negative gradients.

    Why differentiate with respect to the prediction?

    The target weight is fixed; our controllable quantity is the model’s prediction. For row 1, that prediction is about 73.33. Its loss derivative is $73.33-88\approx-14.67$. A small positive change in the prediction lowers the loss, so the desired correction is positive.

    For row 3, the derivative is $73.33-56\approx17.33$. Raising the prediction would make its error worse; reversing the sign asks the next tree for a negative correction.

    There is one desired direction per training row. A tree turns those separate directions into a function of the features, so we can also obtain a correction for an input we have never seen. This is the connection between a gradient calculation on the training rows and a usable prediction rule.

    4. Fit the tree and name its regions

    Video 12:47 ↗

    Train a regression tree with the original features as inputs and these residuals as targets. The lecture’s stump uses height < 1.55 m. Row 3 goes left; rows 1 and 2 go right.

    $R_{jm}$ means leaf region $j$ in tree $m$. $\gamma_{jm}$ is the single correction that leaf will output. For the first tree there are two regions: $R_{1,1}$ and $R_{2,1}$.

    How does a candidate feature rule earn the split?

    For the height rule, the left child has residual $-52/3$ by itself. The right child has residuals $44/3$ and $8/3$, whose mean is $26/3$. The right residuals lie 6 above and 6 below that mean, so their squared deviations add to $6^2+(-6)^2=72$. The singleton left child adds zero.

    This gives a direct way to compare feature rules: temporarily fit each child’s mean residual and measure the squared error left around those means. For this same three-row dataset:

    Comparing three available feature partitions; costs use the sum of squared deviations, without the factor ½.

    Gender = male{1} and {2, 3}200

    The height rule separates the strong downward request from the two upward requests. It leaves less disagreement inside its children than the other rules. Once selected, the rule defines the regions; residual values themselves are never used to route a new person.

    5. Why each leaf outputs the average residual

    Video 14:50 ↗

    Keep the previous predictions fixed. For one region $R$, choose a shared addition $\gamma$ that minimizes the loss of its rows:

    $$\gamma=\arg\min_a\sum_{i:x_i\in R}\frac12\bigl(y_i-F_{m-1}(x_i)-a\bigr)^2.$$

    The expression $y_i-F_{m-1}(x_i)$ is the residual $r_i$. So we are fitting one constant to the residuals inside this leaf:

    $$\frac{d}{da}\sum_{i:x_i\in R}\frac12(r_i-a)^2=\sum_{i:x_i\in R}(a-r_i)=0.$$
    $$\gamma=\frac{\sum_{i:x_i\in R}r_i}{\text{number of rows in }R}.$$

    The left leaf has just one residual, so it outputs $-17.33\ldots$. The right leaf outputs $(14.666\ldots+2.666\ldots)/2=8.666\ldots$. Averaging is therefore the squared-loss solution, not an extra arbitrary rule.

    Why minimizing the residual fit also improves the original task

    After adding a leaf correction $a$, a row’s new error is $y_i-(F_{m-1}(x_i)+a)$. That is exactly $r_i-a$. Therefore the squared error from fitting the residual with $a$ is the original prediction’s squared error after adding $a$.

    This identity is why residual prediction is useful, rather than a separate task with an unrelated score. A good fit to the remaining errors supplies a good additive correction to the original model.

    For squared loss, fitting each region’s mean residual and choosing its best final correction lead to the same number. In classification they need not: the residual helps choose a group, then the original classification loss determines the score correction. That is the next pair of lectures.

    6. Update predictions and repeat

    Video 20:38 ↗

    With learning rate $\nu=0.1$, add one tenth of the leaf output to each row’s previous prediction:

    First-round predictions, calculated before rounding.
    RowsPrevious predictionLeaf correctionUpdated prediction
    1 and 273.33…+8.666…74.2
    373.33…−17.33…71.6

    The next residuals are $88-74.2=13.8$, $76-74.2=1.8$, and $56-71.6=-15.6$. Fit the next tree to these new targets.

    $$F_m(x)=F_{m-1}(x)+\nu\sum_{j=1}^{J_m}\gamma_{jm}\mathbf1\{x\in R_{jm}\}.$$

    $J_m$ is the number of leaves. The indicator is 1 only for the leaf reached by $x$, so exactly one leaf contributes per tree. The sum is a way to write the tree’s prediction; a row does not enter multiple leaves.

    A second round on the same regions

    To see the update clearly, keep the same height stump for one more round. Its new residuals are $[13.8,1.8,-15.6]$. The right leaf’s new mean is $(13.8+1.8)/2=7.8$; the left outputs −15.6.

    The tree contributes a tenth of these outputs. This is a worked continuation with a fixed split; a normal training run searches for the best next tree and may choose different regions.

    If we kept this partition forever, rows 1 and 2 would always receive the same prediction. It would approach their mean, 82 kg, leaving errors +6 and −6. Another feature split is needed to distinguish them. More rounds cannot create that distinction if every tree is forced to treat them identically.

    7. Use the fitted sum for new data

    Video 24:59 ↗

    After $M$ rounds, keep $F_M$. For a new input, follow the feature rules in every tree and add its scaled leaf value to the initial mean. Training used residuals to learn these rules; prediction only needs the features.

    The lecture’s description corrects the leaf indexing and explains the one-leaf indicator. Those corrections are incorporated here.

    The leaf indicator in the update formula is bookkeeping for routing. For someone below 1.55 m in the first tree, the left indicator is 1 and the right indicator is 0. The sum returns the left correction once; it does not average over the leaves.

    The subscript $m-1$ is equally important: it refers to the entire previous ensemble, not just the last tree. Calculating $y_i-h_{m-1}(x_i)$ would throw away the starting prediction and all earlier contributions, producing the wrong targets for the next round.
