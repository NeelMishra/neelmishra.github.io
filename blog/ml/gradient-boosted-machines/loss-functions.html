    Loss Functions and Pseudo-Residuals

    September 2026 · 6 min read

    The loss decides what counts as an error. A tree then groups rows that can share a useful correction. Keep the target, the model’s score, and the quantity reported to a user separate.

    Ask how one prediction should move

    For row $i$, let $y_i$ be its observed target, $F_i$ its current score, and $\ell(y_i,F_i)$ its loss. The correction target $r_i$, often called a pseudo-residual, is the negative derivative with respect to that score:

    $$r_i=-\frac{\partial\ell(y_i,F_i)}{\partial F_i}.$$

    This says which small score change would reduce loss. A fitted tree approximates those targets with shared leaf values. See why gradients supply correction targets for the derivation.

    Choose the prediction scale first

    In this table, $y$ is the observed target and $F$ is the score updated by trees. For binary labels, $p$ is the positive-class probability. For counts, $\mu$ is the expected count over the stated observation interval. For Huber loss, $e=y-F$ is the residual and $\delta>0$ is the threshold.

    Start at the loss’s best constant

    Before the first tree, give every training row the same raw score $c$. Choose $c^*$ to minimize $J(c)=\sum_i\ell(y_i,c)$, then start the ensemble at $F_0(x)=c^*$. The reported prediction may be a transformation of that score.

    Loss — open its derivationReported predictionInitial raw score $c^*$
    Half squared error$\bar y$$\bar y$
    Absolute errorA median targetA median target
    Binary log loss$\bar y$$\log[\bar y/(1-\bar y)]$
    Poisson, equal exposure$\bar y$$\log\bar y$
    Huber, fixed thresholdA clipped-residual balance$\sum_i\operatorname{clip}(c^*-y_i,-\delta,\delta)=0$

    The finite binary score requires both classes; the finite Poisson score requires a positive mean count. The linked pages derive each result and explain the boundary cases. Start with the best-constant overview and loss explorer to compare them.

    The fitted target need not be the final leaf value

    The tree chooses the regions; pseudo-residuals are the targets used to score its candidate splits. For a first-order GBM, test feature rules such as $x<2.5$. Within each candidate child, fit a constant to that child’s pseudo-residuals. The best constant for this squared-error tree fit is their mean.

    Score the split by adding the squared deviations from the two child means:

    $$\mathrm{split\ cost}=\sum_{i\in R_L}(r_i-\bar r_L)^2+\sum_{i\in R_R}(r_i-\bar r_R)^2.$$

    Here $r_i$ is row $i$’s pseudo-residual; $\bar r_L$ and $\bar r_R$ are the means in the candidate children. A low cost means each child’s rows ask for similar corrections, so one leaf value can approximate them well.

    Example. Suppose the feature values are $x=[1,2,3,4]$ and the current pseudo-residuals are $r=[-3,-2,2,3]$. Compare all three possible thresholds:

    Feature splitLeft residualsRight residualsTotal squared error
    $x<1.5$$[-3]$$[-2,2,3]$14
    $x<2.5$$[-3,-2]$$[2,3]$1
    $x<3.5$$[-3,-2,2]$$[3]$14

    The middle split wins. Its means are $(-3-2)/2=-2.5$ and $(2+3)/2=2.5$. Each of the four residuals is only 0.5 from its child mean, so the cost is $4(0.5)^2=1$. Leaving all rows together would use mean 0 and cost $9+4+4+9=26$.

    The chosen regions are therefore $R_L=\{x:x<2.5\}$ and $R_R=\{x:x\ge2.5\}$. The left leaf asks to lower the score; the right asks to raise it. Further splits repeat this process inside a child. New data follows the feature rules; it does not need a residual to choose a branch.

    After choosing regions, we may refine each leaf’s correction using the original loss. For squared error, the fitted residual mean is already optimal. For other losses, the final value can differ.

    For example, two rows in one leaf have labels 1 and 0 and current probabilities 0.2 and 0.4. Their negative gradients sum to 0.8 − 0.4 = 0.4. Their curvatures sum to 0.2 × 0.8 + 0.4 × 0.6 = 0.4. An unregularized Newton correction is 0.4 / 0.4 = +1 score unit, before the learning rate.

    This is a local approximation, not an exact probability update. Penalties or step limits can moderate it when curvature is small. The leaf-value derivation introduces the general ratio.

    Quantile loss

    Quantile loss gives unequal penalties to underprediction and overprediction to estimate a chosen conditional percentile. Two fitted quantiles can form an interval, but its coverage still needs validation. Continue to how a tree groups these correction targets.
