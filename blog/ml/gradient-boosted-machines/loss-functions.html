    Loss Functions and Pseudo-Residuals

    September 2026 · 6 min read

    The loss decides what counts as an error. A tree then groups rows that can share a useful correction. Keep the target, the model’s score, and the quantity reported to a user separate.

    Ask how one prediction should move

    For row $i$, let $y_i$ be its observed target, $F_i$ its current score, and $\ell(y_i,F_i)$ its loss. The correction target $r_i$, often called a pseudo-residual, is the negative derivative with respect to that score:

    $$r_i=-\frac{\partial\ell(y_i,F_i)}{\partial F_i}.$$

    This says which small score change would reduce loss. A fitted tree approximates those targets with shared leaf values. See why gradients supply correction targets for the derivation.

    Choose the prediction scale first

    In this table, $y$ is the observed target and $F$ is the score updated by trees. For binary labels, $p$ is the positive-class probability. For counts, $\mu$ is the expected count over the stated observation interval. For Huber loss, $e=y-F$ is the residual and $\delta>0$ is the threshold.

    Start at the loss’s best constant

    Before the first tree, give every training row the same raw score $c$. Choose $c^*$ to minimize $J(c)=\sum_i\ell(y_i,c)$, then start the ensemble at $F_0(x)=c^*$. The reported prediction may be a transformation of that score.

    Loss — open its derivationReported predictionInitial raw score $c^*$

    The finite binary score requires both classes; the finite Poisson score requires a positive mean count. The linked pages derive each result and explain the boundary cases. Start with the best-constant overview and loss explorer to compare them.

    Why predict the residual instead of the original target?

    We still want to predict the original target, such as someone’s weight. But we already have a prediction. The next tree’s job is to predict how much to add to it. Start with squared-error regression:

    1. A person in the training data weighs 88 kg. Our model currently predicts 71.2 kg.
    2. The missing amount is $88-71.2=16.8$ kg. This is the residual.
    3. Train the next tree to predict that missing amount from the person’s features. Suppose its output is 16.8.

    Current prediction + predicted residual = updated prediction

    $71.2+16.8=88$ kg.

    That addition is why predicting the residual helps. The tree’s 16.8 is a correction to the existing prediction. If it predicted the full weight, 88, and we added that instead, we would get $71.2+88=159.2$ kg.

    We can calculate residuals for training rows because their actual targets are known. For a new person, the true weight—and therefore the true residual—is unknown. The tree learns a relationship between features and residuals so it can estimate the correction for new inputs too.

    How this connects to regions

    The tree tries feature rules, such as height below a threshold, looking for groups with similar residuals. Each leaf’s feature-defined group is a region. In squared-error regression, the leaf predicts the average training residual in that group.

    The correction can still help without being exact. If our person shares a leaf with someone whose residual is 14.2, that leaf predicts $(16.8+14.2)/2=15.5$. Adding it gives $71.2+15.5=86.7$ kg. The error falls from 16.8 to 1.3 kg.

    Then take a smaller step and repeat

    Boosting usually scales the correction by a learning rate to control how quickly it fits the training data. With rate 0.1 and the original tree output 16.8, the prediction becomes $71.2+0.1\times16.8=72.88$. The next tree learns the remaining residual, $88-72.88=15.12$, using errors from the whole updated ensemble.

    For squared error, pseudo-residuals are exactly these ordinary residuals. For other losses, they are negative gradients: targets that tell the next tree how to move the model’s score to reduce loss.

    The 71.2 → 88 example comes from StatQuest’s regression explanation, 7:17–9:40.

    Quantile loss

    Quantile loss gives unequal penalties to underprediction and overprediction to estimate a chosen conditional percentile. Two fitted quantiles can form an interval, but its coverage still needs validation. Continue to how a tree groups these correction targets.
