    Loss Functions and Pseudo-Residuals

    September 2026 · 7 min read

    The loss decides what counts as an error. A tree then groups rows that can share a useful correction. Keep the target, the model’s score, and the quantity reported to a user separate.

    Ask how one prediction should move

    For row $i$, let $y_i$ be its observed target, $F_i$ its current score, and $\ell(y_i,F_i)$ its loss. The correction target $r_i$, often called a pseudo-residual, is the negative derivative with respect to that score:

    $$r_i=-\frac{\partial\ell(y_i,F_i)}{\partial F_i}.$$

    This says which small score change would reduce loss. A fitted tree approximates those targets with shared leaf values. See why gradients supply correction targets for the derivation.

    Hold the observed target $y$ fixed and differentiate with respect to $F$. A positive derivative says that increasing the score would increase the loss, so the correction points downward; a negative derivative calls for an increase. This is why the target is $r=-\partial\ell/\partial F$. It gives a local direction to fit, not automatically the final step size.

    Choose the prediction scale first

    In this table, $y$ is the observed target and $F$ is the score updated by trees. For binary labels, $p$ is the positive-class probability. For counts, $\mu$ is the expected count over the stated observation interval.

    Half squared error: differentiate the residual

    The loss squares the residual $y-F$. The chain rule contributes a factor of $-1$ because increasing $F$ decreases that residual:

    $$\begin{aligned}\ell(y,F)&=\frac12(y-F)^2,\\g=\frac{\partial\ell}{\partial F}&=\frac12\cdot2(y-F)\cdot(-1)\\&=F-y,\\r=-g&=y-F.\end{aligned}$$

    The gradient’s magnitude grows with the prediction error, so larger residuals exert more influence. Curvature is constant: the quadratic approximation is exact. Because the score already has the target’s scale, adding a tree’s output directly changes the predicted target by that amount, before shrinkage.

    Binary log loss: differentiate through the sigmoid

    For $y\in\{0,1\}$, let $p=\sigma(F)=1/(1+e^{-F})$. The score is $F=\log(p/(1-p))$. Binary log loss is

    $$\begin{aligned}\ell(y,p)&=-y\log p\\&\quad-(1-y)\log(1-p),\\\ell(y,F)&=\log(1+e^F)-yF.\end{aligned}$$

    The second form writes the same loss on the scale that the trees actually update. To see how the probability form gives the correction, differentiate through the sigmoid:

    $$\begin{aligned}\frac{\partial\ell}{\partial p}&=-\frac{y}{p}+\frac{1-y}{1-p}\\&=\frac{p-y}{p(1-p)},\\\frac{dp}{dF}&=p(1-p).\end{aligned}$$

    The loss changes through $p$, and $p$ changes through $F$, so the chain rule multiplies these two derivatives. The sigmoid factor cancels the denominator:

    $$\begin{aligned}g=\frac{\partial\ell}{\partial F}&=\frac{\partial\ell}{\partial p}\frac{dp}{dF}\\&=\frac{p-y}{p(1-p)}\,p(1-p)\\&=p-y,\\r=-g&=y-p.\end{aligned}$$

    A positive-label row asks for an increased score unless its probability is already one; a negative-label row asks for a decreased score unless its probability is zero. The pseudo-residuals are real numbers, so the new base learner is a regression tree even though the overall task is classification.

    If the tree supplies correction $f_m(x)$, the update is

    $$\begin{aligned}F_m&=F_{m-1}+\nu f_m,\\p_m&=\sigma(F_m).\end{aligned}$$

    It is not $p_m=p_{m-1}+\nu f_m$. Locally, $\Delta p\approx p(1-p)\Delta F$: the same score change has different probability effects depending on the current probability. This is why score corrections and probability corrections must not be interchanged.

    Poisson loss: differentiate the log-mean score

    For a count target $y\in\{0,1,2,\ldots\}$, model the positive conditional mean as $\mu=e^F$. The Poisson model assigns the observed count probability

    $$P(Y=y\mid\mu)=\frac{e^{-\mu}\mu^y}{y!}.$$

    Taking its negative logarithm gives the loss. Substitute $\mu=e^F$ and $\log\mu=F$:

    $$\begin{aligned}-\log P(Y=y\mid\mu)&=\mu-y\log\mu+\log(y!)\\&=e^F-yF+\log(y!).\end{aligned}$$

    The observed $y$ is fixed, so $\log(y!)$ has derivative zero and can be dropped during optimization. Differentiate the remaining terms and negate:

    $$\begin{aligned}\ell(y,F)&=e^F-yF,\\g=\frac{\partial\ell}{\partial F}&=e^F-y=\mu-y,\\r=-g&=y-\mu.\end{aligned}$$

    The correction target resembles a residual on the mean-count scale, but the tree’s final value is added to the log-mean score. The corresponding prediction update is multiplicative:

    $$\mu_m(x)=\mu_{m-1}(x)\exp\!\bigl(\nu f_m(x)\bigr).$$

    When observations have known exposure $e_i>0$, a rate model uses $\mu_i=e_i\exp(F_i)$. The fixed term $\log e_i$ acts as an offset. The derivatives remain $g_i=\mu_i-y_i$ and $h_i=\mu_i$; the distinction is whether $\exp(F_i)$ denotes a rate or an expected count.

    Start at the loss’s best constant

    Squared error starts at the mean target; absolute error starts at the median. Binary log loss starts at the score whose sigmoid equals the training positive rate. For a positive rate of 0.2, that score is $\ln(0.2/0.8)\approx-1.386$.

    Poisson loss starts at the log of the mean count for equal exposure. Pure-class binary data and all-zero counts require explicit handling because the unconstrained optimal score is infinite in magnitude.

    The fitted target need not be the final leaf value

    Fitting pseudo-residuals chooses regions that can share a correction. One can then refine each region’s constant using the original loss. For squared error, it is the mean residual. Logistic loss often uses a curvature-based approximation instead.

    For example, two rows in one leaf have labels 1 and 0 and current probabilities 0.2 and 0.4. Their negative gradients sum to 0.8 − 0.4 = 0.4. Their curvatures sum to 0.2 × 0.8 + 0.4 × 0.6 = 0.4. An unregularized Newton correction is 0.4 / 0.4 = +1 score unit, before the learning rate.

    This is a local approximation, not an exact probability update. Penalties or step limits can moderate it when curvature is small. The leaf-value derivation introduces the general ratio.

    Huber loss: derive the clipped residual

    Let $e=y-F$ and choose a threshold $\delta>0$. Huber loss transitions from quadratic to linear growth:

    $$\ell_\delta(e)=\begin{cases}\frac12e^2,&|e|\le\delta,\\\delta|e|-\frac12\delta^2,&|e|>\delta.\end{cases}$$

    First differentiate with respect to the residual $e$. In the quadratic region, the derivative of $\tfrac12e^2$ is $e$. Outside it, the derivative of $\delta|e|$ is $+\delta$ for positive $e$ and $-\delta$ for negative $e$; the constant term contributes zero.

    We need a derivative with respect to the score, however. Since $de/dF=-1$, the chain rule and the negative-gradient rule supply two minus signs that cancel:

    $$\begin{aligned}r=-\frac{\partial\ell_\delta}{\partial F}&=-\frac{d\ell_\delta}{de}\frac{de}{dF}\\&=-\frac{d\ell_\delta}{de}(-1)\\&=\frac{d\ell_\delta}{de}.\end{aligned}$$

    Therefore the correction target is exactly the residual clipped to the interval $[-\delta,\delta]$:

    $$r=\begin{cases}-\delta,&e<-\delta,\\e,&-\delta\le e\le\delta,\\\delta,&e>\delta.\end{cases}$$

    The slopes agree at $e=\pm\delta$, so the target is continuous at both boundaries. Small errors keep their ordinary residual; large positive or negative errors request corrections of $+\delta$ or $-\delta$. This limits one row’s gradient contribution, but is not a guarantee of robustness to every kind of data contamination.

    Quantile loss gives unequal penalties to underprediction and overprediction to estimate a chosen conditional percentile. Two fitted quantiles can form an interval, but its coverage still needs validation. Continue to how a tree groups these correction targets.
