    PCA and LDA: Choose What a Projection Preserves

    October 2026 · 8 min read

    Expanded from PCA and LDA.pdf · View a handwritten page

    PCA and LDA both replace several measurements with fewer coordinates. They choose those coordinates for different reasons. PCA looks for directions with large overall variation. LDA uses class labels to find directions that separate classes relative to their internal spread. We will calculate both, including the ten-point LDA example from the handwritten page.

    PCA: start with two measurements per row

    A small PCA teaching dataset. Both measurements use comparable units.
    PointMeasurement 1Measurement 2Centered pair
    A11(−1.5, −1.5)
    B23(−0.5, 0.5)
    C32(0.5, −0.5)
    D44(1.5, 1.5)

    Both column means are 2.5. Subtract them to center the data. PCA will describe variation around this mean, so keep the means for projecting or reconstructing later observations.

    Calculate the covariance matrix

    Use sample covariance, dividing by $n-1=3$. For the first coordinate, the squared centered values sum to $2.25+0.25+0.25+2.25=5$. The second coordinate has the same sum. The cross-products sum to $2.25-0.25-0.25+2.25=4$.

    $$S=\frac{X_c^\top X_c}{n-1} =\begin{bmatrix}5/3&4/3\\4/3&5/3\end{bmatrix}.$$

    $X_c$ holds the centered rows. The diagonal entries are the individual variances; the off-diagonal entries measure how the coordinates move together. Using $1/n$ would rescale all eigenvalues equally and leave the principal directions unchanged.

    Choose a direction with large projected variance

    A unit vector $v$ specifies a direction. The projection score of one centered point is $z=v^\top(x-\mu)$. Its variance over the data is $v^\top Sv$. We require $v^\top v=1$ because otherwise multiplying a direction by 100 would multiply the score variance by 10,000 without finding a better direction.

    $$\max_v v^\top Sv \quad\text{subject to }v^\top v=1.$$

    A Lagrange multiplier gives $Sv=\lambda v$. This is the eigenvector equation: choose the unit eigenvector whose eigenvalue is largest. In our matrix:

    $$v_1=\frac1{\sqrt2}\begin{bmatrix}1\\1\end{bmatrix}, \quad\lambda_1=3.$$
    $$v_2=\frac1{\sqrt2}\begin{bmatrix}1\\-1\end{bmatrix}, \quad\lambda_2=\frac13.$$

    The first direction follows the diagonal where both measurements increase together. The second follows their difference. The total variance is $3+1/3=10/3$, so the first component explains $3/(10/3)=\mathbf{90\%}$ of the sample variance.

    Project, then reconstruct

    Using only the first component, add the two centered coordinates and divide by $\sqrt2$. For A, the score is $(-1.5-1.5)/\sqrt2\approx-2.1213$.

    Reconstruction places each point back on the retained diagonal.
    PointFirst scoreReconstructed pair
    A−2.1213(1, 1)
    B0(2.5, 2.5)
    C0(2.5, 2.5)
    D2.1213(4, 4)

    Reconstruction uses $\hat x=\mu+zv_1$. B and C become the same point because their only difference lies along the discarded direction. Each has squared reconstruction error $0.5^2+0.5^2=0.5$, so the total is 1. That equals $(n-1)\lambda_2=3\times(1/3)$.

    With several retained components, put their direction vectors into the columns of $V$. Scores are $Z=X_cV$ and reconstructions are $ZV^\top+\mu$. Flipping the sign of a direction also flips its scores and leaves the reconstruction unchanged.

    import numpy as np

X = np.array([[1., 1.], [2., 3.], [3., 2.], [4., 4.]])
mean = X.mean(axis=0)
centered = X - mean
values, vectors = np.linalg.eigh(centered.T @ centered / 3)
v = vectors[:, -1]  # eigenvalues are returned in ascending order
scores = centered @ v
reconstructed = mean + np.outer(scores, v)
print(values[-1] / values.sum())  # 0.9
print(np.sum((X - reconstructed)**2))  # 1.0

    Centering and scaling are separate decisions. Centering removes means; scaling changes the relative importance of feature units. Fit those operations and the PCA directions on training data, then reuse them for validation or new inputs. SVD of the centered matrix provides the same principal directions without explicitly constructing the covariance matrix.

    LDA: now use the labels in the handwritten example

    A direction can have large variance without separating the classes we care about. LDA uses labels to compare between-class separation with within-class spread. Here are the ten points listed on page 4 of the original notes:

    Average within each class: $\mu_A=(3,3.6)$ and $\mu_B=(8.4,7.6)$. Their difference is $(5.4,4)$. Projecting along that difference would separate the means, but it would not yet account for how broadly the classes spread in each direction.

    Measure spread around each class mean

    For each point, subtract its own class mean, form the outer product of that difference with itself, and add. For A1, the deviation is $(1,-2.6)$, contributing:

    $$ \begin{bmatrix}1\\-2.6\end{bmatrix} \begin{bmatrix}1&-2.6\end{bmatrix} =\begin{bmatrix}1&-2.6\\-2.6&6.76\end{bmatrix}.$$

    Summing all five contributions in each class gives:

    $$S_A=\begin{bmatrix}4&-2\\-2&13.2\end{bmatrix}, \quad S_B^{\mathrm{class}}=\begin{bmatrix}9.2&-0.2\\-0.2&13.2\end{bmatrix}.$$

    Add them to get the within-class scatter matrix:

    $$S_W=\begin{bmatrix}13.2&-2.2\\-2.2&26.4\end{bmatrix}.$$

    Here “scatter” is an unnormalized sum. Dividing this entire matrix by a common constant rescales the Fisher score without changing its best direction. Dividing each class's contribution by a different count, however, changes class weighting when sizes differ. Keeping sums explicit avoids mixing those conventions.

    Balance separation against spread

    For two classes, Fisher's criterion is the squared gap between projected means divided by their combined projected scatter:

    $$J(w)= \frac{\left[w^\top(\mu_B-\mu_A)\right]^2} {w^\top S_Ww}.$$

    Multiplying $w$ by a constant scales both numerator and denominator equally, so only direction matters. If $S_W$ is invertible, the maximizing direction is proportional to $S_W^{-1}(\mu_B-\mu_A)$. Solve the corresponding linear system:

    $$ \begin{bmatrix}13.2&-2.2\\-2.2&26.4\end{bmatrix}w =\begin{bmatrix}5.4\\4\end{bmatrix}.$$

    The solution is approximately $(0.44046,0.18822)$. Normalizing its length gives $v=(0.91956,0.39295)$. Projecting the class means onto $v$ gives approximately 4.1733 and 10.7107. The rule has adjusted the mean-difference direction for the observed within-class spread.

    import numpy as np

A = np.array([[4,1], [2,4], [2,3], [3,6], [4,4]], dtype=float)
B = np.array([[9,10], [6,8], [9,5], [8,7], [10,8]], dtype=float)
ma, mb = A.mean(0), B.mean(0)
Ac, Bc = A-ma, B-mb
within = Ac.T @ Ac + Bc.T @ Bc
w = np.linalg.solve(within, mb-ma)
v = w / np.linalg.norm(w)
print(within)  # [[13.2, -2.2], [-2.2, 26.4]]
print(v)       # [0.91955932, 0.39295122]

    Generalize the same idea to several classes

    Let $n_c$ be a class's size, $\mu_c$ its mean, and $\mu$ the overall, sample-weighted mean. Define:

    $$S_W=\sum_c\sum_{i\in c} (x_i-\mu_c)(x_i-\mu_c)^\top.$$
    $$S_{\mathrm{between}}=\sum_c n_c (\mu_c-\mu)(\mu_c-\mu)^\top.$$

    The class counts weight the between-class scatter. Solve the generalized eigenvector problem $S_{\mathrm{between}}v=\lambda S_Wv$ and keep directions with the largest eigenvalues. With $C$ classes and $d$ inputs, at most $\min(d,C-1)$ directions can separate class means. In our two-class example, that leaves one direction.

    Distinguish a projection from a classifier

    Fisher's projection criterion can be computed without assuming Gaussian data. The probabilistic LDA classifier adds a model: each class has a multivariate Gaussian distribution and all classes share a covariance matrix. Its discriminant score is:

    $$\delta_c(x)= x^\top\Sigma^{-1}\mu_c -\tfrac12\mu_c^\top\Sigma^{-1}\mu_c +\log\pi_c.$$

    $\pi_c$ is the class prior. The quadratic term in $x$ cancels between classes because the covariance is shared, leaving linear decision boundaries. With equal priors and the shared-covariance model, our two-class projection can use the midpoint of the projected means: about 7.4420. Different priors move the decision threshold.

    The Gaussian assumption concerns the joint distribution within each class. Bell-shaped histograms of individual features alone do not establish it. With very few observations relative to the number of features, $S_W$ can be singular; covariance shrinkage or regularization can stabilize the calculation.

    Know what information each method can lose

    PCA can discard a low-variance direction that contains useful class information. Fisher LDA can miss classes with the same mean but different spreads, or classes arranged in curved patterns. High explained variance and strong class separation answer different questions.

    Choose the projection according to what you need to preserve.
    QuestionPCAFisher LDA
    Uses class labels?NoYes
    Prefers directions with…Large overall varianceLarge class-mean gaps relative to within-class spread
    Maximum useful dimensionsRank of the centered dataAt most min(d, C − 1)

    For new observations, reuse the fitted preprocessing and directions. If a later classifier uses these projections, fit the whole sequence inside the training portion of each evaluation split. This keeps the comparison focused on how well the learned representation works on unseen data.
