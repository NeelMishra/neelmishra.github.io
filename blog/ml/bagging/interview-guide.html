    2. 10 minutes: solve the calculations below without opening the answers.

    Explain the mechanism

    Why are deep trees often bagged?

    They can fit flexible boundaries but change sharply under small training changes. Bootstrap samples create alternative fits, and averaging reduces the unshared part of their prediction variance. Shared bias and shared fluctuations remain. A stable base learner offers less room for this benefit. Review instability.

    How is a random forest different from bagging trees?

    It adds a fresh candidate-feature subset at each split. This may stop one dominant feature from controlling every tree and reduce correlation. It may also weaken splits, so feature count is a tuning tradeoff. Sampling features once per tree is a random-subspace construction. Trace the nodes.

    Does adding more trees cure overfitting?

    More trees stabilize the average. They do not automatically fix brittle leaves, leakage, a distribution shift, or shared bias. Diagnose train and validation behavior; compare leaf size and feature count, then increase trees until gains justify cost. Avoid claiming finite validation scores must improve monotonically.

    Why can two independently trained trees have correlated errors?

    Separate training jobs share the original observations, features, labels, and learning rule. Independence of training execution is not statistical independence across repeated datasets. Name what is random before using an independence claim. Conditional versus repeated-data variability.

    Do the arithmetic aloud

    Ten trees each have variance 4 and pairwise correlation 0.25. What is the ensemble variance and limiting floor?

    With the equal-variance/equal-correlation assumptions, $4[0.25+0.75/10]=1.3$. As the number of trees grows, the limit is $4(0.25)=1$. Increasing to 100 gives 1.03, so most of the remaining variability is shared. This is variance at a fixed input, not the total test MSE.

    For four rows and four draws, what is the expected omitted fraction?

    A particular row is missed by each draw with probability 3/4. All four draws miss it with probability $(3/4)^4=81/256\approx0.3164$. The expected number omitted is $4(81/256)=1.265625$. The 36.8% rule is a large-$n$ limit, not the exact answer here.

    Two eligible OOB trees predict 2 and 6 for target 4. What error belongs in OOB MSE?

    The row prediction is (2+6)/2=4, so its squared error is zero. Averaging the separate tree errors would give 4 and evaluate the wrong predictor. First aggregate eligible predictions per row; then apply the loss; then aggregate row losses.

    Three tree probabilities are 0.51, 0.51, and 0.01. What does the forest predict?

    Specify the convention. Hard labels give two positive votes and a positive majority. Probability averaging gives 1.03/3 ≈ 0.3433 and a negative label at threshold 0.5. Never switch aggregation conventions halfway through evaluation.

Keep the training samples as lists for fitting and use membership sets only to find omitted rows.

    Before coding, write these acceptance cases:

    • The worked example produces predictions [2, 6, 2.25, 6, 4], counts [1, 1, 1, 1, 1], and MSE 6.6125.
    • A tree trained on row 1 must never contribute to row 1's OOB sum, even if it predicts perfectly.
    • Two eligible predictions 2 and 6 for target 4 produce zero error.
    • A row with no eligible tree is reported as uncovered, not predicted as zero. If every row is uncovered, the score is unavailable.
    • Reordering trees leaves results unchanged apart from floating-point rounding.

    What should a stump do when every input value is identical?

    There is no valid threshold separating observed values. Return a single constant leaf: the mean target for squared loss, or weighted class proportions for probability prediction. Do not create empty leaves or divide by their zero count. Process tied feature values together when searching valid boundaries.

    Diagnose before tuning

    OOB accuracy is 98%, but performance on new customers is 70%. What do you investigate first?

    Check whether customers have multiple rows. A tree can omit one visit but train on another visit from the same customer. Use entity-based splits for unseen-customer evaluation, check feature availability at prediction time, and inspect learned preprocessing. Then compare a simple baseline and subgroup performance on the corrected split. Match evaluation to deployment.

    A random numeric ID has the highest impurity importance. Should it be a production feature?

    First check whether it leaks chronology or labels. Many candidate thresholds can inflate training impurity importance. Evaluate held-out performance with and without it, and inspect held-out permutation importance with repeated shuffles. A predictive association still does not establish a stable or causal relationship.

    A forest underpredicts prices beyond the largest training examples. Should you add trees?

    Ordinary regression forests average leaf target means, so they do not extrapolate beyond the observed training-target range. More trees preserve that structural limitation. Examine the extrapolation need, features, and an appropriate baseline such as a model with an explicit trend.

    Judge the answer, not just the final number

    For each question, award yourself one point for the correct mechanism, one for a calculation or concrete example, and one for the relevant assumption or limitation. Revisit an answer that gets fewer than all three before treating that topic as ready. This is a self-check rubric, not a guarantee about any particular interview.

    The question that leads to boosting

    You can now build diverse models and average their predictions. But suppose they all underpredict the same region. Adding more of the same kind of opinion can preserve that error. The boosting path asks the next learner to respond to what the current ensemble gets wrong. We will retain the ideas of trees, losses, and held-out evaluation while changing the training dependency.
