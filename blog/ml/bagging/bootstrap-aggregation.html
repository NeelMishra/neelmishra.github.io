    Bagging & Random Forests

    September 2026 · 10 min read

    Bagging averages models trained on resampled data. Its benefit depends on how differently those models behave. We will follow the sampling first, then calculate what averaging can remove.

    Make one bootstrap sample

    From row IDs [1, 2, 3, 4, 5], draw five times with replacement. A possible sample is [1, 1, 3, 5, 5]. Rows 1 and 5 count twice in training; rows 2 and 4 are omitted. Repeat the sampling for each tree.

    Specify how predictions combine

    Regression predictions 2, 4, and 6 average to 4. Classification needs a convention: hard-label majority vote and average class probability can disagree. Probabilities [0.51, 0.51, 0.01] give two positive votes, but their average is only 0.343.

    Many trees help only with the part they do not share

    Hold one input fixed and imagine repeatedly collecting training data and fitting the ensemble. Prediction variance measures the spread across those fits. Suppose each tree’s variance is 9. If two predictions were independent, their average would have variance 4.5. Two identical predictions would still have variance 9.

    To express intermediate cases, let $B$ be the number of trees, $\sigma^2$ their common variance, and $\rho$ their common pairwise correlation. Correlation describes how their prediction fluctuations move together. Write their average as $\bar f$:

    $$\operatorname{Var}(\bar f)=\sigma^2\left(\rho+\frac{1-\rho}{B}\right).$$

    With 100 trees, variance 9, and correlation 0.2, the result is 9 × (0.2 + 0.8/100) = 1.872. More trees approach a floor of 1.8 in this example. The shared component stays.

    Derive the variance formula

    The variance of a sum contains individual variances and pairwise covariances. There are $B$ variance terms and $B(B-1)$ ordered cross terms. Each covariance here is $\rho\sigma^2$. Averaging divides the sum’s variance by $B^2$:

    $$\operatorname{Var}(\bar f)=\frac{B\sigma^2+B(B-1)\rho\sigma^2}{B^2}.$$

    The equal-variance and equal-correlation assumptions simplify the illustration. Independent training jobs do not imply independent predictions: the jobs still use the same original dataset.

    Randomize the available split features

    A random forest samples candidate features at each node before choosing a split. Hiding a dominant feature sometimes lets another useful feature shape the tree. This can reduce correlation, but it can also weaken a tree’s predictions. Tune the subset size with that tradeoff in mind.

    Sampling features once per tree is a different random-subspace construction. Extra Trees additionally randomize candidate thresholds; bootstrap use depends on configuration. The tree companion illustrates resampling and voting.

    Evaluate each row using trees that omitted it

    A row is out of bag for trees whose bootstrap samples excluded it. Combine only those trees’ predictions for that row, then compute its loss. Average the row losses to get the OOB score. With too few trees, some rows may have no OOB prediction.

    Why are roughly 37% of rows omitted?

    With $n$ original rows, one uniform draw misses a particular row with probability $1-1/n$. A bootstrap makes $n$ independent draws, so the probability all of them miss it is

    $$P(\text{row omitted})=\left(1-\frac1n\right)^n\longrightarrow e^{-1}\approx0.368.$$

    For five rows the probability is 0.8⁵ = 0.32768. The 37% figure is a large-sample expectation, not an exact size for every omitted subset.

    Fit three stumps and build each row’s eligible committee

    Give the five original rows feature values $x=[1,2,3,4,5]$ and targets $y=[1,2,4,4,7]$. We will fit three regression stumps to fixed, possible bootstrap draws. A stump has one split and predicts the training-target mean within each leaf. We count every repeated draw when fitting.

    For each stump, try midpoints between adjacent distinct sampled inputs and minimize the sum of squared residuals. Use the explicit routing rule left if $x<t$, right otherwise; if candidate costs tie, choose the smaller threshold. This makes every prediction reproducible, including a new input that equals a threshold.

    For T1, the right-leaf targets are $[4,7,7]$, whose mean is 6. Dropping the repeated row would give 5.5 and change the fit. For T3, the left-leaf mean is $(1+2+2+4)/4=2.25$. The split at 4.5 has total SSE 4.75, below the alternative split at 3 with SSE $31/6\approx5.1667$.

    Why does T1 choose 2 when a threshold of 4 also fits equally well?

    At threshold 2, the leaves have targets [1,1] and [4,7,7], means 1 and 6, and total SSE 6. At threshold 4, the leaves have [1,1,4] and [7,7], means 2 and 7, and the same SSE 6. Our smaller-threshold rule chooses 2. This equality concerns training cost; the two stumps can predict differently for omitted rows. Row 2 has x=2, so our strict comparison sends it right and predicts 6. Specify both tie handling and routing when reproducing a small tree example.

    The OOB MSE is $(1+16+3.0625+4+9)/5=6.6125$. Here every row has exactly one eligible stump by construction. With more trees, first average all eligible predictions for a row, then square its error. Averaging the separate tree errors instead evaluates a different predictor.

    For example, if two eligible trees predict 2 and 6 for a target of 4, their averaged prediction has squared error 0; their separate squared errors average to 4. More generally, average individual squared error equals squared error of the average plus the variance of those predictions.

    These five observations and three fixed draws are a transparent arithmetic example, not evidence that bagging improves a real task. A row with no eligible tree must remain unscored until more trees provide coverage; silently giving it a prediction of zero changes the evaluation.

    The standard-library reproduction enumerates every stump candidate using exact fractions, records the fit and OOB trace, and regenerates the correlation figure:

    python3 blog/ml/bagging/figures/bagging_oob_example.py

    OOB is useful for appropriately sampled independent rows. It does not enforce time or entity boundaries, undo preprocessing leakage, or remain an untouched test after repeated tuning. Keep the evaluation design aligned with how predictions will be used.

    Match each control to a problem

    ControlEffect to evaluate
    Tree countStability of the average versus training and serving cost
    Candidate feature countDiversity versus the strength of individual splits
    Leaf size and depthFine detail versus sample-specific noise
    Sample fractionMore diverse fits versus less information per tree

    Know what averaging cannot fix

    A shared bias survives averaging. Ordinary regression forests average leaf target means, so they also stay within the training-target range instead of extrapolating a rising trend. Prediction cost grows with the paths visited across all trees; memory grows with stored node count.

    For interpreting predictions, distinguish feature contributions from predictive importance. Neither is automatically a causal effect.
