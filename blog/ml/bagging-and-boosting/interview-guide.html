    Bagging & Boosting: Interview Practice

    September 2026 · 11 min read

    Try answering each question aloud before opening it. A strong answer gives the mechanism first, then an equation or example, and finally one limitation. Use the linked guides when you cannot justify a step.

    Core questions

    1. Explain bagging versus boosting in 30 seconds.

    Bagging trains learners on resampled datasets and aggregates their predictions, often stabilizing high-variance learners. A random forest adds feature subsampling at each split. Boosting builds an additive predictor sequentially: each new learner is chosen using the current model’s errors or loss derivatives. Bagging trees can train independently; boosting rounds depend on earlier rounds.

    2. Does an ensemble always beat a single model?

    No. Correlated errors, shared bias, weak features, noisy labels, leakage, or a poorly tuned objective can erase the benefit. A simpler model may generalize better or meet latency and interpretability requirements more easily.

    3. Why are deep trees often used for forests and small trees for boosting?

    A deep tree can have low training bias but high variance, making it a useful candidate for averaging. Boosting adds many corrections, so smaller trees control each step’s capacity. These are typical choices, not requirements: tune leaf size and depth to the dataset.

    4. How is random forest different from bagging and Extra Trees?

    Bagging changes training samples. A classic random forest also samples candidate features per split. Extra Trees additionally randomize candidate thresholds. Bootstrap use and other details depend on the implementation; explain the sources of randomness instead of assuming the names specify every option.

    5. Why is about 36.8% of the data out of bag?

    A row is omitted from one draw with probability $1-1/n$, hence from $n$ replacement draws with probability $(1-1/n)^n\to e^{-1}$. This is a large-sample expectation per tree, not a permanent 36.8% validation subset.

    6. Can I keep adding forest trees without overfitting?

    With fixed tree-building settings, additional trees mainly reduce averaging variability and the prediction converges. Validation metrics need not improve monotonically. More trees do not fix overly complex individual trees, shared bias, or leakage, and they increase serving cost.

    7. How does boosting differ from stacking?

    Boosting selects the next learner using the current ensemble’s objective. Stacking trains a separate meta-model to combine base-model outputs. Train that meta-model on out-of-fold predictions; using base models’ in-sample predictions can teach it an unrealistically favorable view of their performance.

    Whiteboard follow-ups

    8. Derive the variance of an average of correlated trees.

    Expand the variance of the sum into $B$ variances and $B(B-1)$ ordered covariances, then divide by $B^2$. Equal variance $\sigma^2$ and pairwise correlation $\rho$ give $\sigma^2[\rho+(1-\rho)/B]$. Explain the $\rho\sigma^2$ floor and the assumptions. Full proof and figure →

    9. Why does AdaBoost use a log-ratio vote?

    For a fixed binary classifier, the exponential-loss ratio after a step is $(1-\epsilon)e^{-\alpha}+\epsilon e^\alpha$. Setting its derivative to zero gives $\alpha=\tfrac12\ln[(1-\epsilon)/\epsilon]$. The error is weighted under the current round’s distribution. Derivation →

    10. What happens to an AdaBoost mistake’s weight?

    Before normalization, it multiplies by $e^\alpha$ while a correct row multiplies by $e^{-\alpha}$. Their relative weight ratio therefore multiplies by $e^{2\alpha}=(1-\epsilon)/\epsilon$. For $\epsilon=0.2$, that relative factor is 4; normalization is still necessary to obtain absolute new weights.

    11. Does a weak learner only need good ordinary accuracy?

    No. In binary AdaBoost it must achieve weighted error below $1/2$ under the current weights. A classifier that ignores a few now-heavily-weighted rows may have high ordinary accuracy and still fail this condition. A uniform edge gives the familiar exponential training bound.

    12. Why fit negative gradients instead of residuals?

    Residuals are the negative gradients of squared loss. For binary cross-entropy with logit $F$, the negative gradient is $y-\sigma(F)$. Changing the loss changes the direction that improves it. The tree approximates that direction within its limited function class.

    13. Are classification boosting trees themselves classifiers?

    In the usual gradient-boosting construction, they are regression trees fitted to continuous gradient targets. Their outputs update scores; a sigmoid or softmax turns the final scores into probabilities. Discrete AdaBoost instead uses classifiers whose outputs are weighted votes.

    14. Derive an XGBoost leaf value and split gain.

    Sum gradients and Hessians in a leaf, minimize $Gw+\tfrac12(H+\lambda)w^2$, and obtain $w=-G/(H+\lambda)$. Substitute back, then compare one parent with two children and subtract the extra leaf penalty. Mention positive curvature and the omitted L1/constraint terms. Worked calculation →

    15. Can many stumps model interactions or extrapolate trends?

    A sum of stumps on original features is additive across features, so it cannot represent arbitrary interactions such as XOR. Deeper trees or interaction features can help. A usual random forest regression prediction stays within training-target bounds. Boosted sums can leave those bounds, but finite axis-aligned trees still produce constant predictions beyond their outermost thresholds along a feature; that is not learned linear extrapolation.

    Practical scenarios

    16. Forest training accuracy is 99%, validation is 70%. Add trees?

    First rule out leakage, split mismatch, and misleading metrics. Then inspect depth, leaf size, noisy labels, and feature quality. More trees can stabilize results but will not necessarily close a generalization gap caused by overly specific splits. Tune capacity and feature/sample fractions with suitable cross-validation.

    17. Boosting training loss falls while validation loss rises. What changes?

    Use early stopping and the best validation iteration. Consider a smaller learning rate with sufficient rounds, fewer leaves or less depth, larger minimum leaf support, subsampling, and regularization. Check whether a small validation set is making the curve noisy before reacting to one bad round.

    18. Fraud accuracy is 99.5%. Is the model good?

    Not if predicting every transaction as legitimate already reaches 99.5%. Choose metrics tied to the decision: precision/recall at investigation capacity, PR-AUC, or expected cost. Tune thresholds on validation data. Class weighting or resampling can help training but may alter probability calibration; evaluate on the deployment prevalence.

    19. OOB looks excellent, but next month’s performance collapses. Why?

    Random row omission may share users, near-duplicate events, or future information across training and evaluation. Use chronological and, where appropriate, group-aware splits. Audit feature availability at prediction time and fit all learned preprocessing within training folds. Distribution drift is a separate possibility to measure.

    20. Should I standardize or one-hot encode everything?

    Exact axis-aligned trees usually do not require standardization: a strictly monotonic feature transform preserves training-row order and candidate partitions. Finite precision, binning, and interpolation between observed values can introduce differences. Categorical handling depends on the estimator: arbitrary integer codes impose an ordering unless a native categorical mechanism is enabled. Fit any encoding using training data only.

    21. Can feature importance prove a feature causes the outcome?

    No. Impurity and permutation importances describe a fitted model and dataset. Correlation, cardinality, leakage, and the evaluation distribution influence them. Use held-out permutation checks and inspect correlated groups; causal conclusions require additional assumptions and study design.

    22. Are ensemble probabilities calibrated? How do I assess them?

    Not automatically. Inspect reliability curves and proper scoring rules such as log loss or Brier score on held-out data. If needed, fit a calibration model on separate or cross-validated predictions, then evaluate on an untouched test set. Ranking quality, threshold accuracy, and probability calibration are different properties.

    23. Which library would you choose?

    Use data and constraints to form a hypothesis: categorical-heavy data motivates evaluating CatBoost; large tabular problems motivate comparing efficient histogram implementations. Benchmark against a forest and a simple baseline on identical valid splits. Report quality, training budget, memory, and serving latency; do not claim a universal winner.

    24. What are the time and memory costs?

    A prediction follows one path in each tree: roughly $O(Td)$ with $T$ trees and maximum depth $d$, times any class-specific ensemble factor. Memory follows total node count. Training complexity depends on exact versus histogram splits, feature sampling, sorting reuse, and balance. Forest trees parallelize independently; boosting rounds are sequential but within-round work can parallelize.

    What to implement in a coding round

    A weighted decision stump: for each candidate feature, sort distinct values and try thresholds between adjacent values. Assign each side its weighted-majority label, compute total weighted error, and keep the best valid split. Prefix sums of positive and negative weights let each threshold be scored without rescanning all rows. Include a constant classifier for unsplittable data; use a deterministic tie rule.

    # Binary AdaBoost sketch; weights sum to 1
weights = uniform(n)
for each round:
    stump = fit_weighted_stump(X, y, weights)
    error = sum(weights[i] for misclassified rows i)
    handle error == 0 or error >= 0.5
    alpha = 0.5 * log((1 - error) / error)
    weights *= exp(-alpha * y * stump(X))
    weights /= sum(weights)
    save stump and alpha

    A squared-error boosting stump: initialize predictions to the mean target. Fit each stump to the current residuals using squared-error reduction, store its mean residual per leaf, and add learning-rate-scaled outputs. Recompute residuals before the next round. At inference, include the initial mean and sum the same scaled trees.

    Check observable behavior: duplicate-feature values must not create empty leaves; AdaBoost weights must normalize; a perfect learner must not produce an infinity; the five-row example should match by hand; the four-row regression example should produce MSE 3.25 after one step. Keep training and prediction label conventions consistent.

    A defensible tuning workflow

    1. Define the decision and split. Pick the business metric, a simple baseline, and stratified, group-aware, or chronological evaluation as appropriate.
    2. Build preprocessing inside each training fold. Include feature selection and target encoding. Reserve a final test set.
    3. Tune capacity before spending on huge ensembles. For forests, leaf size/depth and feature fractions matter. For boosting, tune tree complexity and learning rate with early stopping.
    4. Review more than one aggregate score. Check variability across valid splits, important subgroups, calibration, and prediction latency.
    5. Finalize without consulting test outcomes repeatedly. Fix the configuration and training procedure, evaluate once, and report the relevant limitations.

    Five-minute final recall

    Without looking up the notes, derive the bootstrap omission probability, the correlated-average variance, and AdaBoost’s vote. Explain why logistic boosting fits $y-p$ but updates logits. Calculate an XGBoost leaf value. Then explain why a random time-series split or target encoding before cross-validation can invalidate every number you just reported.
