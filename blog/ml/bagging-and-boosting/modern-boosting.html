    XGBoost, LightGBM & CatBoost

    September 2026 · 5 min read

    These are gradient-boosting systems with different training and engineering choices. Understand the common objective first; then explain the particular choices without claiming that one library always wins.

    XGBoost: use slope and curvature

    At the current prediction $F_i$, define the gradient $g_i=\partial\ell(y_i,F_i)/\partial F_i$ and Hessian $h_i=\partial^2\ell(y_i,F_i)/\partial F_i^2$. Here $h_i$ means curvature, not a weak learner. A second-order Taylor expansion approximates the effect of a new tree $f$:

    $$\ell(y_i,F_i+f(x_i))\approx\ell(y_i,F_i)+g_if(x_i)+\frac12h_if(x_i)^2.$$

    The first term is constant while choosing the new tree. Suppose it has $L$ leaves and value $w_j$ in leaf $j$, with a penalty $\gamma L+\tfrac12\lambda\sum_jw_j^2$. Write $G_j=\sum_{i\in j}g_i$ and $H_j=\sum_{i\in j}h_i$. Grouping rows by leaf leaves the objective:

    $$\widetilde{\mathcal L}=\sum_{j=1}^L\left[G_jw_j+\frac12(H_j+\lambda)w_j^2\right]+\gamma L.$$

    Differentiate one leaf’s quadratic: $G_j+(H_j+\lambda)w_j=0$. Therefore:

    $$\boxed{w_j^*=-\frac{G_j}{H_j+\lambda}}.$$

    The numerator says which way to move; the denominator moderates the move using curvature and L2 regularization. This derivation assumes $H_j+\lambda>0$ and omits L1 penalties and additional constraints. The learning rate shrinks the resulting update afterward. See the official objective derivation.

    Derive the split gain

    Substituting $w_j^*$ into the quadratic gives $-\tfrac12G_j^2/(H_j+\lambda)$. Replacing one parent with two children reduces the optimized approximate objective by:

    $$\mathrm{Gain}=\frac12\left[\frac{G_L^2}{H_L+\lambda}+\frac{G_R^2}{H_R+\lambda}-\frac{(G_L+G_R)^2}{H_L+H_R+\lambda}\right]-\gamma.$$

    Two leaves replace one, so the leaf-count penalty increases by exactly $\gamma$. A positive gain makes the split worthwhile under this approximation, subject to the other constraints.

    Worked split: let $(G_L,H_L)=(-6,2)$, $(G_R,H_R)=(6,2)$, $\lambda=1$, and $\gamma=0.5$. The parent has $G=0$, so its optimal value is zero. The children’s values are $+2$ and $-2$. The gain is $\tfrac12(36/3+36/3)-0.5=11.5$. With $\eta=0.1$, the actual score corrections are $+0.2$ and $-0.2$.

    For half-squared loss, $g=F-y$ and $h=1$; with $\lambda=0$, the leaf value is the mean residual. For logistic loss, $g=p-y$ and $h=p(1-p)$, so curvature depends on the current probabilities. This also explains why a minimum sum of Hessians is not generally the same as a minimum number of rows.

    What makes training efficient?

    The XGBoost paper describes sparsity-aware split search, approximate split proposals, and engineering for efficient data access. In a sparsity-aware split, a default branch for missing values is selected based on the training objective. “Handles missing values” still does not mean missingness is harmless or that training and serving schemas can differ.

    Histogram methods map continuous values into bins and accumulate counts or gradient statistics in each bin. Split search then scans bins instead of every distinct value. This trades some threshold precision for efficiency. Histograms are supported by multiple systems; they are not unique to LightGBM.

    LightGBM: grow where the gain is largest

    LightGBM’s leaf-wise growth expands the eligible leaf offering the largest gain, so different branches may reach different depths. This can fit efficiently but needs controls such as leaf count, depth, and minimum leaf support. A level-wise strategy instead expands nodes by depth.

    Two additional techniques are GOSS, which retains large-gradient examples and samples/reweights smaller-gradient ones, and exclusive feature bundling, which combines suitably sparse, largely non-overlapping features. These are specific techniques, not a claim that every configuration uses the same sampling. See LightGBM’s feature documentation.

    CatBoost: categorical features without self-label shortcuts

    Naive target encoding can leak: if a category appears once, its mean training target is exactly that row’s label. A simplified ordered statistic instead uses matching-category rows that occur earlier in a permutation, plus a smoothed prior:

    $$\mathrm{enc}_i=\frac{\sum_{k\prec i}\mathbf1\{c_k=c_i\}y_k+a\mu}{\sum_{k\prec i}\mathbf1\{c_k=c_i\}+a}.$$

    Here $k\prec i$ means “row $k$ comes before row $i$ in the chosen permutation”; $a>0$ is prior strength and $\mu$ a prior mean. Row $i$’s own label does not enter its category sum. With no earlier match, the value is $\mu$.

    CatBoost also introduces ordered boosting, a distinct permutation-based approach to reduce prediction shift in gradient estimation. Ordered statistics and ordered boosting solve related but different problems; this formula illustrates the former, not the complete library. See the CatBoost paper. Internal ordering does not replace an external time-based or group-based validation split.

    How to discuss library choice

    SystemUseful distinctionWhat to verify
    XGBoostRegularized objective, gradient/Hessian split scoring, multiple tree-building methods.Tree method, memory, missing/categorical representation, validation loss.
    LightGBMHistogram training and leaf-wise growth.Leaf support, number of leaves, fit on smaller/noisier datasets.
    CatBoostOrdered categorical statistics and ordered boosting techniques.Correct categorical columns, chosen mode, rare/unseen-category behavior.

    These are distinctions, not exclusive feature lists or a universal speed ranking. Benchmark compatible objectives on the same leakage-safe splits, under a comparable tuning budget. Compare prediction quality, calibration, training cost, memory, and serving latency. Library capabilities and defaults change; state your configuration when making a performance claim.
