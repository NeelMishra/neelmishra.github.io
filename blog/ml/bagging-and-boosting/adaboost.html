    AdaBoost: Weights, Votes & the Proof

    September 2026 · 4 min read

    AdaBoost changes which training examples matter, then combines the resulting classifiers with weighted votes. We will use the binary, discrete version: labels and each weak learner’s predictions are $y_i,h_t(x_i)\in\{-1,+1\}$. Other variants use different conventions.

    The full algorithm in four equations

    Start with uniform example weights $D_1(i)=1/n$. In round $t$, fit a classifier using those weights. A decision stump is common, but not required. Measure its weighted error:

    $$\epsilon_t=\sum_iD_t(i)\,\mathbf1\{y_i\ne h_t(x_i)\}.$$

    Give it a vote strength, then update and normalize the example weights:

    $$\alpha_t=\frac12\ln\frac{1-\epsilon_t}{\epsilon_t},\qquad D_{t+1}(i)=\frac{D_t(i)e^{-\alpha_ty_ih_t(x_i)}}{Z_t}.$$

    $Z_t$ is the sum of all unnormalized new weights, so the next weights sum to one. Correct predictions have $y_ih_t(x_i)=+1$ and get multiplied by $e^{-\alpha_t}$; incorrect ones have product $-1$ and get multiplied by $e^{\alpha_t}$.

    $$F_T(x)=\sum_{t=1}^T\alpha_th_t(x),\qquad \hat y(x)=\operatorname{sign}(F_T(x)).$$

    Specify a deterministic tie rule if $F_T(x)=0$. The model adds votes; it does not take an unweighted majority. These equations use the standard binary convention underlying Freund and Schapire’s boosting algorithm.

    Two rounds with actual numbers

    Suppose there are five rows, each initially weighted $0.2$. The first learner misclassifies only row 1. Then $\epsilon_1=0.2$ and $\alpha_1=\tfrac12\ln4=\ln2\approx0.693$.

    The normalizer is $Z_1=0.4+4(0.1)=0.8$. One previously missed row now carries half the total training weight. This is a change to the training objective; no new observation has been collected.

    Suppose the second learner misses rows 2 and 3. Its weighted error is $0.125+0.125=0.25$, even though its ordinary error is $2/5=0.4$. Thus $\alpha_2=\tfrac12\ln3\approx0.549$. Applying the same update gives:

    $$D_3=\left(\frac13,\frac14,\frac14,\frac1{12},\frac1{12}\right).$$

    On a new input where $h_1(x)=+1$ and $h_2(x)=-1$, the score is $0.693-0.549=0.144$, so the prediction is positive. The more reliable learner under its own round’s weights has the stronger vote.

    Where the log-ratio vote comes from

    Consider exponential loss $L(F)=\sum_i e^{-y_iF(x_i)}$. Hold the previous ensemble fixed and add a classifier: $F_{\mathrm{new}}=F+\alpha h$. Define $D(i)=e^{-y_iF(x_i)}/L(F)$. These normalized loss contributions are exactly the weights produced by repeated AdaBoost updates.

    Split the new loss into examples that $h$ gets right and wrong. Their total old weights are $1-\epsilon$ and $\epsilon$, respectively:

    $$\frac{L(F+\alpha h)}{L(F)}=(1-\epsilon)e^{-\alpha}+\epsilon e^\alpha.$$

    Differentiate with respect to the single unknown $\alpha$ and set the result to zero:

    $$-(1-\epsilon)e^{-\alpha}+\epsilon e^\alpha=0 \quad\Rightarrow\quad e^{2\alpha}=\frac{1-\epsilon}{\epsilon} \quad\Rightarrow\quad\alpha=\frac12\ln\frac{1-\epsilon}{\epsilon}.$$

    For $0<\epsilon<1$, the second derivative is positive, so this stationary point is the minimum. For $\epsilon<1/2$, minimizing weighted classification error also minimizes the optimized loss factor $Z=2\sqrt{\epsilon(1-\epsilon)}$.

    Why training can continue after zero classification error

    The signed margin is $y_iF(x_i)$. A positive margin means a correct classification; a larger positive margin means a stronger vote in the right direction. Even once every margin is positive, increasing them can still reduce exponential loss. Zero classification error therefore does not imply that boosting has finished optimizing its objective.

    Counting ties conservatively as errors, $\mathbf1\{y_iF_T(x_i)\le0\}\le e^{-y_iF_T(x_i)}$. Since $F_0=0$ and $L(F_0)=n$:

    $$\text{training error}\le\frac{L(F_T)}n=\prod_t Z_t \le\exp\left(-2\sum_t\gamma_t^2\right),\qquad \gamma_t=\frac12-\epsilon_t.$$

    The last inequality follows from $Z_t=\sqrt{1-4\gamma_t^2}$ and $\ln(1-u)\le-u$. A uniform positive edge $\gamma_t\ge\gamma>0$ gives exponential decay in rounds. Merely saying every error is slightly below $1/2$ does not give a uniform rate, and this is a training bound, not a test-error guarantee.

    Edge cases and failure modes

    • $\epsilon=0$: the formula asks for an infinite vote. A practical implementation handles this as a perfect learner and stops, rather than computing an infinity.
    • $\epsilon=1/2$: the vote is zero and that round makes no progress.
    • $\epsilon>1/2$: reject or refit the learner; in this binary setting flipping all its predictions makes its error less than $1/2$. A useful weak learner must beat chance under the current weights.
    • Noisy labels: repeatedly misclassified examples can attract large weights. Deep learners, too many rounds, or corrupted labels can hurt test performance. Tune capacity and rounds on validation data.

    The score is not automatically a calibrated probability. Also, “boosting resamples misclassified rows” is too narrow: weighting can be implemented directly, and gradient boosting usually fits gradient targets instead. A learning-rate multiplier on $\alpha_t$ is a regularized variant; the exact line-minimization proof above describes the unshrunk update.
