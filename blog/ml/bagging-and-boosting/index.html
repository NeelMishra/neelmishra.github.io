    Bagging & Boosting: Interview Roadmap

    September 2026 · 4 min read

    A decision tree can change dramatically when a few training rows change. An ensemble combines trees so that the final prediction is more useful than any one tree. Bagging trains different versions of a learner and aggregates them; boosting builds an additive predictor in stages, using the current predictor to decide the next update.

    These notes build the argument from scratch. You need only the idea of a decision tree, a mean, and a derivative; each less familiar step is explained where it appears. Read the first five guides once, then use the final guide for active recall.

    The study route

    1. Bagging & random forests

      Bootstrap samples, why averaging works, the correlation floor, out-of-bag evaluation, and tuning.

    2. AdaBoost

      Follow two rounds of example weights, derive the learner’s vote, and understand margins and noisy labels.

    3. Gradient boosting

      Go from residual fitting to gradients, then from regression to classification with logits.

    4. XGBoost, LightGBM & CatBoost

      Derive a leaf value and split gain, then distinguish the engineering and categorical-feature choices.

    5. Interview questions & practical decisions

      Explain algorithms aloud, answer follow-ups, diagnose failures, and rehearse a coding round.

    The comparison to remember

    QuestionBagging / random forestBoosting
    What changes between trees?Sampled rows; in forests, candidate features too.The training objective or targets depend on the current ensemble.
    How are predictions combined?Average values or probabilities; voting is another classification rule.Add learned corrections; AdaBoost uses weighted votes.
    Training dependency?Trees can train independently.Each round depends on earlier rounds.
    Main intuition?Cancel fluctuations across diverse learners.Improve an objective through successive corrections.
    Typical capacity controls?Leaf size, depth, feature/sample fractions.Those controls plus learning rate and number of rounds.

    “Bagging reduces variance; boosting reduces bias” is a useful opening, but an incomplete answer. Boosting can also reduce variance through regularization, and bagging can change bias. Neither mechanism guarantees better test performance.

    Why diversity helps

    For squared-error regression, let $Y=f^*(x)+\epsilon$, with zero-mean noise independent of the training data $D$ and variance $\sigma_\epsilon^2$. At a fixed input:

    $$\mathbb E_{D,\epsilon}\!\left[(Y-\hat f_D(x))^2\right] =\underbrace{\left(f^*(x)-\mathbb E_D[\hat f_D(x)]\right)^2}_{\text{bias squared}} +\underbrace{\operatorname{Var}_D(\hat f_D(x))}_{\text{variance}} +\underbrace{\sigma_\epsilon^2}_{\text{noise}}.$$

    The expectation over $D$ means: imagine collecting a new training dataset and refitting, repeatedly. To see the identity, add and subtract the mean prediction inside the square. The cross terms vanish because the deviations and noise have mean zero. Averaging predictions can reduce the variance term; it cannot remove irreducible noise. This particular decomposition is for squared loss, not a formula to apply unchanged to classification error.

    Notation without surprises

    There are $n$ training examples $(x_i,y_i)$, $p$ input features, and $T$ boosting rounds or $B$ bagged trees. A tree is a function that routes $x$ through splits and returns its leaf’s value. A stump has one split and two leaves. $F_t(x)$ denotes the ensemble’s score after round $t$; a score is not necessarily a probability.

    Your interview target: explain the mechanism, derive its central equation, walk through a small example, and identify when it fails. Memorizing algorithm names does not substitute for these four abilities.
