    K-Nearest Neighbors (KNN): Overview, Voronoi Geometry & Reading Guide

    October 2026 · 11 min read

    Parametric models such as linear regression and logistic regression compress the entire training dataset $\mathcal D = \{(x_i, y_i)\}_{i=1}^n$ into a fixed-length parameter vector $\hat\beta \in \mathbb R^{p+1}$ and discard the individual observations at prediction time. $K$-Nearest Neighbors (KNN) makes the opposite trade-off: it is a non-parametric, instance-based (lazy) learner that retains the training observations and forms predictions locally around each new query point $q \in \mathbb R^p$.

    Looking for the step-by-step handwritten-notes walkthrough? Read the companion K-Nearest Neighbors handwritten-notes guide for a six-point table with exact integer distances, hand-verified $K=1, 3, 5$ majority votes, inverse-distance weights, and the Income-vs.-Age feature scaling calculation.

    1. The Neighborhood Operator and Local Prediction

    Given a distance metric $d: \mathbb R^p \times \mathbb R^p \to [0, \infty)$ and a query vector $q \in \mathbb R^p$, permute the training indices $(1), (2), \ldots, (n)$ in non-decreasing order of distance to $q$:

    $$d\bigl(x_{(1)}, q\bigr) \le d\bigl(x_{(2)}, q\bigr) \le \cdots \le d\bigl(x_{(K)}, q\bigr) \le \cdots \le d\bigl(x_{(n)}, q\bigr).$$

    The $K$-nearest neighborhood of $q$ is the index set of the first $K$ training points,

    $$\mathcal N_K(q) = \bigl\{(1), (2), \ldots, (K)\bigr\}.$$

    From $\mathcal N_K(q)$, KNN estimates the conditional target distribution or conditional expectation directly:

    • Classification posterior estimate: $\hat P(Y = c \mid X = q) = \dfrac{1}{K}\sum_{i \in \mathcal N_K(q)} \mathbb I(y_i = c)$, predicting $\hat y(q) = \arg\max_c \hat P(Y = c \mid X = q)$.
    • Regression conditional mean estimate: $\hat f(q) = \dfrac{1}{K}\sum_{i \in \mathcal N_K(q)} y_i$.

    2. Voronoi Tessellations and Decision Boundaries

    When $K = 1$ under Euclidean distance, every training point $x_i$ owns a convex polyhedral region called its Voronoi cell:

    $$V_i = \bigl\{q \in \mathbb R^p : \lVert q - x_i\rVert_2 \le \lVert q - x_j\rVert_2 \;\text{for all } j \ne i\bigr\}.$$

    Each boundary segment between adjacent cells $V_i$ and $V_j$ is a portion of the perpendicular bisector hyperplane $\{q : (x_j - x_i)^\top q = \frac12(\lVert x_j\rVert_2^2 - \lVert x_i\rVert_2^2)\}$. The $1$-NN decision boundary is the union of all Voronoi edges separating cells of different classes. As $K$ increases, the boundary averages over overlapping $K$-order Voronoi regions, smoothing out isolated single-point islands.

    3. Why Simple 1-NN Works: The Cover–Hart Theorem

    A foundational result by Cover and Hart (1967) explains why nearest-neighbor classification is such a strong baseline. Suppose $(X, Y)$ are drawn i.i.d. on a separable metric space and let $\eta(x) = P(Y = 1 \mid X = x)$ be the true posterior probability in binary classification. The best possible classifier—the Bayes optimal classifier—predicts $\mathbb I(\eta(x) \ge 1/2)$ and incurs pointwise risk $r^*(x) = \min\{\eta(x), 1 - \eta(x)\}$ and overall Bayes error rate $\mathcal R^* = \mathbb E[r^*(X)]$.

    As $n \to \infty$, the closest training point $X_{(1)}$ to $x$ converges to $x$ with probability 1 ($X_{(1)} \to x$). Because $\eta$ is continuous almost everywhere, $Y_{(1)}$ is drawn with success probability $\eta(X_{(1)}) \to \eta(x)$ independently of $Y$. A $1$-NN error at $x$ happens when $(Y = 1, Y_{(1)} = 0)$ or $(Y = 0, Y_{(1)} = 1)$, giving asymptotic conditional risk:

    $$r_{1\text{-NN}}(x) = \eta(x)\bigl(1 - \eta(x)\bigr) + \bigl(1 - \eta(x)\bigr)\eta(x) = 2\eta(x)\bigl(1 - \eta(x)\bigr).$$

    Since $r^*(x) = \min\{\eta(x), 1 - \eta(x)\}$, we have $2\eta(1-\eta) \le 2r^*(1 - r^*)$. Taking expectations and applying Jensen's inequality yields the famous Cover–Hart bound:

    $$\mathcal R^* \;\le\; \lim_{n \to \infty} \mathcal R_{1\text{-NN}} \;\le\; 2\mathcal R^*(1 - \mathcal R^*) \;\le\; 2\mathcal R^*.$$

    In plain words: with enough data in a fixed dimension, even the simplest $1$-NN classifier has an error rate at most twice the Bayes optimal error rate. Moreover, if $K \to \infty$ while $K/n \to 0$, the $K$-NN risk converges all the way to the Bayes optimal risk $\mathcal R^*$ (Stone's theorem, 1977).

    4. Dual View: Fixed-Radius Parzen Windows vs. Variable-Radius KNN

    Given $n$ points in $\mathbb R^p$, a local density estimator around $q$ containing $K$ points inside a ball of volume $V_K(q)$ estimates the probability density as:

    $$\hat p(q) = \frac{K}{n\,V_K(q)}.$$
    • Parzen / Kernel Density Estimation (KDE): fixes the bandwidth volume $V(q) = V_h$ and lets the count $K(q)$ vary—which can leave empty balls ($K=0$) in sparse tails.
    • $K$-Nearest Neighbors: fixes the neighbor count $K$ and lets the ball volume $V_K(q) \propto r_K(q)^p$ expand automatically in sparse regions and shrink in dense regions.
