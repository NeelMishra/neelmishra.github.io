    Choosing K, Bias-Variance, the Curse of Dimensionality & Spatial Indexing

    October 2026 · 16 min read

    In polynomial regression, raising the degree $d$ makes the fitted curve wigglier. In $K$-Nearest Neighbors, the integer hyperparameter $K$ acts in the exact opposite direction: $K = 1$ trusts a single noisy observation to carve $n$ jagged islands, whereas $K = n$ ignores the query point's coordinates completely and predicts the global average $\bar y$ everywhere.

    In this final part of the series, we prove the exact bias–variance decomposition of KNN from first principles, trace Leave-One-Out Cross-Validation (LOOCV) by hand to see why $K = 1$ always fakes zero training error, quantify why distances lose contrast in high dimensions, and step through an exact 2D KD-Tree branch-and-bound query that prunes half the dataset without computing distances.

    1. Step-by-Step Proof of the KNN Bias–Variance Decomposition

    Fix $n$ deterministic design points $x_1, \dots, x_n \in \mathbb R^p$, and suppose training targets obey the additive noise model $y_i = f(x_i) + \varepsilon_i$, where $f: \mathbb R^p \to \mathbb R$ is the unknown true regression function and $\varepsilon_1, \dots, \varepsilon_n$ are independent noise variables satisfying $\mathbb E[\varepsilon_i] = 0$ and $\operatorname{Var}(\varepsilon_i) = \sigma^2$. At a query point $q \in \mathbb R^p$, a fresh test outcome $Y = f(q) + \varepsilon_{\text{new}}$ arrives with $\mathbb E[\varepsilon_{\text{new}}] = 0$, $\operatorname{Var}(\varepsilon_{\text{new}}) = \sigma^2$, and $\varepsilon_{\text{new}}$ independent of $\{\varepsilon_i\}_{i=1}^n$.

    Because the design points $\{x_i\}_{i=1}^n$ and query $q$ are fixed, the $K$-nearest index set $\mathcal N_K(q)$ is fixed across noise realizations. Let us first compute the mean $\mu_K(q)$ and variance of the uniform $K$-NN estimator $\hat f_K(q) = \frac{1}{K}\sum_{i \in \mathcal N_K(q)} y_i$:

    $$\mu_K(q) := \mathbb E\!\left[\hat f_K(q)\right] = \frac{1}{K}\sum_{i \in \mathcal N_K(q)} \bigl(f(x_i) + \mathbb E[\varepsilon_i]\bigr) = \frac{1}{K}\sum_{i \in \mathcal N_K(q)} f(x_i),$$ $$\operatorname{Var}\!\left(\hat f_K(q)\right) = \operatorname{Var}\!\left(\frac{1}{K}\sum_{i \in \mathcal N_K(q)} \varepsilon_i\right) = \frac{1}{K^2}\sum_{i \in \mathcal N_K(q)} \operatorname{Var}(\varepsilon_i) = \frac{K\sigma^2}{K^2} = \frac{\sigma^2}{K}.$$

    Now split the test error $Y - \hat f_K(q)$ into three interpretable pieces by inserting $\pm f(q)$ and $\pm \mu_K(q)$:

    $$Y - \hat f_K(q) = \underbrace{\bigl(Y - f(q)\bigr)}_{\varepsilon_{\text{new}}} + \underbrace{\bigl(f(q) - \mu_K(q)\bigr)}_{\text{bias } b_K(q)} + \underbrace{\bigl(\mu_K(q) - \hat f_K(q)\bigr)}_{\text{zero-mean estimation noise}}.$$

    When we square this sum and take expectations, all three pairwise cross-terms vanish identically: $b_K(q)$ is a deterministic constant while $\mathbb E[\varepsilon_{\text{new}}] = 0$ and $\mathbb E[\mu_K(q) - \hat f_K(q)] = 0$, and $\mathbb E\bigl[\varepsilon_{\text{new}}(\mu_K(q) - \hat f_K(q))\bigr] = \mathbb E[\varepsilon_{\text{new}}]\,\mathbb E[\mu_K(q) - \hat f_K(q)] = 0$ by independence between test noise and training noise. Only the three squared terms survive:

    $$\mathbb E\!\left[\bigl(Y - \hat f_K(q)\bigr)^2 \;\middle|\; X = q\right] = \underbrace{\sigma^2}_{\text{Irreducible Noise}} + \underbrace{\left(f(q) - \frac{1}{K}\sum_{i \in \mathcal N_K(q)} f(x_i)\right)^2}_{\operatorname{Bias}^2(\hat f_K(q))} + \underbrace{\frac{\sigma^2}{K}}_{\operatorname{Var}(\hat f_K(q))}.$$

    Effective Degrees of Freedom $\operatorname{df} = n / K$ and Minimax Optimal $K^*$

    In linear smoothers $\hat y = S_K y$ (where $\hat y \in \mathbb R^n$ stacks the fitted values at the $n$ training points), Hastie & Tibshirani define effective degrees of freedom as $\operatorname{df}(S_K) = \operatorname{tr}(S_K)$. Because every distinct training point $x_i$ sits at distance $d(x_i, x_i) = 0$ from itself, $x_i$ always belongs to $\mathcal N_K(x_i)$ with weight $S_{ii} = 1/K$, giving:

    $$\operatorname{df}(\hat f_K) = \operatorname{tr}(S_K) = \sum_{i=1}^n \frac{1}{K} = \frac{n}{K}.$$

    At $K = 1$, $\operatorname{df} = n$ (one parameter per row); at $K = n$, $\operatorname{df} = 1$ (a single intercept $\bar y$). For twice-differentiable $f$ on a positive density in $\mathbb R^p$, a second-order Taylor expansion over a ball of radius $r_K \propto (K/n)^{1/p}$ shows $\operatorname{Bias}^2 \propto r_K^4 \propto (K/n)^{4/p}$ (the linear gradient term cancels by symmetry inside interior balls). Minimizing $C_1 (K/n)^{4/p} + \sigma^2 / K$ with respect to $K$ yields the theoretical sweet spot $K^* \asymp n^{4/(p+4)}$ and minimax MSE rate $O\!\left(n^{-4/(p+4)}\right)$.

    2. Selecting $K$ via Cross-Validation: Worked 6-Point LOOCV U-Curve

    Why must you never choose $K$ using training error? Look at $K = 1$: for every training point $x_i$, its single closest neighbor in the training set is itself ($d(x_i, x_i) = 0$), so $\hat f_1(x_i) = y_i$ and $\text{MSE}_{\text{train}}(K = 1) = 0$—even if $y_i$ is pure Gaussian white noise! Evaluating on held-out folds (such as $V$-fold cross-validation or Leave-One-Out Cross-Validation, LOOCV) bars self-lookup so noise memorization gets penalized immediately.

    Let us verify the classic U-curve on a tie-free 6-point 1D regression dataset consisting of two noisy clusters: $x = (1.00,\; 1.15,\; 1.40,\; 8.60,\; 8.85,\; 9.00)$ with targets $y = (10,\; 16,\; 10,\; 22,\; 16,\; 22)$ (left cluster mean $12$, right cluster mean $20$). In LOOCV, holding out row $i$ leaves $n - 1 = 5$ training candidates, so we test odd $K \in \{1, 3, 5\}$:

    $K = 1$ $6.0$ $(10, 16, 10, 22, 16, 22)$ $0.0000$ $(16, 10, 16, 16, 22, 16)$ $36.0000$ Overfitting (High Variance): every point copies its noisy neighbor ($\pm 6$ error on every row).

    Notice how monotonically deceptive Training MSE is ($0.00 \to 8.00 \to 15.84$), whereas LOOCV MSE traces the true U-curve ($36.00 \to \mathbf{25.33} \to 34.56$) and selects $K^* = 3$.

    3. The Curse of Dimensionality: Why High-$p$ Neighborhoods Stop Being Local

    Look back at the minimax MSE exponent $O\!\left(n^{-4/(p+4)}\right)$: in $p = 1$ dimension, cutting root-MSE in half requires roughly $2^{5/2} \approx 5.7\times$ more rows; in $p = 36$ dimensions, cutting root-MSE in half requires $2^{20} \approx \mathbf{1{,}048{,}576\times}$ more rows! Two geometric phenomena explain why Euclidean neighborhoods collapse as $p$ grows:

    Pillar A: Hypercube Edge Expansion $\ell_p(\alpha) = \alpha^{1/p}$

    Draw $n$ training points uniformly on the $p$-dimensional unit hypercube $[0, 1]^p$. To enclose a tiny fraction $\alpha = K/n \in (0, 1)$ of the data inside a local axis-aligned subcube of volume $\ell_p^p = \alpha$, each coordinate edge must span $\ell_p(\alpha) = \alpha^{1/p}$:

    Required per-coordinate side length $\ell_p(\alpha) = \alpha^{1/p}$ of a subcube capturing $\alpha = 1\%$ or $\alpha = 10\%$ of uniform data in $[0, 1]^p$.
    Data Fraction $\alpha$ $p = 1$ $p = 2$ $p = 5$ $p = 10$ $p = 20$ $p = 50$ $p = 100$
    $\alpha = 0.01$ ($1\%$ of data) $0.010$ $0.100$ $0.398$ $0.631$ $0.794$ $0.912$ $0.955$
    $\alpha = 0.10$ ($10\%$ of data) $0.100$ $0.316$ $0.631$ $0.794$ $0.891$ $0.955$ $0.977$

    Already at $p = 10$, a "local" $1\%$ neighborhood must span $63.1\%$ of every coordinate's full range; by $p = 50$, it spans $91.2\%$—nearly the entire input box!

    Pillar B: Relative Distance Concentration (Beyer et al., 1999)

    Suppose coordinates $(X_{ij} - q_j)^2$ are i.i.d. across $j = 1, \dots, p$ with finite mean $\mu_d > 0$ and variance $v_d < \infty$. Squared distance $D_p^2 = \sum_{j=1}^p (X_{ij} - q_j)^2$ has mean $p\mu_d$ and standard deviation $\sqrt{p v_d}$, so its relative spread $\operatorname{SD}(D_p^2)/\mathbb E[D_p^2] = \sqrt{v_d} / (\mu_d \sqrt{p}) \to 0$ as $p \to \infty$. Consequently, for fixed sample size $n$, the farthest and nearest training points collapse onto a thin spherical shell around $q$:

    $$\frac{d_{\max}(q) - d_{\min}(q)}{d_{\min}(q)} \xrightarrow{\;P\;} 0 \qquad \text{as } p \to \infty.$$

    The Manifold Escape Hatch: Why does KNN still shine on $768$-dimensional text or image embeddings? Because real signals rarely fill $[0, 1]^p$ uniformly—they concentrate near a low-dimensional manifold of intrinsic dimension $d_{\text{int}} \ll p$. Projecting high-dimensional inputs onto informative subspaces via PCA, Fisher LDA, or metric/representation learning (Behavioral Embeddings) restores distance contrast before neighbor search.

    4. Spatial Indexing & Worked 2D KD-Tree Pruning Trace

    Scanning all $n$ rows by brute force takes $O(np)$ distance arithmetic plus $O(n \log K)$ selection via a size-$K$ max-heap. When $p$ is low ($p \lesssim 15$), exact spatial trees organize training points during a one-time $O(np \log n)$ build so queries skip distant regions:

    Hand-Tracing a 6-Point 2D KD-Tree Build and Query

    Store six 2D points: $P_1 = (2, 3)$, $P_4 = (4, 7)$, $P_2 = (5, 4)$, $P_6 = (7, 2)$, $P_5 = (8, 1)$, and $P_3 = (9, 6)$. Alternating split axes by depth ($\text{axis} = \text{depth} \bmod 2$):

    1. Build phase: At depth $0$ ($x_1$-axis), choose median pivot $P_2 = (5, 4)$ splitting the plane at vertical line $x_1 = 5$. Left points $\{(2, 3), (4, 7)\}$ split at depth $1$ ($x_2$-axis) on $P_1 = (2, 3)$ ($x_2 = 3$, with $P_4 = (4, 7)$ above). Right points $\{(7, 2), (8, 1), (9, 6)\}$ split at depth $1$ ($x_2$-axis) on median $P_6 = (7, 2)$ ($x_2 = 2$, with $P_5 = (8, 1)$ below and $P_3 = (9, 6)$ above).
    2. Descend toward query $q = (2.5, 3.5)$: At root $P_2 = (5, 4)$, record $d(q, P_2) = \sqrt{(2.5 - 5)^2 + (3.5 - 4)^2} = \sqrt{6.50} \approx 2.5495$ ($d_{\text{best}} = 2.5495$). Since $q_1 = 2.5 < 5$, descend into the left child $P_1 = (2, 3)$.
    3. Tighten $d_{\text{best}}$ locally: Evaluate $d(q, P_1) = \sqrt{(2.5 - 2)^2 + (3.5 - 3)^2} = \sqrt{0.50} \approx \mathbf{0.7071}$, tightening $d_{\text{best}} \leftarrow 0.7071$. Since $q_2 = 3.5 > 3$, inspect $P_1$'s upper child $P_4 = (4, 7)$ ($d = \sqrt{14.50} \approx 3.8079$, ignored); $P_1$'s lower child is empty.
    4. Branch-and-bound pruning at root $x_1 = 5$: Backtracking to $P_2$, could any point in the right half-plane $x_1 \ge 5$ beat $d_{\text{best}} = 0.7071$? Every point $x$ with $x_1 \ge 5$ satisfies $\lVert x - q\rVert_2 \ge |x_1 - q_1| \ge |5.0 - 2.5| = \mathbf{2.5000}$. Since $2.5000 > 0.7071$, the search ball $B(q, 0.7071)$ never crosses $x_1 = 5$—pruning $\{P_6, P_5, P_3\}$ ($50\%$ of the dataset) with zero distance evaluations!

    Verify the 6-point LOOCV U-curve & KD-Tree pruning trace in Python 
    import numpy as np
from sklearn.model_selection import LeaveOneOut, cross_val_score
from sklearn.neighbors import KDTree, KNeighborsRegressor

# 1. Exact 6-point LOOCV U-curve across K in {1, 3, 5}
X_1d = np.array([[1.00], [1.15], [1.40], [8.60], [8.85], [9.00]])
y_1d = np.array([10.0, 16.0, 10.0, 22.0, 16.0, 22.0])
loo = LeaveOneOut()

for K in [1, 3, 5]:
    reg = KNeighborsRegressor(n_neighbors=K).fit(X_1d, y_1d)
    tr_mse = np.mean((y_1d - reg.predict(X_1d)) ** 2)
    loo_mse = -np.mean(cross_val_score(reg, X_1d, y_1d, cv=loo, scoring="neg_mean_squared_error"))
    print(f"K={K} (df={len(X_1d)/K:.1f}): Train MSE = {tr_mse:7.4f} | LOOCV MSE = {loo_mse:7.4f}")

# 2. Verify 2D KD-Tree winner & root-split pruning gap for q = (2.5, 3.5)
pts_2d = np.array([[2, 3], [4, 7], [5, 4], [7, 2], [8, 1], [9, 6]], dtype=float)
q_2d = np.array([[2.5, 3.5]])
tree = KDTree(pts_2d, leaf_size=1)
dist, ind = tree.query(q_2d, k=1)
print(f"KD-Tree 1-NN: point={pts_2d[ind[0, 0]].tolist()}, d_best={dist[0, 0]:.4f}, root plane gap |2.5 - 5.0|={abs(2.5 - 5.0):.4f}")
