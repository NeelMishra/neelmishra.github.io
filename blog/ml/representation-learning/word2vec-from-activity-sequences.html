    Word2Vec for User Behavior: From Sequences to Training Pairs

    September 2026 · 18 min read

    Before you start: an embedding is a learned table lookup. Each item ID selects a vector. This chapter explains where the learning signal comes from.

    Skip-gram learns an item’s vector by asking it to predict nearby items. In text, the items are words. In a behavior log, they might be product views in a shopping session. We will follow one pair, jacket → socks, from the session that creates it to the exact numerical gradient steps that move its vectors.

    A session becomes several training examples

    Consider a five-event browsing session boots → socks → jacket → boots → hat. Choose jacket (at position 3) as the center item and a symmetric context window radius $w = 1$. Its immediate left and right neighbors create two observed (“positive”) training pairs: (jacket, socks) and (jacket, boots). Here “positive” means co-observed within window radius $w$—it does not mean purchased, liked, or interchangeable.

    The window is clipped cleanly at session start and end boundaries. Applying the same window rule to every position generates the positive pair stream. Because a symmetric window treats left and right neighbors identically, the pair (jacket, socks) erases whether socks was viewed before or after jacket; directional next-item prediction requires causal (past-only) context construction instead.

    Two tables give each item two roles

    Let $\mathcal{V}$ denote the catalog vocabulary of $|\mathcal{V}|$ unique items, and let $d \ge 1$ be the embedding dimension. Skip-gram maintains two separate parameter matrices of shape $|\mathcal{V}| \times d$:

    • Input (center) table $V \in \mathbb{R}^{|\mathcal{V}| \times d}$: row $v_w \in \mathbb{R}^d$ represents item $w \in \mathcal{V}$ when $w$ acts as the center item.
    • Context (output) table $U \in \mathbb{R}^{|\mathcal{V}| \times d}$: row $u_c \in \mathbb{R}^d$ represents item $c \in \mathcal{V}$ when $c$ acts as a candidate context neighbor.
    • Pair score $s \in \mathbb{R}$: the raw inner product between the context vector $u_{\text{socks}}$ and the center vector $v_{\text{jacket}}$:
    $$s = u_{\text{socks}}^\top v_{\text{jacket}} = \sum_{r=1}^{d} u_{\text{socks}, r}\, v_{\text{jacket}, r}.$$

    The scalar $s$ is an unconstrained real number—not yet a probability, not length-normalized like cosine similarity, and computed across two different tables ($U$ and $V$). Every catalog item owns two parameter rows ($v_i$ and $u_i$): when socks later becomes the center item of its own window, training looks up $v_{\text{socks}}$ in $V$ rather than $u_{\text{socks}}$ in $U$.

    How this relates to full-softmax skip-gram

    Let $\mathcal{V}$ be the full item vocabulary, $w \in \mathcal{V}$ the center item ID, $c \in \mathcal{V}$ the observed context item ID, and $j \in \mathcal{V}$ an index ranging over every item in the catalog. Full-softmax skip-gram normalizes exponentiated dot products across all $|\mathcal{V}|$ items:

    $$P(c\mid w)=\frac{\exp(u_c^\top v_w)}{\sum_{j\in\mathcal{V}}\exp(u_j^\top v_w)}.$$

    Computing that denominator and its gradient requires touching all $|\mathcal{V}|$ rows of $U$ on every single training pair. Negative sampling replaces multi-class normalization over $\mathcal{V}$ with $K + 1$ independent binary logistic comparisons; its sigmoid outputs do not sum to $1$ across $\mathcal{V}$.

    Turn one observed pair into a small binary classification task

    Label the observed window pair (jacket, socks) as $y = 1$. Draw $K = 2$ noise context IDs from a background noise distribution—say kettle and mug—and label (jacket, kettle) and (jacket, mug) as $y = 0$. (Label $0$ simply means “drawn from background noise rather than observed in this context window,” not user dislike.)

    For any scalar pair score $s \in \mathbb{R}$, map $s$ into a discriminator probability $D \in (0, 1)$ using the logistic sigmoid $\sigma(s)$, where $e \approx 2.71828$ is Euler’s base:

    $$D = \sigma(s) = \frac{1}{1 + e^{-s}}.$$

    At $s = 0$, $D = 0.5$. Because $1 - \sigma(s) = \sigma(-s)$, the probability assigned to label $y = 0$ for a noise pair with score $s_{n_j}$ is $\sigma(-s_{n_j})$.

    Let $s_+ = u_c^\top v_w$ be the score of the observed pair, let $K \ge 1$ be the number of sampled negatives, and let $s_{n_j} = u_{n_j}^\top v_w$ ($j = 1, \dots, K$) be the score of the $j$-th negative sample. Summing negative log-likelihoods across the $1 + K$ binary comparisons yields the per-pair SGNS loss $\ell$:

    $$\ell = -\log\sigma(s_+) - \sum_{j=1}^{K}\log\sigma(-s_{n_j}).$$

    When all $1 + K = 3$ scores start at $0$, every comparison assigns $0.5$ to its target label, giving initial loss $\ell = -3\log(0.5) = 3\log 2 \approx 2.079442$.

    Watch an actual update change the selected rows

    Initialize $v_{\text{jacket}} = (1, 0)$ and context rows $u_{\text{socks}} = (0, 1)$, $u_{\text{kettle}} = (0, -1)$, and $u_{\text{mug}} = (0, -0.5)$. Because every dot product with $(1, 0)$ picks out the first coordinate, all three initial scores equal $0$, while the nonzero second coordinates produce nonzero gradients.

    For each comparison $r$ with label $y_r \in \{0, 1\}$, differentiating $-\log\sigma(\dots)$ with respect to score $s_r$ gives the signed prediction error $(\sigma(s_r) - y_r)$: $-0.5$ for socks and $+0.5$ for both noise items at step $0$. With learning rate $\eta = 0.2$, one simultaneous gradient step moves $v_{\text{jacket}}$ to $(1.0000, 0.2500)$, moves the three participating rows of $U$, and shifts the three dot-product scores to $(+0.3500, -0.3500, -0.2250)$.

    Follow the first gradient step by hand

    Let $r \in \{0, 1, \dots, K\}$ index the $1 + K$ comparisons in the mini-problem, with target label $y_r \in \{0, 1\}$, context vector $u_r \in \mathbb{R}^d$, and shared center vector $v \in \mathbb{R}^d$. Applying the chain rule to $s_r = u_r^\top v$ gives:

    $$\nabla_v \ell = \sum_{r=0}^{K}\bigl(\sigma(u_r^\top v) - y_r\bigr)u_r, \qquad \nabla_{u_r}\ell = \bigl(\sigma(u_r^\top v) - y_r\bigr)v.$$

    At step $0$: $(\sigma(0)-1)u_{\text{socks}} = -0.5(0, 1) = (0, -0.5)$; $(\sigma(0)-0)u_{\text{kettle}} = +0.5(0, -1) = (0, -0.5)$; and $(\sigma(0)-0)u_{\text{mug}} = +0.5(0, -0.5) = (0, -0.25)$. Summing the three terms gives $\nabla_v \ell = (0, -1.25)$, so $v_{\text{jacket}} \leftarrow (1, 0) - 0.2(0, -1.25) = (1, 0.25)$.

    Using the pre-step $v_{\text{old}} = (1, 0)$ simultaneously for all context updates gives $u_{\text{socks}} \leftarrow (0, 1) - 0.2(-0.5)(1, 0) = (0.1, 1)$, $u_{\text{kettle}} \leftarrow (-0.1, -1)$, and $u_{\text{mug}} \leftarrow (-0.1, -0.5)$. Evaluating $U_{\text{new}} v_{\text{new}}$ yields $0.1(1) + 1(0.25) = +0.35$, $-0.1(1) - 1(0.25) = -0.35$, and $-0.1(1) - 0.5(0.25) = -0.225$.

    The noise distribution changes what the model learns

    Let $f_i > 0$ denote the training corpus frequency (event count) of item $i \in \mathcal{V}$, and let $\alpha \in [0, 1]$ be a smoothing exponent. Word2Vec sets $\alpha = 3/4 = 0.75$ and draws negative item $i$ with probability $P_n(i)$:

    $$P_n(i) = \frac{f_i^{3/4}}{\sum_{j\in\mathcal{V}} f_j^{3/4}}.$$

    In a skewed six-item catalog with counts $[64, 64, 8, 8, 1, 1]$ (total $146$), unsmoothed unigram sampling ($\alpha = 1$) draws mug only $1 / 146 \approx 0.68\%$ of the time. Smoothing with $\alpha = 0.75$ maps weights to $[22.627, 22.627, 4.757, 4.757, 1, 1]$ (sum $56.768$), boosting each rare item’s negative-sampling rate more than $2.5\times$ to $1.76\%$ so rare rows in $U$ still receive repulsive updates.

    Note that negative-sampling smoothing ($\alpha = 0.75$) is distinct from frequent-item subsampling (randomly dropping ultra-popular items like homepage banners from the positive stream before windowing so windows span meaningful item pairs). Document both choices—along with whether accidental positive-ID collisions during negative draws are kept or rejected.

    Session boundaries and event types define “related”

    On behavioral clickstreams, sessionization is feature engineering for the objective function:

    • Never concatenate across user or session boundaries: sorting the entire database by timestamp without grouping by (user_id, session_id) pairs User A’s final item with User B’s first item.
    • Deduplicate consecutive reloads deliberately: five rapid page refreshes of boots fill a radius-2 window with self-pairs (boots, boots) unless collapsed or excluded.
    • Match event types to the retrieval goal: search-refinement clicks and side-by-side detail comparisons emphasize substitutes, whereas same-basket checkout events emphasize complements.

    A small implementation you can inspect

    The self-contained NumPy code below enforces per-session window boundaries and reproduces our 20-step update table to six decimal places.

    Python: session pairs and the computed 20-update example
    import numpy as np

def session_pairs(sessions, radius=1):
    if not isinstance(radius, int) or isinstance(radius, bool) or radius < 1:
        raise ValueError("radius must be a positive integer")
    for tokens in sessions:  # Never concatenate separate sessions.
        for i, center in enumerate(tokens):
            for j in range(max(0, i-radius), min(len(tokens), i+radius+1)):
                if i != j:
                    yield center, tokens[j]

def sgns_step(V, U, center, context, negatives, rate=0.2):
    # V and U are separate floating-point arrays: [vocabulary, dimension].
    # IDs must be valid rows. This function uses a SUM over this fixed batch.
    ids = np.array([context, *negatives], dtype=int)
    labels = np.zeros(len(ids)); labels[0] = 1
    v_old = V[center].copy()
    scores = U[ids] @ v_old
    probabilities = np.exp(-np.logaddexp(0, -scores))  # Stable sigmoid.
    errors = probabilities - labels
    loss = np.logaddexp(0, -scores[0]) + np.logaddexp(0, scores[1:]).sum()
    grad_v = errors @ U[ids]  # All derivatives use the old parameters.
    V[center] -= rate * grad_v
    # Repeated context/noise IDs must accumulate into the same output row.
    np.add.at(U, ids, -rate * errors[:, None] * v_old)
    return float(loss)

# IDs: jacket=0, socks=1, kettle=2, mug=3.
V = np.array([[1., 0.], [.2, .1], [.1, -.2], [-.1, .2]])
U = np.array([[.3, .2], [0., 1.], [0., -1.], [0., -.5]])
for step in range(21):
    scores = U[[1, 2, 3]] @ V[0]
    loss = np.logaddexp(0, -scores[0]) + np.logaddexp(0, scores[1:]).sum()
    if step in [0, 1, 5, 20]:
        print(step, np.round(scores, 6), round(float(loss), 6))
    if step < 20:
        sgns_step(V, U, center=0, context=1, negatives=[2, 3])
# 0  [0. 0. 0.]                         2.079442
# 1  [0.35 -0.35 -0.225]                1.653726
# 5  [1.469952 -1.469952 -1.080366]     0.70618
# 20 [3.566834 -3.566834 -3.225431]     0.094677

    np.add.at accumulates updates cleanly when a sampled noise ID appears twice in the same draw, and np.logaddexp(0, x) prevents overflow on large scores.

    Always verify a library’s exact objective before assuming parity: Apache Spark MLlib’s Word2Vec, for instance, trains with hierarchical softmax rather than negative sampling, and its DataFrame transformer averages item vectors across a sequence.

    Choose the vectors that the retrieval task will consume

    At serving time, $v_i^\top v_j$ (input-to-input similarity, capturing items sharing similar contexts), $u_i^\top u_j$ (context-to-context), and $u_j^\top v_i$ (input-to-context, directly trained to score first-order co-occurrence) answer different questions. Export the table and normalization rule alongside the immutable item-to-row dictionary and an explicit fallback for cold-start catalog additions, then move to Chapter 2: Evaluate Embeddings and Choose Their Dimension.
