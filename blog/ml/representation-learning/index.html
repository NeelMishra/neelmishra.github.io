    Behavioral Embeddings: Reading Guide

    September 2026 · 11 min read

    Behavioral embeddings turn repeated patterns of user activity into dense numerical vectors for items. Once each product, song, or article lives as a $d$-dimensional coordinate row, nearest-neighbor search can retrieve related items in milliseconds and downstream models can consume those vectors as dense features. Yet an embedding has no intrinsic meaning apart from two engineering choices: which events become training pairs and which downstream task judges the resulting neighborhood.

    This two-part series connects both sides of that contract without black-box handwaving. Chapter 1 builds skip-gram with negative sampling (SGNS) from raw session logs down to exact gradient updates; Chapter 2 separates task relevance from approximate nearest-neighbor (ANN) overlap and tests how dimensionality reduction reshapes vector neighborhoods. An embedding-table lookup is the only prerequisite.

    Follow the information from raw events to a task decision

    Imagine an outdoor gear shop building a “similar items” module on product pages. Raw clickstream logs record views, add-to-carts, and purchases. A sliding context window quickly discovers that boots frequently co-occurs with socks—which is great for a “complete the kit” complement widget, and terrible when an out-of-stock boot page needs an interchangeable waterproof boot substitute.

    Worked toy example: co-occurrence, dot products, and cosine similarity

    Before diving into gradients, trace a five-item catalog in $d = 2$ dimensions to see how co-occurrence shapes vectors—and why dot product and cosine similarity rank candidates differently.

    Suppose three browsing sessions occur before our training cutoff:

    • Session 1: boots → socks → jacket
    • Session 2: boots → socks
    • Session 3: trail_shoe → socks → jacket

    Notice two distinct relationships: boots and socks co-occur directly inside Sessions 1 and 2 (first-order co-occurrence: complements). Meanwhile, boots and trail_shoe never appear in the same session, yet both appear immediately beside socks followed by jacket (second-order distributional similarity: substitutes sharing the same context distribution!). Finally, a kitchen mug never shares contexts with outdoor apparel.

    For any two exported item vectors $x = (x_1, \dots, x_d) \in \mathbb{R}^d$ and $y = (y_1, \dots, y_d) \in \mathbb{R}^d$, define:

    • Dot product (inner product): $x^\top y = \sum_{r=1}^{d} x_r y_r \in (-\infty, +\infty)$, sensitive to both directional alignment and vector lengths.
    • Euclidean norm ($\ell_2$ length): $\|x\|_2 = \sqrt{\sum_{r=1}^{d} x_r^2} \ge 0$, which often grows larger for high-frequency items that receive many gradient updates.
    • Cosine similarity: the length-normalized angle score between nonzero vectors,
    $$\cos(x, y) = \frac{x^\top y}{\|x\|_2 \, \|y\|_2} \in [-1, 1].$$

    Querying our catalog with $x = v_{\text{boots}} = (1.6, 1.2)$ (whose Euclidean norm is $\|v_{\text{boots}}\|_2 = \sqrt{1.6^2 + 1.2^2} = \sqrt{4.00} = 2.0$) against four candidate items illustrates the exact trade-off:

    Querying candidate items against boots $v_{\text{boots}} = (1.6, 1.2)$ ($\|v_{\text{boots}}\|_2 = 2.0$).
    Candidate item Vector $y \in \mathbb{R}^2$ Norm $\|y\|_2$ Dot product $x^\top y$ Cosine $\cos(x,y)$ Behavioral role
    socks $(1.8, 2.4)$ $3.0$ $5.76$ (#1 dot) $0.96$ (#2 cos) Frequent co-viewed complement
    trail_shoe $(0.8, 0.6)$ $1.0$ $2.00$ (#2 dot) $1.00$ (#1 cos) Lower-frequency substitute (same angle)
    jacket $(0.0, 1.0)$ $1.0$ $1.20$ (#3 dot) $0.60$ (#3 cos) Broader apparel session neighbor
    mug $(-0.8, -0.6)$ $1.0$ $-2.00$ (#4 dot) $-1.00$ (#4 cos) Unrelated homeware item

    Look closely at the top two rows: trail_shoe $(0.8, 0.6)$ points in the exact same direction as boots $(1.6, 1.2)$, earning $\cos = 1.00$. Yet because popular socks has a three-times-larger norm ($\|y\|_2 = 3.0$), raw dot product scores socks at $5.76$ versus $2.00$ for trail_shoe. Choosing dot product versus cosine similarity—and deciding whether to $\ell_2$-normalize vectors before indexing—is therefore a core modeling decision, not an implementation footnote.

    Make the first experiment specific enough to evaluate

    Before choosing an embedding dimension or an ANN index, write down a five-line experiment contract so training choices and evaluation metrics cannot drift apart:

    Use the interactive labs as checkpoints

    1. In the session window lab, click each occurrence of boots. Verify that sequence positions generate distinct training pairs while both occurrences update the same row of $V$.
    2. In the gradient update lab, step from update $0$ to update $1$ and confirm by hand why $v_{\text{jacket}}$ moves from $(1, 0)$ to $(1, 0.25)$.
    3. In the retrieval comparison lab, inspect why two approximate ANN runs with identical $50\%$ exact-neighbor overlap achieve $0\%$ versus $66.7\%$ task recall.
    4. In the orthogonal rotation lab, rotate the 2D vectors by $90^\circ$ and watch full Euclidean distance stay invariant while single-coordinate truncation flips the nearest neighbor from A to B.

    Continue with the question your experiment raises

    • Dimensionality reduction — compare variance-preserving projection (PCA) and manifold visualization objectives before compressing embeddings.
    • HNSW and Annoy internals — understand how navigable small-world graphs and random-projection trees trade recall for sub-millisecond latency.
    • Relevance judgments — design graded rubrics and pooled test collections.
    • Retrieval and ANN diagnostics — isolate representation misses from ANN search misses and second-stage reranker errors.
    • Model comparison — compute paired bootstrap intervals before declaring a dimension or objective change an improvement.

    For a classic industry adaptation, Barkan and Koenigstein’s Item2Vec applies SGNS to unordered item sets (baskets/orders) by pairing all items in a set regardless of sequence distance—a helpful contrast to the position-window construction in Chapter 1.
