    Clicks Are Feedback, Not Relevance Labels

    September 2026 · 17 min read

    Before you start: paired comparisons and the experimental unit. This chapter moves from offline human relevance labels to biased behavioral logs and live online experiments.

    Position 1 almost always receives far more clicks than position 5. Sometimes rank 1 genuinely holds a better document; just as often, users simply inspect rank 1 four times as frequently. Because the production ranker chooses which items occupy top slots and then trains or evaluates on the resulting click log, treating raw clicks as unbiased relevance labels creates a self-confirming feedback loop.

    This chapter untangles three distinct causal mechanisms with exact arithmetic: position examination bias, off-policy Inverse Propensity Scoring (IPS) (including variance clipping and support failures), and post-treatment denominator bias in randomized online A/B tests.

    Separate examining a slot from clicking an attractive result

    Suppose two items A and B have identical snippet attractiveness: whenever a user actually examines either item, they click with probability $0.50$. However, users examine position $1$ with probability $0.80$ and position $5$ with probability $0.20$. Consequently, the observed click-through rate at position $1$ is $0.80 \times 0.50 = 0.40$ ($40\%$), whereas position $5$ receives $0.20 \times 0.50 = 0.10$ ($10\%$)—a $4\times$ gap driven purely by screen rank!

    Define every variable in the position-based examination model (PBM) before writing its equation:

    • $q$, $d$, $i \in \{1, 2, \dots\}$: the query, candidate document, and 1-based displayed rank position.
    • $E \in \{0, 1\}$: latent binary indicator equal to $1$ if the user examines position $i$.
    • $C \in \{0, 1\}$: observed binary click indicator ($C = 1$ only if $E = 1$).
    • $e_i = P(E = 1 \mid i) \in (0, 1]$: position examination propensity at rank $i$.
    • $c_{q,d} = P(C = 1 \mid E = 1, q, d) \in [0, 1]$: intrinsic snippet click attractiveness of document $d$ for query $q$ given examination.
    $$P(C = 1 \mid q, d, i) = e_i \, c_{q,d}.$$

    Inverse examination weighting recovers snippet attractiveness—not deep relevance

    Across $N_{q,d}$ logged impressions of $(q, d)$ displayed at ranks $i_1, \dots, i_{N_{q,d}}$ with known positive propensities $e_{i_t} > 0$ and observed click outcomes $C_t \in \{0, 1\}$, weighting each click by $1 / e_{i_t}$ yields an unbiased estimator $\hat{c}_{q,d}$ of snippet attractiveness under the PBM:

    $$\hat{c}_{q,d} = \frac{1}{N_{q,d}}\sum_{t=1}^{N_{q,d}} \frac{C_t}{e_{i_t}}, \qquad \mathbb{E}[\hat{c}_{q,d}] = \frac{1}{N_{q,d}}\sum_{t=1}^{N_{q,d}} \frac{e_{i_t} c_{q,d}}{e_{i_t}} = c_{q,d}.$$

    At position $1$ ($e_1 = 0.80$), expected weighted click is $0.40 / 0.80 = 0.50$; at position $5$ ($e_5 = 0.20$), expected weighted click is $0.10 / 0.20 = 0.50$. Notice two caveats: first, non-clicks ($C_t = 0$) must stay in the denominator $N_{q,d}$; second, $\hat{c}_{q,d}$ estimates click attractiveness, which clickbait titles inflate without satisfying post-click task utility.

    Off-policy evaluation: Inverse Propensity Scoring (IPS) and weight clipping

    Now ask a counterfactual policy question on a single recommendation slot with two candidate items $\{A, B\}$. Suppose a historical logging policy $\mu$ showed $A$ $90\%$ of the time and $B$ $10\%$ of the time, and we want to estimate the click rate of a new target policy $\pi$ that shows $A$ and $B$ $50\%$ each. Define all symbols before writing the estimators:

    • $N \ge 1$: total logged impressions (both clicked and unclicked), indexed by $t = 1, \dots, N$.
    • $x_t, a_t, r_t \in \{0, 1\}$: logged context, chosen action (item), and observed reward ($1$ for click, $0$ otherwise) on impression $t$.
    • $\mu(a \mid x) \in [0, 1]$: known probability that the logging policy selected action $a$ in context $x$.
    • $\pi(a \mid x) \in [0, 1]$: probability that the candidate target policy selects action $a$ in context $x$.
    • $M > 0$: optional maximum weight cap used by Clipped IPS ($\widehat{V}_{\mathrm{cIPS}}$) to bound single-event variance at the cost of downward bias.
    $$\widehat{V}_{\mathrm{IPS}}(\pi) = \frac{1}{N}\sum_{t=1}^{N}\frac{\pi(a_t \mid x_t)}{\mu(a_t \mid x_t)}\, r_t, \qquad \widehat{V}_{\mathrm{cIPS}}(\pi; M) = \frac{1}{N}\sum_{t=1}^{N}\min\!\left(M,\, \frac{\pi(a_t \mid x_t)}{\mu(a_t \mid x_t)}\right) r_t.$$

    Python: reproduce the four logging policies and weight clipping
    from fractions import Fraction as F

def evaluate(b_probability, cap=None):
    mu = [1 - b_probability, b_probability]
    target = [F(1, 2), F(1, 2)]
    reward = [F(1, 5), F(4, 5)]
    impressions = [1000 * p for p in mu]
    clicks = [n * r for n, r in zip(impressions, reward)]
    raw = sum(clicks) / 1000
    if any(p > 0 and m == 0 for p, m in zip(target, mu)):
        return raw, None
    weights = [p / m for p, m in zip(target, mu)]
    if cap is not None:
        weights = [min(w, cap) for w in weights]
    ips = sum(w * c for w, c in zip(weights, clicks)) / 1000
    return raw, ips

for p in [F(1, 2), F(1, 10), F(1, 100), F(0)]:
    print(p, evaluate(p), evaluate(p, cap=F(2)))

    Online A/B experiments: keep the Intent-to-Treat denominator intact

    When offline metrics and off-policy logs reach their limits, a randomized online experiment assigns $N_0 = 100$ eligible users to Control ($Z = 0$) and $N_1 = 100$ eligible users to Treatment ($Z = 1$). Suppose Control records $60$ clickers and $30$ task completions, while Treatment records $80$ clickers and $32$ task completions.     Use offline–online disagreement to pinpoint what changed

    When offline $\operatorname{nDCG}@10$ rises while live task completion stays flat, check four culprits in order: (1) query-mix mismatch (gains concentrated on rare tail queries), (2) p95/p99 latency regressions offsetting ranking gains, (3) snippet/UI layout changes shifting examination propensities, or (4) sample-ratio mismatch (SRM) in experiment logging.
