    Search Evaluation Starts with Relevance, Not a Metric

    September 2026 · 4 min read

    Before you start: Basic train/validation/test splits. No ranking-metric knowledge is required.

    A search engine returns five attractive products for “waterproof hiking shoes.” Every item is popular, every image looks good, and every result gets clicks. But only one item is waterproof. Before choosing a metric, decide what a successful answer to this query means. Evaluation begins with a contract between a query, the available catalog, and a judgment of usefulness.

    Define the unit of relevance

    A query is more than its text. “Running shoes” might be issued in a women’s category, with a size filter, in a location with limited stock. Record the context that the system was permitted to use. For document search, define whether you judge a passage, a document, or an entire answer. For recommendations, define the user and decision time. Otherwise annotators and models solve different tasks.

    An illustrative rubric; decide whether hard-constraint failures receive grade 0 before labeling.
    GradeMeaning for the exampleExample
    3Satisfies the intent and hard constraintsWaterproof hiking shoes, available size
    2Useful but a softer preference is unmetWaterproof hiking shoes above preferred price
    1Related, but misses an essential needOrdinary hiking shoes
    0Irrelevant or ineligibleWaterproof jacket

    Have annotators judge query–item pairs without seeing the producing system or its rank. Provide concise instructions and difficult boundary examples. Double-label a sample, inspect disagreements, and adjudicate ambiguous cases. Agreement is a diagnostic of the rubric, not proof that the rubric captures user value. Keep the original judgments as well as the adjudicated label.

    Build a collection that can detect your failure modes

    Sample ordinary traffic, then explicitly include rare intents, tail queries, new items, spelling errors, ambiguous requests, and strict filters. Store a slice tag on each query. A traffic-weighted average estimates average traffic performance; a balanced diagnostic set reveals failure modes. Both are useful, but they answer different questions. Do not silently report the balanced average as a traffic estimate.

    Keep the query set, catalog, rubric, qrels (query relevance judgments), and run files versioned independently. A new judgment should not require silently rewriting the previous report. Record the evaluator version too: changing a gain function can change an nDCG value without changing one retrieved item.

    Why unjudged does not mean irrelevant

    Large catalogs cannot be exhaustively labeled. A practical pool contains the union of top results from diverse systems: lexical, dense, hybrid, and a candidate new model. Deduplicate documents before annotation. Pooling makes labeling tractable, but a new retriever may find useful documents outside the old pool. Treating all of those as irrelevant biases the comparison toward the systems that built the pool.

    Report the judged fraction at the evaluation cutoff, especially for each competing system. If system A has 98% judged coverage and B has 63%, investigate B’s unjudged results before declaring it worse. A common scoring convention assigns zero gain to unjudged results; that convention makes a reproducible number, not a factual negative label. A judged-only metric changes the task by removing unknown results and may also distort comparisons. Show the policy explicitly.

    Freeze what was knowable at the decision time

    Split before fitting embeddings, choosing thresholds, mining training negatives, or tuning prompts. Group variants of the same query and near-duplicate products when memorization would otherwise cross the split. For a future-traffic test, features, graph edges, stock, and user history must all predate each query. A timestamp on the label alone is insufficient if the features contain future evidence.

    For click-derived labels, “not clicked” can mean “not seen.” Preserve exposure information and read the click-bias chapter before turning logs into relevance targets. For an LLM judge, freeze its prompt, model/version, rubric, and response parser, and audit it against independent human judgments.

    Check your understanding: A new retriever finds many unjudged documents. Is its low nDCG enough to reject it?

    No. First inspect judged coverage and label a representative pool of the new results. Keep the original evaluation for reproducibility, then compare systems under the expanded judgments.
