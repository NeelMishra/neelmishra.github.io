    nDCG: How Much Does the Order of Useful Results Matter?

    September 2026 · 15 min read

    Before you start: precision, recall, AP and reciprocal rank. Those metrics collapse relevance to yes/no; this chapter preserves graded differences between exact matches and partial matches.

    Suppose two useful documents swap places on page one: a definitive, exact-match answer drops to rank 3 while a tangentially helpful page rises to rank 1. Binary Precision@3, Recall@3, and even binary AP@3 cannot tell the difference—both documents have binary relevance $1$. Normalized Discounted Cumulative Gain ($\operatorname{nDCG}$, Järvelin & Kekäläinen 2002) solves both halves of that problem: higher relevance grades earn larger gains, and lower screen positions suffer logarithmic discounts.

    We begin with a compact four-document catalog—A (grade 3), B (grade 2), C (grade 1), and D (grade 0)—returned in order B, D, A, C and evaluated at cutoff $K = 3$, then also show the complete 5-slot step-by-step calculation for our six-document series benchmark in retrieval_metrics.py.

    Turn a grade into credit, then discount by rank position

    Before writing the $\operatorname{DCG}@K$ equation, define every symbol explicitly:

    • $K \ge 1$: the integer rank cutoff (number of top positions evaluated).
    • $i \in \{1, 2, \dots, K\}$: the 1-based rank position in the returned list.
    • $r_i \in \{0, 1, 2, 3, \dots\}$: the non-negative relevance grade assigned under the declared rubric to the document placed at rank $i$.
    • $g(r) \ge 0$: the monotonic gain function converting grade $r$ into raw utility units—most commonly exponential gain $g_{\text{exp}}(r) = 2^r - 1$ (mapping grades $0, 1, 2, 3 \mapsto 0, 1, 3, 7$) or linear gain $g_{\text{lin}}(r) = r$.
    • $1 / \log_2(i + 1) \in (0, 1]$: the logarithmic rank discount at position $i$, equal to $1 / \log_2 2 = 1.0000$ at rank $1$, $1 / \log_2 3 \approx 0.6309$ at rank $2$, $1 / \log_2 4 = 0.5000$ at rank $3$, and $1 / \log_2 5 \approx 0.4307$ at rank $4$.

    Summing discounted gains across the top $K$ positions defines Discounted Cumulative Gain ($\operatorname{DCG}@K$):

    $$\operatorname{DCG}@K = \sum_{i=1}^{K} \frac{g(r_i)}{\log_2(i + 1)}.$$

    Normalize against the ideal top-$K$ from the full eligible catalog

    Raw $\operatorname{DCG}@K$ depends on how many high-grade documents exist for a query: a query with four grade-3 answers naturally achieves a much larger $\operatorname{DCG}$ than a query with a single grade-1 answer. To put queries on a common $[0, 1]$ scale, sort all eligible judged documents in the catalog in non-increasing order of relevance grade $r_1^* \ge r_2^* \ge \dots \ge r_K^*$ (padding with $0$ if fewer than $K$ positive documents exist) and compute Ideal DCG ($\operatorname{IDCG}@K$) and Normalized DCG ($\operatorname{nDCG}@K$):

    $$\operatorname{IDCG}@K = \sum_{i=1}^{K} \frac{g(r_i^*)}{\log_2(i + 1)}, \qquad \operatorname{nDCG}@K = \frac{\operatorname{DCG}@K}{\operatorname{IDCG}@K}.$$

    Never build $\operatorname{IDCG}$ solely from returned items

    Click Miss A at K=3 above so the top-3 returned list becomes $[\text{B}, \text{C}, \text{D}]$ (omitting grade-3 A completely). Passing only those three returned grades $[2, 1, 0]$ to ndcg_score computes an internal-order denominator of $3 + 1/\log_2 3 \approx 3.6309$ and awards a deceptive score of $1.0000$! Always compute $\operatorname{IDCG}@K$ over the full eligible judged catalog ($9.3928$, giving the honest $\operatorname{nDCG}@3 = 3.6309 / 9.3928 \approx 0.3866$):

    Handle empty ideals, library gain conventions, and tied scores

    • Zero-ideal queries ($\operatorname{IDCG}@K = 0$): if every eligible document has grade $0$, $0 / 0$ is undefined. retrieval_metrics.py returns None and logs the excluded query count rather than silently injecting zeros or ones.
    • Library gain conventions differ! Both NIST trec_eval and scikit-learn’s ndcg_score(y_true, y_score) use the supplied numbers linearly as gains unless you explicitly transform 2.0 ** grades - 1 beforehand.
    • Tied model scores: scikit-learn averages across tied-score permutations by default unless ignore_ties=True; production UIs display one deterministic order, so break ties deterministically before scoring.
    Python: make the gain mapping explicit in scikit-learn

    Columns correspond to all four eligible catalog items A, B, C, D (never drop C even when $K=3$, or ndcg_score cannot see the full catalog ideal):

    import numpy as np
from sklearn.metrics import dcg_score, ndcg_score

grades = np.array([[3, 2, 1, 0]])  # columns A, B, C, D
scores = np.array([[2, 4, 1, 3]])  # order B, D, A, C; no ties
for name, gains in [("exponential", 2.0 ** grades - 1), ("linear", grades)]:
    actual = dcg_score(gains, scores, k=3)
    normalized = ndcg_score(gains, scores, k=3)
    print(name, round(actual, 4), round(normalized, 4))
# exponential 6.5 0.692
# linear      3.5 0.735

    What nDCG still cannot see

    Because $\operatorname{DCG}@K$ sums each slot independently, ten near-duplicate copies of the same grade-2 product earn the exact same $\operatorname{nDCG}@10$ as ten diverse grade-2 options. Pair $\operatorname{nDCG}@K$ with duplicate/subtopic coverage metrics when result diversity matters, and proceed to Chapter 4 to separate Stage-1 candidate ceiling losses from Stage-2 reranking errors.
