    Search & Retrieval: Reading Guide

    September 2026 · 10 min read

    A vector index can return its exact nearest neighbors in two milliseconds while delivering poor results to the user. Why? Because three distinct subsystems share responsibility for every search page or RAG context window: the representation defines what “near” means in coordinate space; candidate retrieval approximates that search across millions of documents under strict latency and filter constraints; and reranking + task evaluation turns candidate IDs into ordered answers or grounded citations. Separating those boundaries is the single highest-leverage habit in retrieval engineering.

    This guide connects two foundational chapters on approximate nearest-neighbor (ANN) mechanics and benchmarking with a seven-chapter series on search and RAG evaluation. If $\ell_2$ vector normalization and feature scaling are new, review the normalization notes first.

    Two-stage retrieval, reranking, and evaluation architecture

    Modern production search systems rarely score every catalog item with their heaviest model. To see why—and where quality can leak—define the cascade sets for a query $q$:

    • $\mathcal{C}$: the eligible catalog of $|\mathcal{C}| = N$ documents or items (often $10^6$–$10^9$).
    • $R(q) \subseteq \mathcal{C}$: the ground-truth set of task-relevant items for query $q$ ($|R(q)| \ge 1$).
    • $C_M(q) \subset \mathcal{C}$: the Stage-1 candidate pool of size $M \ll N$ (typically $M \in [100, 1000]$) retrieved in milliseconds via lexical inverted indexes (BM25) and/or dense ANN search (HNSW, IVF-PQ).
    • $L_K(q) \subseteq C_M(q)$: the final ordered top-$K$ list ($K \ll M$, typically $K \in [5, 20]$) produced after Stage-2 cross-encoder or gradient-boosted learning-to-rank (LTR) scoring.

    Because Stage 2 only scores items present in $C_M(q)$, every retrieval cascade obeys the Stage-1 recall ceiling inequality:

    $$|L_K(q) \cap R(q)| \le |C_M(q) \cap R(q)| \implies \operatorname{Recall}@K\bigl(L_K(q)\bigr) \le \operatorname{Recall}@M\bigl(C_M(q)\bigr).$$

    No reranker—no matter how large its Transformer cross-attention—can promote a relevant document that Stage-1 retrieval or over-aggressive metadata filtering already dropped.

    Keep two references strictly distinct throughout your experiments: exact vector neighbors (defined purely by embeddings and distance metric, measuring index approximation error) versus human relevance judgments (defined by the user’s information need, measuring task utility).

    Continue from neighbor agreement to search quality

    The Search & Retrieval Evaluation Guide organizes seven focused chapters around specific engineering claims:

    • Define relevance and build the test collection before treating any metric number as ground truth.
    • Binary ranking metrics (P@K, Recall@K, MAP, MRR) and graded ranking utility (nDCG) with worked step-by-step calculations.
    • Stage-by-stage retrieval vs reranking diagnostics and paired bootstrap uncertainty.
    • Position-biased clicks, IPS, and online A/B experiments plus end-to-end RAG evidence, claim, and citation auditing.

    Build one small, auditable experiment before scaling up

    Start on a miniature slice where you can inspect raw distances, filter predicates, and exact top-$K$ IDs directly. Log corpus snapshot IDs, embedding model versions, normalization flags, filter predicates, index hyperparameters, hardware threads, and per-query latencies (including empty or error responses) so every aggregate number traces cleanly back to individual queries.

    For hands-on code, pair the exact-reference benchmark snippet with the zero-dependency standard-library ranking metric lab (retrieval_metrics.py).
