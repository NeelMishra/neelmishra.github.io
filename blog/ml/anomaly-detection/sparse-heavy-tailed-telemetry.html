    Model Sparse, Heavy-Tailed Telemetry Without Flagging Every Large Customer

    September 2026 · 12 min read

    Before you start: Isolation Forest draws uniform random splits across the feature coordinates you supply. Its score is only as meaningful as that representation.

    Suppose an application emits $2{,}000$ events in a large tenant ($1{,}000$ active users) and $60$ events in a small tenant ($10$ active users). Ranking raw counts flags the large tenant solely for its size. Dividing by active-user exposure reverses the picture: the large tenant is at its ordinary baseline ($2$ events/user), while the small tenant is at $3\times$ that rate ($6$ events/user).

    The row defines the behavior you can detect

    Let $c_t$ be the seven-day event count and $n_t$ the active-user exposure count for tenant $t$ under a fixed application. Aggregating to one row per application pools numerators and denominators across all tenants, whereas one row per app–tenant pair preserves the local rate:

    $$r_{\text{app}} = \frac{\sum_t c_t}{\sum_t n_t} = \frac{2000 + 60 + 0}{1000 + 10 + 1} = \frac{2060}{1011} \approx \mathbf{2.0376}, \qquad r_t = \frac{c_t}{n_t}$$

    Against a peer baseline rate of $r_0 = 2.0$ events per active user, the pooled app rate $r_{\text{app}} \approx 2.04$ ($1.02\times r_0$) masks the small tenant's local spike $r_{\text{small}} = \frac{60}{10} = \mathbf{6.0}$ ($3.0\times r_0$) beneath the large tenant's volume ($r_{\text{large}} = \frac{2000}{1000} = 2.0$).

    Stabilize low-exposure rates with prior smoothing

    When the exposure denominator $n$ is tiny, raw rates $\hat{r} = c / n$ swing wildly from a handful of events (or become $0/0$ when $n = 0$). Empirical-Bayes (prior) smoothing blends the observed counts with $k$ prior exposures at the peer reference rate $r_0$:

    $$r_{\mathrm{smooth}} = \frac{c + k r_0}{n + k} = \underbrace{\frac{n}{n + k}}_{w}\hat{r} + \underbrace{\frac{k}{n + k}}_{1 - w} r_0$$

    where:

    • $c$ is the observed event count and $n$ is the observed exposure count in the window,
    • $r_0$ is the peer baseline rate ($r_0 = 2$) and $k$ is the prior strength measured in exposure units,
    • $w = \frac{n}{n+k}$ is the share of weight placed on the observed rate $\hat{r} = c/n$.

    A log transform changes the random-cut geometry

    For nonnegative counts, $\operatorname{log1p}(x) = \ln(1 + x)$ maps $0 \mapsto 0$ and compresses heavy right tails. Although $\operatorname{log1p}$ is strictly increasing and preserves the sort order of $[1, 2, 3, 4, 20]$, it changes the interval lengths from which Isolation Forest draws uniform splits.

    Let $u \in (0, 1)$ be a uniform draw between a node's minimum $x_{\min} = 1$ and maximum $x_{\max} = 20$. In raw coordinates, the span is $[1, 20]$; in $\operatorname{log1p}$ coordinates, the span is $[\ln 2, \ln 21]$. Mapping both cuts back to original units gives:

    $$\begin{aligned} q_{\text{raw}}(u) &= x_{\min} + u(x_{\max} - x_{\min}) = 1 + 19u, \\ q_{\log}(u) &= \exp\!\Bigl(\ln 2 + u\bigl(\ln 21 - \ln 2\bigr)\Bigr) - 1 = 2\left(\frac{21}{2}\right)^u - 1. \end{aligned}$$

    Consequently, the probability that the first root cut falls above $4$ and immediately isolates $20$ drops from:

    $$P_{\text{raw}}(\text{isolate } 20) = \frac{20 - 4}{20 - 1} = \frac{16}{19} \approx \mathbf{84.2\%} \quad\longrightarrow\quad P_{\log}(\text{isolate } 20) = \frac{\ln 21 - \ln 5}{\ln 21 - \ln 2} = \frac{\ln(21/5)}{\ln(21/2)} \approx \mathbf{61.0\%}$$

    Separate observed zeros from collection gaps and ineligibility

    A zero in a feature table can represent four distinct states depending on eligibility, collector coverage, and exposure:
