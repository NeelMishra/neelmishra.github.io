    Turn Anomaly Scores into an Investigation Queue

    September 2026 · 15 min read

    Before you start: Isolation scores and the meaning of each telemetry row.

    A detector assigns scores to observations. A reviewer handles cases. Between those two steps, the system must decide which rows qualify, merge repeated evidence, apply a workload limit, and preserve the reason each case entered the queue.

    The queue examples below use invented scores on a common higher-is-more-unusual scale. They are separate from chapter 1’s fitted forest, which we reuse later to examine explanation units. No high score is automatically a confirmed harmful outcome.

    A score cutoff and a capacity limit answer different questions

    A fixed score cutoff admits every eligible row at or above that score. Top K admits the K highest-scoring rows, if that many exist. A fixed percentage admits a share of traffic. A combined policy can require a minimum score while limiting the number admitted.

    At cutoff 0.70, the quiet day admits two rows, the busy day eight, and the lower-score day none. Top 3 fills three places on all three days. That controls volume but can admit weak evidence on the lower-score day. The combined rule admits 2, 3, and 0: capacity is a maximum, not a requirement to fill every slot.

    A fixed share is a workload convention; it does not estimate the fraction of real anomalies. Select and validate the rule on earlier resolved cohorts, then freeze it for the later comparison. The graph-risk threshold exercise shows how a validation-selected rule can miss its precision target on test.

    Measure arrivals as well as service capacity. If eight new cases arrive per day and reviewers resolve three, a queue that admits all eight grows by five per day before other changes. A daily cap limits new admissions, but unresolved cases can still accumulate. Report backlog age, time to first review, and resolution time alongside admission counts.

    Apply the budget to the unit reviewers actually handle

    On the busy day, the top three rows all have case key K1. Imagine repeated scoring of the same app–tenant pair within one grouping window. Taking three rows and then merging them yields just one case. Grouping first can surface K1, K2, and K3 within a three-case budget.

    Keep all underlying members when a case is admitted. Merging should not erase event evidence or make a later score unreproducible. Choose keys and grouping windows that match investigation work; a coarse key may merge separate incidents, while a narrow key may flood the queue with repetitions. A maximum score is a chosen ranking rule, not the probability that a group contains harmful behavior.

    A small group-before-budget implementation

    This function implements the figure’s group-first rule. Its optional minimum score applies to each group’s highest-scoring member. Download the synthetic rows and explanation data to reproduce the examples.

    def select_cases(rows, budget, minimum_score=None):
    # Prevalidated rows: unique IDs, finite scores, and known grouping keys.
    # Scores use one common, higher-is-more-unusual scale.
    ranked = sorted(rows, key=lambda row: (-row["score"], row["id"]))
    groups = {}
    for row in ranked:
        groups.setdefault(row["group"], []).append(row)
    cases = []
    for key, members in groups.items():
        representative = members[0]  # Highest score, then lowest ID.
        if minimum_score is None or representative["score"] >= minimum_score:
            cases.append({"group": key, "score": representative["score"],
                          "representative": representative["id"],
                          "members": [row["id"] for row in members]})
    # Dict insertion order follows ranked representatives.
    return cases[:budget]  # budget is a nonnegative integer case count.

    Grouping keys must be computable at the decision time. Future confirmed incident IDs can be useful evaluation groups, but using those future conclusions to build the historical admission queue would leak information.

    Measure queue quality with the right denominator

    Define the positive outcome before calculating a metric. Is it a confirmed harmful application, a policy violation, or a case with at least one actionable finding? The detector’s own predict = −1 output is a model decision, not a ground-truth label.

    If the budget counts cases, report case-level precision using a declared case-outcome rule. If another report counts individual app–tenant rows, keep that denominator explicit. Three duplicate positive rows in one investigation are not three separate recovered incidents.

    Reviewed alerts can estimate resolved queue precision under the review protocol. They cannot alone identify how much risk remains outside the queue. Use the outside-queue audit example for the population estimate, and report unresolved outcomes separately. Compare models after comparable label-maturation windows; an unreviewed or pending case is not a confirmed negative.

    Reviewer effort can differ across cases. Alongside precision and findings, report time per case, duplicates merged, and useful findings per reviewer-hour. A queue that catches more true cases but consumes far more investigation time needs that cost made visible.

    Show the behavior, reference, and reason for admission

    A compact case explanation should let a reviewer inspect the evidence without decoding the feature pipeline. For the small tenant in chapter 2, that means “60 events from 10 active users over seven days; observed rate 6 versus reference rate 2; collection complete.” Name the peer population and link the underlying event records.

    Separate that behavioral description from the admission reason: “highest score among this case’s members; ranked third after grouping; admitted under the three-case daily cap.” A score, a peer percentile, and a feature attribution are different quantities. Each needs its own units and reference.

    An additive explanation must name its output

    Some Isolation Forest explainers operate on corrected path length. The IsoTree adapter in SHAP’s source assigns corrected path lengths to tree leaves. A negative contribution in those units shortens the path, which raises the anomaly score. Labeling that negative number “less risk” would reverse its meaning.

    We can show this exactly with chapter 1’s one-feature forest. Choose the five training observations as an equally weighted reference population. Their mean corrected path is 2.8. Query 20’s path is 1.3333, so its single feature contributes 1.3333 − 2.8 = −1.4667 path units. There is only one feature, so the whole difference belongs to it.

    For this single-feature game, the attribution is the output minus its reference mean, consistent with the additive explanation defined by Lundberg and Lee. The score-space reference is about 0.4595, and query 20’s score-space contribution is about +0.2127. Those numbers differ from exponentiating the path reference and path contribution separately.

    To recover the anomaly score from a path-length explanation, first add the path reference and all path contributions, then apply the forest’s path-to-score transform to the complete sum. A linear negation would reverse contribution signs, but the exponential transform is nonlinear. A score-space explanation requires an explanation of that transformed output.

    With multiple features, reference choice and how missing features are modeled also matter. The TreeSHAP validation notes and TreeExplainer documentation explain the output and reconstruction checks. An attribution explains a model calculation; it does not establish the cause of the observed behavior.

    Preserve the decision before learning from its outcome

    Save the score, admission rule, grouping result, feature snapshot, and evidence as they existed when the case entered the queue. Append timestamped review outcomes rather than overwriting the original decision record. A finding learned tomorrow can inform a later model or score; it must not silently improve yesterday’s replay.

    Track outcome categories such as confirmed positive, confirmed benign under the chosen definition, insufficient evidence, and pending. A case dismissed because the team ran out of time is not a reliable negative training label. The admission policy selected which cases were reviewed, so preserve audit samples and their sampling probabilities when using feedback for evaluation or training.

    Monitor concentration by tenant, peer group, collection state, and grouping key. A sudden wave may reflect a real incident, a telemetry outage, a changed population, or a new admission rule. Compare saved inputs and policy versions before interpreting a score trend as a change in risk.

    This schema illustrates the busy-day example, not a complete production contract. Store actual window boundaries and decision times, collection coverage, explanation output units and reference version, and the timestamped outcome history. The record should support both a reviewer’s next action and a faithful later evaluation.

    References and further reading

    • Lundberg & Lee: A Unified Approach to Interpreting Model Predictions — Additive feature attributions, the reference output, and local reconstruction.
    • SHAP tree-explainer source: IsoTree — The Isolation Forest adapter’s corrected path-length representation; inspect the version actually used.
    • SHAP: TreeExplainer — Output-space and background semantics. A reconstruction check must compare the same output on the same rows.
