    Turn Anomaly Scores into an Investigation Queue

    September 2026 · 5 min read

    A detector ranks unusual cases. A review team still has to decide which cases to investigate, what evidence to show, and how to judge whether the queue is useful. Start with the number of cases reviewers can actually handle.

    Choose a score cutoff or a review budget

    PolicyMeaningWhat can change
    Fixed score cutoffReview every score above a chosen level.Queue size rises or falls with the score distribution.
    Top 200 per dayReview the highest-ranked 200 cases.The lowest admitted score changes with the day’s population.
    Top 1%Review a fixed share of cases.The number reviewed changes with traffic volume.

    A fixed share does not estimate the true anomaly rate. Replay candidate policies on later windows and measure reviewer workload. Group related cases into one investigation while retaining their individual scores and evidence.

    Know what precision and recall count

    Precision asks what fraction of flagged cases are confirmed positives. Recall asks what fraction of all positives were flagged. In this teaching dataset, five of ten cases are positive. A cutoff of 0.70 selects four cases, three of them positive:

    Precision = 3 / 4 = 75%

    Recall = 3 / 5 = 60%. The two denominators answer different questions.

    In a real queue, labels outside the reviewed set are usually incomplete. Reviewing only flagged cases can estimate queue precision once outcomes are resolved, but cannot reveal population recall. Sample cases outside the queue as well, and account for their sampling probabilities when estimating population quantities.

    Keep unresolved cases separate from confirmed negatives. Compare cohorts after similar investigation time so slower-to-resolve cases do not look like model errors prematurely.

    Connect the score to inspectable evidence

    A useful case shows the raw behavior, its comparison population, and supporting records. For example: “40 events from 20 active users in seven days; event counts are unusual relative to this application’s named peer group.” The rate, percentile, and model contribution are different pieces of evidence.

    If you attach SHAP values, name the output they explain. For Isolation Forest, an explainer may represent path length rather than your final anomaly score. Negation reverses contribution signs; the nonlinear path-to-score transform cannot be applied independently to every contribution. Check reconstruction in the explainer’s own output units; the TreeSHAP validation notes explain the check.

    Preserve what was known at review time

    Save model and feature versions, the policy that admitted the case, evidence links, and timestamped outcomes. Future training can use investigator feedback, but remember that the queue selected which cases were labeled. Keep the outside-queue audits too.

    Watch for concentration in one tenant or missing-data state. A surge may reflect an incident or a collection change; inspect raw evidence before interpreting the score trend.

    Keep one compact case record

    case = {
    "entity": ["app-A", "tenant-7"],
    "model_version": "iforest-v3",
    "score_name": "negative_score_samples",
    "score": 0.63,
    "queue_policy": "top-200-per-day-v2",
    "feature_snapshot": "features-v8",
    "evidence_ids": ["event-group-41"],
    "outcome": "unresolved",
}  # Illustrative schema; store explanation units separately.
