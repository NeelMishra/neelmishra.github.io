    Unsupervised Clustering: K-Means Objective, Lloyd's Algorithm, K-Means++ & Failure Modes

    November 2026 · 14 min read

    Every supervised classifier we have built so far—from logistic regression and KNN to Support Vector Machines—starts with ground-truth target labels $y_i$. In customer behavior logs, single-cell RNA-seq expression matrices, satellite imagery, and document archives, no teacher provides $y_i$: you only receive an unlabeled cloud of feature vectors $\mathcal D = \{x_1, x_2, \ldots, x_n\} \subset \mathbb R^d$ and must ask what natural groups hide inside the geometry.

    Clustering is not a single algorithm because "a natural group" means fundamentally different things depending on domain geometry. Before diving into $K$-Means, let us map the four classical clustering families covered across this 4-part series so you always know which inductive bias you are buying.

    1. The WCSS Objective & Why Lloyd's Algorithm Always Terminates

    Suppose we want to partition $n$ observations $x_1, \ldots, x_n \in \mathbb R^d$ into $K$ disjoint clusters $C_1, \ldots, C_K$, summarizing each cluster $C_k$ by a representative vector (centroid) $\mu_k \in \mathbb R^d$. Let $r_{ik} \in \{0, 1\}$ be a binary one-hot assignment indicator equal to $1$ if point $x_i$ belongs to cluster $k$ and $0$ otherwise (with $\sum_{k=1}^K r_{ik} = 1$ for every $i$). $K$-Means minimizes the Within-Cluster Sum of Squares (WCSS)—called inertia_ in scikit-learn—which measures total squared Euclidean quantization error:

    $$J(r, \mu) = \sum_{i=1}^n \sum_{k=1}^K r_{ik}\,\|x_i - \mu_k\|_2^2 = \sum_{k=1}^K \sum_{i \in C_k} \|x_i - \mu_k\|_2^2.$$

    Optimizing $J(r, \mu)$ jointly over discrete binary indicators $r \in \{0, 1\}^{n \times K}$ and continuous vectors $\mu \in \mathbb R^{K \times d}$ is NP-hard even for $K = 2$ in general dimension $d$. Instead, Lloyd's algorithm (1957/1982) performs block coordinate descent, alternating between holding $\mu$ fixed and holding $r$ fixed:

    1. Assignment Step (Optimize $r$ given fixed $\mu^{(t)}$): Since each point $x_i$ contributes a separate non-negative term $\sum_{k=1}^K r_{ik}\|x_i - \mu_k^{(t)}\|_2^2$, minimizing over one-hot $r_{i,\cdot}$ assigns $x_i$ to its nearest centroid (breaking exact ties deterministically by lowest cluster index $k$): $$r_{ik}^{(t+1)} = \begin{cases} 1 & \text{if } k = \arg\min_{1 \le j \le K} \|x_i - \mu_j^{(t)}\|_2^2, \\ 0 & \text{otherwise.} \end{cases}$$ Geometrically, this slices $\mathbb R^d$ into $K$ convex Voronoi cells bounded by perpendicular bisector hyperplanes.
    2. Update Step (Optimize $\mu$ given fixed $r^{(t+1)}$): With cluster memberships $C_k = \{i : r_{ik}^{(t+1)} = 1\}$ frozen (assuming $|C_k| \ge 1$), let $\bar x_k = \frac{1}{|C_k|}\sum_{i \in C_k} x_i$ be the sample mean. By the bias–variance (Huygens–Steiner) identity, for any candidate vector $\mu \in \mathbb R^d$: $$\sum_{i \in C_k} \|x_i - \mu\|_2^2 = \sum_{i \in C_k} \|(x_i - \bar x_k) + (\bar x_k - \mu)\|_2^2 = \sum_{i \in C_k} \|x_i - \bar x_k\|_2^2 + |C_k|\,\|\bar x_k - \mu\|_2^2,$$ because the cross-term $2(\bar x_k - \mu)^\top \sum_{i \in C_k}(x_i - \bar x_k) = 0$ vanishes identically! That second term $|C_k|\,\|\bar x_k - \mu\|_2^2$ is uniquely minimized at zero when $\mu_k^{(t+1)} = \bar x_k$: $$\mu_k^{(t+1)} = \frac{\sum_{i=1}^n r_{ik}^{(t+1)} x_i}{\sum_{i=1}^n r_{ik}^{(t+1)}}.$$

    Monotonicity Proof vs. The Local Minimum Trap

    Combining both steps yields a non-increasing chain bounded below by zero:

    $$J\bigl(r^{(t)}, \mu^{(t)}\bigr) \;\ge\; J\bigl(r^{(t+1)}, \mu^{(t)}\bigr) \;\ge\; J\bigl(r^{(t+1)}, \mu^{(t+1)}\bigr) \;\ge\; 0.$$

    Because there are at most $K^n$ distinct partitions of $n$ points into $K$ clusters—and under deterministic tie-breaking, a partition $r$ uniquely determines $\mu(r)$, so no strictly lower-cost step can ever revisit an old partition—Lloyd's algorithm is guaranteed to halt in a finite number of iterations (costing $O(n K d)$ per iteration).

    Does finite termination mean we found the global minimum $J^*$? Not at all! Place four points at the corners of a tall $2 \times 10$ rectangle: $(0, 0), (2, 0), (0, 10), (2, 10)$ with $K = 2$. Initializing near the bottom and top pairs yields $\mu_1^* = (1, 0), \mu_2^* = (1, 10)$ with global optimum $J^* = 1^2 + 1^2 + 1^2 + 1^2 = \mathbf{4.0}$. Initializing on the left and right edges at $\mu_1^{(0)} = (0, 5), \mu_2^{(0)} = (2, 5)$ assigns $\{(0,0), (0,10)\}$ to $\mu_1^{(0)}$ and $\{(2,0), (2,10)\}$ to $\mu_2^{(0)}$, whose means are already $(0, 5)$ and $(2, 5)$—freezing immediately at a stable fixed point with $J = 5^2 + 5^2 + 5^2 + 5^2 = \mathbf{100.0}$ ($25\times$ worse than optimal!).

    2. K-Means++ $D(x)^2$ Seeding & Worked 5-Point Trace

    Uniformly sampling $K$ random points as initial centers fails whenever two seeds land inside the same dense cluster while a distant cluster receives zero seeds. Arthur and Vassilvitskii (2007) introduced $K$-Means++, which spreads out the $K$ initial centers sequentially before running standard Lloyd updates:

    1. Pick the first center $c_1$ uniformly at random from $\{x_1, \ldots, x_n\}$.
    2. For $m = 2, \ldots, K$: for every point $x_i$, compute its Euclidean distance to the closest center chosen so far, $D(x_i) = \min_{1 \le j < m} \|x_i - c_j\|_2$, and sample the next center $c_m = x_i$ with probability proportional to squared distance: $$P(c_m = x_i) = \frac{D(x_i)^2}{\sum_{q=1}^n D(x_q)^2}.$$

    Why $D(x)^2$ rather than $D(x)$ or deterministic farthest-first traversal? Deterministic farthest-first gets hijacked by single extreme outliers, whereas $D(x)^2$ weights each unrepresented cluster by its total WCSS contribution—yielding the famous theoretical guarantee $\mathbb E[J] \le 8(\ln K + 2)\,J_{\text{opt}}$ ($O(\log K)$-competitive even in the worst case!).

    Worked $K$-Means++ seeding probabilities for $K = 3$ on five 2D points, starting at $c_1 = A = (0, 0)$ and drawing $c_2 = E = (8, 6)$.

    $A$$(0, 0)$ $0^2 + 0^2 = 0$$0 / 200 = \mathbf{0.00}$ $100$$\min(0, 100) = 0$$0 / 60 = \mathbf{0.0000}$
    $B$$(0, 2)$ $0^2 + 2^2 = 4$$4 / 200 = \mathbf{0.02}$ $80$$\min(4, 80) = 4$$4 / 60 \approx \mathbf{0.0667}$
    $C$$(4, 4)$ $4^2 + 4^2 = 32$$32 / 200 = \mathbf{0.16}$ $20$$\min(32, 20) = 20$$20 / 60 \approx \mathbf{0.3333}$
    $D$$(8, 0)$ $8^2 + 0^2 = 64$$64 / 200 = \mathbf{0.32}$ $36$$\min(64, 36) = 36$$36 / 60 = \mathbf{0.6000}$

    Follow how cleanly the distribution adapts: once $c_1 = A = (0, 0)$ is picked, its close neighbor $B = (0, 2)$ gets only a $2\%$ chance of being duplicated while distant $E = (8, 6)$ gets $50\%$. Once $c_2 = E = (8, 6)$ is drawn, $E$'s probability drops immediately to $0$, and the remaining unrepresented corner $D = (8, 0)$ jumps to the highest selection probability ($60\%$)!

    3. Structural Assumptions, Geometric Failure Modes & Practical Variants

    Because $K$-Means assigns every point using unweighted isotropic Euclidean distance $\|x_i - \mu_k\|_2^2$, it silently bakes three rigid geometric assumptions into your data. Violating any of them breaks $K$-Means even at the exact global minimum of $J$:

    1. Non-Convex Shapes (Concentric Rings & Moons): Voronoi cells are intersections of half-spaces and are therefore always convex polyhedra. Given an inner bullseye inside an outer ring, $K=2$ slices straight across the ring like cutting a pizza in half! (Fix: DBSCAN or Spectral Clustering.)
    2. Anisotropic / Elongated Diagonal Clusters: Suppose two parallel cigar-shaped clusters are tilted at $45^\circ$. Points at the tips of Cigar 1 are closer in raw Euclidean distance to the center of Cigar 2 than to their own center, so $K$-Means chops both cigars transversely! (Fix: Gaussian Mixture Models with full covariance $\Sigma_k$.)
    3. Unequal Cluster Radii, Densities, or Heavy Outliers: Because WCSS sums unweighted $\|x_i - \mu_k\|_2^2$ across all points, a large diffuse cloud splits or donates its fringe points to a nearby compact cluster, and a single extreme outlier squares its residual $\|x_{\text{out}} - \mu_k\|_2^2$, dragging $\mu_k$ far off center.

    Two Workhorse Relatives: Mini-Batch K-Means & K-Medoids (PAM)

    • Mini-Batch $K$-Means (Sculley, 2010): When $n = 10^7$, scanning all $n$ rows per iteration is slow. MiniBatchKMeans samples a tiny batch $B$ of size $b \approx 1{,}024$ at each step, assigns only $x_i \in B$, and updates each centroid via an online running average $\mu_k \leftarrow (1 - \eta_k)\mu_k + \eta_k x_i$ with $\eta_k = 1 / c_k$, achieving near-identical WCSS in orders of magnitude less wall-clock time.
    • $K$-Medoids / Partitioning Around Medoids (PAM): Replaces the continuous mean $\mu_k \in \mathbb R^d$ with an actual training observation $m_k \in \{x_1, \ldots, x_n\}$ and minimizes unsquared distance $\sum_{k=1}^K \sum_{i \in C_k} d(x_i, m_k)$. Like the median vs. the mean, $K$-Medoids resists heavy outliers and works with arbitrary dissimilarity matrices (e.g., edit distance, cosine, or Manhattan $L_1$).
    Show the complete Python verification 
    import numpy as np
from sklearn.cluster import KMeans

# 1. Verify exact 5-point K-Means++ seeding probabilities (K = 3)
pts = np.array([[0., 0.], [0., 2.], [4., 4.], [8., 0.], [8., 6.]])
d2_round1 = np.sum((pts - pts[0]) ** 2, axis=1)
p_round1 = d2_round1 / np.sum(d2_round1)
print("Round 1 D(x)^2:", d2_round1, "-> probs:", p_round1)
# [0. 4. 32. 64. 100.] -> [0.   0.02 0.16 0.32 0.5 ]

d2_to_E = np.sum((pts - pts[4]) ** 2, axis=1)
d2_round2 = np.minimum(d2_round1, d2_to_E)
p_round2 = d2_round2 / np.sum(d2_round2)
print("Round 2 D(x)^2:", d2_round2, "-> probs:", np.round(p_round2, 4))
# [0. 4. 20. 36. 0.]   -> [0.     0.0667 0.3333 0.6    0.    ]

# 2. Verify the 25x local-minimum trap on the 2 x 10 rectangle
X_rect = np.array([[0., 0.], [2., 0.], [0., 10.], [2., 10.]])
km_global = KMeans(n_clusters=2, init=np.array([[1., 0.], [1., 10.]]), n_init=1).fit(X_rect)
km_trapped = KMeans(n_clusters=2, init=np.array([[0., 5.], [2., 5.]]), n_init=1).fit(X_rect)

print("Global optimum WCSS :", km_global.inertia_)   # 4.0
print("Trapped minimum WCSS:", km_trapped.inertia_)  # 100.0
assert np.isclose(km_global.inertia_, 4.0) and np.isclose(km_trapped.inertia_, 100.0)

    Two big limitations of $K$-Means stand out: every boundary point gets a rigid $100\%$-or-$0\%$ assignment with zero uncertainty estimate, and spherical distance $\|x_i - \mu_k\|_2^2$ cannot stretch or tilt to match elongated elliptical clusters. In Part 2, we upgrade $K$-Means into a full generative probability model—Gaussian Mixture Models (GMMs) trained via Expectation–Maximization (EM)—and prove that $K$-Means is literally the zero-variance limit of a GMM!
