    Evaluate Graph Risk at a Fixed Precision and a Realistic Time Cutoff

    September 2026 · 4 min read

    Before you start: Neighborhood features and classification metrics.

    “Recall improved” is incomplete when false positives carry a real investigation cost. A useful risk-model comparison asks how much more confirmed risk a model finds while preserving an acceptable precision and review budget. Graphs add another challenge: related entities can leak across ordinary random splits.

    Calculate the operating point

    Suppose a test set contains 100 confirmed risky applications. At a fixed deployed threshold, model A flags 90 applications: 81 are risky and 9 are benign. Precision is 81/90=90%; recall is 81/100=81%. Model B flags 100 applications with 90 risky and 10 benign: precision remains 90%, recall becomes 90%, and review volume rises by ten.

    These are synthetic counts. The example shows why a recall gain and review cost must be reported together. A precision–recall curve can compare potential operating points descriptively, but a deployment estimate should use a threshold selected on validation data and frozen before test. Finite samples may yield test precision below the target even if validation precision reached it; show intervals and sample counts.

    What does the test actually generalize to?

    Report more than one view when deployment contains both familiar neighborhoods and new entities.
    SplitQuestion answeredMain caveat
    Random applicationsCan the model predict held-out nodes in a similar graph?Related publishers or incidents may cross the boundary
    Publisher/incident groupsCan it generalize beyond known groups?May be harder than routine deployment; define grouping carefully
    Forward in timeCan it score future decisions with past evidence?All edges, features, and labels need historical availability
    New-entity sliceHow does it handle unseen or isolated applications?Neighborhood evidence may be missing entirely

    A transductive setup may legitimately observe unlabeled test-node connections if those connections are available at inference. It must not use test labels to build features or choose the model. An inductive setup asks about new nodes or graphs. State which setting the experiment uses rather than calling all graph access leakage.

    Prove what the graph contributes

    Compare intrinsic-only features, graph-only features, their combination, and relation-specific ablations. Remove publisher edges, generic infrastructure edges, and recent edges one group at a time. Compare one-hop aggregation with deeper propagation. Inspect whether the gain is concentrated in an already heavily investigated incident rather than spread across future cases.

    Use degree buckets and evidence-age slices. A model can improve on dense neighborhoods while worsening on isolated apps. It can also benefit from unusually fresh investigator labels that will arrive too late in production. Delay labels in a replay experiment to match operational latency.

    Investigation creates selection bias

    If only flagged applications are reviewed, confirmed labels describe a selected population. Precision within the reviewed queue can be estimated, but total recall across all applications cannot be recovered from that queue alone without additional assumptions or sampling. An audit sample outside the queue helps estimate missed risk. Keep sampling probabilities when using weighted estimates.

    Distinguish “no finding,” “confirmed benign,” and “not investigated.” Delayed labels require a maturation window or a time-to-outcome treatment; counting all recent unresolved cases as benign can make a new model appear worse. The same principle applies to anomaly queues in the next series.

    Report changes at the right unit

    Applications sharing an incident or publisher have correlated outcomes. Resampling individual rows can understate uncertainty. Use group-aware estimates where the claim concerns performance on independent incidents or publishers, and report the number of groups as well as applications. A small number of large incidents should not masquerade as broad statistical evidence.

    The final report should include frozen threshold, precision and recall with denominators, flagged volume, label availability, split design, group counts, degree/new-entity slices, and latency. Add evidence examples for both recovered threats and new false positives. This connects the score to an operational decision that can be reviewed.

    Check your understanding: Can you estimate recall from labels collected only for the model’s alerts?

    Not without additional information about missed positives. You need a suitable audit sample, broader labels, or explicit assumptions. Alert precision and population recall require different denominators.
