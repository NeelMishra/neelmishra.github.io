    The textbook companion / Jurafsky & Martin

    Speech and Language Processing: A Visual Reading Guide

    September 2026 · Series guide · Based on the August 19, 2026 draft

    Learn how language becomes data, how models learn from that data, and how we check what they have learned. Follow the book with original worked examples, carefully labeled figures, and small experiments you can inspect.

    Start here

    The first six chapter companions are available below. The full roadmap covers all 26 numbered chapter slots and 11 appendices in the current source. Later companions are in preparation; Chapter 12 has not yet been written by the textbook authors, and their Chapter 10 is partial.

    This is an independent study companion to Daniel Jurafsky and James H. Martin’s Speech and Language Processing, third edition draft. Read the book alongside these explanations for its full treatment, exercises, research context, and references. The blogs use their own examples and figures rather than reproducing the book.

    Begin with these three questions

    1. 1. What does a language model predict?Follow one probability distribution into a generated sequence. Adjust temperature, distinguish prompting from training, and choose measurements that fit an application. Available · Chapter 1
    2. 2. What are the units being predicted?Inspect Unicode, define the vocabulary, trace byte-pair merges, and compute an edit distance. The choices made here change everything counted later. Available · Chapter 2
    3. 3. Can we build a language model from counts?Turn a tiny corpus into conditional probabilities, repair zero probabilities with smoothing, and work out what perplexity actually measures. Available · Chapter 3

    Continue from probabilities to learned representations

    1. 4. How does a classifier learn from text?Follow a message through word counts, a linear score, sigmoid, and a gradient update. Move the decision threshold while the model’s probabilities stay fixed. Available · Chapter 4
    2. 5. How do words acquire useful geometry?Construct context vectors, compare cosine with dot product, and train an observed word pair alongside two sampled negatives. Available · Chapter 5
    3. 6. How does an error change a hidden layer?Compute a full forward pass, follow all nine parameter gradients, and open or close a ReLU path in the gradient lab. Available · Chapter 6

    How the book fits together

    The opening volume supplies the main modeling tools. The second volume branches into retrieval, translation, speech, and other extensions. The third gives names and computational methods to the structures inside language: entities, syntax, roles, references, and conversation. These branches answer different questions, so do not treat every task as a synonym for text generation.

    A practical way to study each chapter

    Read the motivating problem before the equations. Then compute the smallest example yourself: one pair count, one conditional probability, one loss, or one step through an algorithm. Follow the figure’s arrows while saying what each object represents. If a symbol has no concrete meaning yet, return to the example before moving on.

    Use the expandable self-checks before looking at the answer. When a page has an interactive experiment, predict the effect of a control first. Afterwards, explain which quantities changed and which stayed fixed. That habit makes it easier to recognize when two implementations are solving different problems despite sharing a familiar name.

    The opening articles assume basic arithmetic. Later topics will call for vectors, derivatives, or probability; relevant foundations will be linked as needed. If you want a broader route through those tools, use the machine-learning prerequisite map.
