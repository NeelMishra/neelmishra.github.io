    01 / The prediction problem

    Language Models: From Next-Token Prediction to Useful Systems

    September 2026 · 16 min read · Companion to SLP 3, Chapter 1

    A language model makes one surprisingly reusable prediction: given the text so far, what could come next? To understand an assistant, first separate that prediction from the choices used to turn it into an answer.

    What you will be able to explain

    How a probability distribution becomes generated text; why training and prompting change different things; what embeddings contribute; and why a fluent response still needs task-specific evaluation. You only need fractions and the idea of a list of numbers.

    Reading companion to Jurafsky and Martin’s Chapter 1: Introduction, August 19, 2026 draft. The examples, illustrations, and interactive calculations below are original teaching material.

    The route through this article

    1. Start with a distribution, not a sentence

    Imagine the unfinished message “Please pour the”. Many continuations make sense: tea, water, or juice. A language model assigns a number to every possible next token. Those numbers are nonnegative and sum to one. They form a conditional probability distribution: conditional because the answer depends on the text already present.

    A token is an item in the model’s vocabulary. It may be a word, part of a word, punctuation, or a representation built from bytes. For this first example, pretend that each displayed word is one token. Real token boundaries are the subject of the next chapter.

    P(next token = v | context)
    For a fixed context: ∑v in vocabulary P(v | context) = 1.

    The vertical bar means “given.” The candidate token is v; the context is everything the model is allowed to condition on. A high value means the model favors that continuation in this context. It does not mean the model has independently checked the truth of a proposition containing that token.

    2. Turn predictions into a sequence

    The decoder is the rule that selects a token. Greedy decoding picks a highest-probability token. Sampling draws a token at random according to a distribution. With the toy probabilities above, repeated independent draws from this one context would select tea about 60% of the time. One draw does not have to select tea.

    Once the decoder chooses a token, the system appends it and repeats. After “Please pour the tea”, the probabilities might favor a period, “into”, or something else. The model is not reusing the same distribution: the context has changed. Generation ends at an end-of-sequence token, a length limit, or another stopping rule.

    The probability of a particular continuation multiplies its conditional probabilities. Suppose our desired continuation is “tea .” followed by an end token. If the successive probabilities are 0.60, 0.50, and 0.80, its probability is 0.60 × 0.50 × 0.80 = 0.24. Each factor refers to the context containing the preceding chosen tokens.

    P(y₁, …, yₘ | prompt) = ∏t=1…m P(yₜ | prompt, y₁, …, yₜ₋₁)

    This is the probability chain rule. It does not require a neural network; the n-gram chapter will construct these probabilities from counts. Also, locally greedy choices need not yield the most probable complete sequence: an initially less likely token can lead to much more likely later tokens.

    Try it: temperature changes the choice distribution

    Temperature rescales how concentrated the distribution is before sampling. If the original probabilities are positive, the transformed probabilities are q(v) = p(v)1/T / ∑ p(u)1/T, for T > 0. At T = 1, nothing changes. A smaller T concentrates probability on the leading candidate; a larger T spreads it out. This does not change the learned model weights.

    One fixed context · three-token toy vocabulary
    tea

    At T = 1.00, probabilities are unchanged. Greedy decoding chooses tea at every temperature shown.

    Notice what remains stable: tea stays the most likely candidate. Temperature preserves probability ordering. “More random” therefore does not mean “more accurate,” and low temperature cannot make an unsupported answer true. Some systems also truncate the candidate set with top-k or top-p sampling; this lab applies only temperature.

    3. Learn from text by making many small predictions

    Take the training sentence “Birds build nests”. It supplies several prediction tasks: from a start marker predict Birds; from Birds predict build; from Birds build predict nests. The text itself provides the targets. This is why next-token training is called self-supervised: a person need not label a separate answer for every position.

    During ordinary next-token training, each prediction is conditioned on the actual preceding tokens in the training example. The model does not have to roll out its own mistakes first. At generation time, later predictions instead condition on the tokens the decoder chose. That difference matters: one poor generated choice can change the rest of the continuation.

    A common training loss is the negative log probability of the observed target. If the target is nests, assigning it probability 0.8 incurs about 0.322 bits of loss; assigning it 0.2 incurs about 2.322 bits. Both distributions might still choose nests greedily. Loss rewards putting more probability on the observed target, not merely winning an argmax contest.

    loss for one target = −log₂ P(observed token | preceding tokens)
    average loss = sum of target losses / number of scored targets

    Training usually uses natural logarithms, giving units of nats instead of bits. Changing the log base scales the loss; it does not change which probabilities minimize it. An optimizer adjusts learned numerical parameters to reduce the training loss over many examples. The goal is useful prediction on held-out text, not just recollection of the training sentences.

    Why can this teach more than spelling? Predicting the last token of “The keys beside the lamp are” requires different cues from predicting a city after an address prefix. Repeated contexts can encourage representations of agreement, topics, word meaning, and recurring facts. However, being helpful for prediction is not proof of a particular internal reasoning procedure. A system can also learn shortcuts, repeated errors, and social stereotypes from its data.

    4. Represent context with numbers

    A token ID is just an index. If tea has ID 42 and water has ID 900, the difference 858 has no linguistic meaning. An embedding table maps each ID to a learned vector, such as a list of hundreds of real numbers. The model learns useful relationships between these vectors while training.

    A contextual network transforms the vectors using the surrounding tokens. The representation of “bat” after “the cave sheltered a” should support different predictions from the representation after “the batter swung a”. The initial token lookup may be identical; the context-dependent representation need not be.

    The final representation is converted into one score, or logit, per vocabulary token. A softmax converts those scores to probabilities. If logits are z(v), then p(v) = exp(z(v)) / ∑ exp(z(u)). Adding the same constant to every logit leaves the probabilities unchanged. Only their relative values matter.

    Transformers are one way to implement the contextual network. Attention lets a token position combine information from eligible earlier positions. We will build that calculation in the transformer chapter; for now, the key separation is tokenization → numerical representation → contextual computation → vocabulary distribution.

    5. Training changes weights; prompting changes context

    Pretraining learns general prediction patterns from a large corpus. Instruction tuning then trains on demonstrations of desired responses: for example, a request to classify a message paired with the intended label. The training recipe may mask prompt tokens out of the loss so the optimization focuses on the response.

    Preference training uses comparisons or reward signals to favor some responses over others. A more helpful explanation might be preferred to an evasive one; a supported answer might be preferred to an invented one. What improves depends on the examples, feedback, and objective. “Preferred” is a training signal, not a universal definition of correctness.

    Some post-training uses verifiable rewards, such as whether generated code passes a set of tests. This gives a concrete signal, but the verifier still has a boundary: passing incomplete tests does not prove a program correct for every input. These stages describe common roles; an actual training pipeline may repeat, combine, or omit stages.

    At inference, an ordinary generation pass keeps those learned weights fixed. A prompt can supply instructions, retrieved passages, previous conversation, or examples. In-context learning means behavior adapts to examples in that context without an optimizer updating the weights during that pass. A product may later use feedback for training or store persistent memory; those are separate mechanisms.

    6. Language structure still matters

    Language technology has used hand-written rules, probabilistic counts, recurrent networks, and transformers. Each gives us useful concepts. Count-based models make probability estimates inspectable. Grammars describe structure. Neural representations make it possible to share statistical evidence across many related contexts. Learning the older tools helps explain what a newer system gains and what it still must handle.

    Consider “Leena handed Omar the map because he was lost.” Several kinds of analysis are possible. A syntactic analysis asks which expressions are subjects, objects, and modifiers. A semantic-role analysis asks who gave what to whom. Coreference asks who he refers to. Discourse analysis asks how the reason clause connects to the preceding event.

    These are different questions, not interchangeable labels for “understanding.” A model may predict a reasonable continuation without exposing any of these structures explicitly. Conversely, an NLP application may need an explicit output: a name span, relation, parse tree, or timestamp. That is why the book moves beyond generation into linguistic annotation, speech, and conversation.

    Interpretability studies can ask whether internal representations contain information about such structure. Being able to decode a property from a representation is one kind of evidence; establishing that the model actually relies on it for a decision is a stronger question. Keep that distinction in mind when reading claims that a model has “learned grammar.”

    7. Evaluate the whole system against its job

    An assistant contains more than a language model. It can assemble a prompt, retrieve documents, call a calculator, execute a tool, or validate a proposed output. If an assistant answers a question from a retrieved manual, the result depends on finding the right passage and generating a faithful answer. Good next-token prediction alone does not establish either one.

    Match the measurement to the intended behavior
    System’s jobUseful checkWhat one score can miss
    Predict unseen textHeld-out average log lossTruth, usefulness, and behavior on a particular task
    Extract meeting datesPrecision and recall on annotated datesWhich date formats or languages fail
    Answer from a manualAnswer correctness and support in retrieved evidenceMissing retrieval results and unsupported extra claims
    Transcribe speechWord error rate, inspected across speakers and conditionsWhether a small error changes a crucial name or number

    Decide on a held-out evaluation set before tuning. If the same documents, answers, or near duplicates appear in training, apparent performance can reflect leakage. If evaluation contains only clean, short English messages, it says little about noisy, long, or multilingual input. Averages can also hide failures concentrated on particular dialects or user groups.

    For a concrete example, suppose a date extractor finds 18 dates, 15 correctly, in documents containing 20 true dates. Precision is 15/18 ≈ 83.3%; recall is 15/20 = 75%. It made three false detections and missed five dates. Whether this is acceptable depends on the use: a calendar assistant that creates appointments has different costs from a search tool that merely highlights candidates.

    Also measure latency and resource use when they matter. Decide how uncertainty and missing evidence should appear to the user. If the system can act, evaluate the consequences of the actions as well as the words describing them. A pleasant explanation cannot compensate for booking the wrong date.

    Check your understanding

    A token has probability 0.9. Is the statement containing it 90% likely to be true?

    No. This is a probability assigned to a textual continuation under the model and its context. It is not automatically calibrated confidence in the truth of the full statement. The model could strongly predict a familiar false claim.

    A prompt includes five labeled examples. Have the model’s parameters been fine-tuned?

    Not in an ordinary inference pass. The examples change the input context. Fine-tuning requires an explicit training procedure that updates parameters.

    A generator always chooses tea in the lab. What does lowering temperature change?

    It changes the sampling probabilities, but tea remains the argmax. A greedy decoder chooses tea throughout the slider range. A sampling decoder chooses tea more frequently as temperature falls.

    Why can lower training loss coexist with worse performance on new documents?

    The model may fit training-specific details or duplicates instead of transferable patterns. The new documents may also come from a different distribution. Held-out evaluation checks behavior that the training objective alone cannot establish.
