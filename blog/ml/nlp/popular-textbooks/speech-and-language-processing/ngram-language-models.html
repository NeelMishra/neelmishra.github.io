    N-gram Language Models: Count, Predict, and Measure Surprise

    September 2026 · 16 min read + probability lab

    A language model makes a small bet at every token. Build one from four sentences, see exactly where it fails, and learn to measure how surprised it is by new text.

    What you will be able to do: turn text counts into a normalized next-token distribution; distinguish an exact probability identity from a modeling assumption; explain why a missing pair destroys a sentence score; and calculate perplexity without hiding the boundary tokens.

    Before you begin: the words and tokens companion explains why the units we count are a design choice. This article uses lowercase, whitespace-separated words and explicit sentence boundaries.

    An original learning companion to Jurafsky and Martin’s Chapter 3, “N-gram Language Models”, in the August 19, 2026 draft. The examples, figures, and lab here were created for this article.

    1. A sentence is a sequence of conditional bets

    Imagine a keyboard that has seen i drink. It could suggest tea, coffee, or something else. A language model supplies a probability for every allowed next token. Those probabilities must sum to one. Choosing the most likely suggestion is one use of that distribution; scoring a whole sentence is another.

    Let w₁, …, wₜ be the tokens already written. The next-token question is P(wₜ₊₁ | w₁, …, wₜ): how likely is this candidate, given the preceding text? The vertical bar means “given.” Multiplying such conditional probabilities gives the probability of a complete sequence:

    P(w₁, w₂, w₃) = P(w₁) × P(w₂ | w₁) × P(w₃ | w₁, w₂)

    This is the chain rule, an exact identity of probability. It does not assume that words are independent. The difficult part is estimating each factor. If the entire history is long, that exact history may never occur in the training data, even when its component words are common.

    An n-gram model solves a smaller problem: retain only the previous n − 1 tokens. A unigram ignores the history; a bigram keeps one previous token; a trigram keeps two. Here “bigram” names both a pair such as drink tea and a model that predicts the second member from the first.

    Bigram approximation: P(wₜ | w₁, …, wₜ₋₁) ≈ P(wₜ | wₜ₋₁)

    The approximation is the Markov assumption. Now the model treats i drink and you drink identically when predicting the next word. That pooling gives us more evidence per context. It also throws away information: the topic established earlier in a paragraph cannot affect the prediction.

    2. Build the entire model from four sentences

    We will train on this deliberately tiny corpus. Repeated occurrences count separately. The markers <s> and </s> mean beginning and end of sentence.

    <s> i   drink tea    </s>
<s> i   drink coffee </s>
<s> you drink tea    </s>
<s> i   like  tea    </s>

    For any history h, count each next token w, then divide by the total number of continuations after that history:

    PMLE(w | h) = C(h, w) / ∑v ∈ V C(h, v)

    C means count, and V is the set of possible output tokens. This is maximum likelihood estimation (MLE): among unrestricted conditional probability tables, these relative frequencies maximize the probability of our observed training events.

    Every nonzero row in our bigram model
    Previous tokenObserved continuationsTotalNext-token probabilities
    <s>i: 3; you: 14P(i) = 3/4; P(you) = 1/4
    idrink: 2; like: 13P(drink) = 2/3; P(like) = 1/3
    youdrink: 11P(drink) = 1
    drinktea: 2; coffee: 13P(tea) = 2/3; P(coffee) = 1/3
    liketea: 11P(tea) = 1
    tea</s>: 33P(end) = 1
    coffee</s>: 11P(end) = 1

    Each probability in the final column is conditional on its row. Every unlisted continuation has probability zero. To calculate P(tea | drink), the denominator is three occurrences of the history drink, not the sixteen target tokens in the whole corpus. Row normalization is what makes a conditional distribution.

    Our output vocabulary has seven members: i, you, drink, like, tea, coffee, and </s>. The start marker is supplied as context; it is never predicted. The end marker is predicted, because stopping is an event the model must assign probability to. Once it appears, generation stops: we do not count an artificial transition from one sentence’s end to the next sentence’s start.

    A useful boundary check: there are four sentences with three ordinary words each, so training contains 12 word targets + 4 end targets = 16 predictions. It contains four start contexts, but no start targets. A trigram would need two start markers to supply its first two-token history.

    With these boundaries, a sentence score includes the chance that it ends at that point. Omitting the end prediction instead scores a prefix. Adding an end symbol alone does not prove every imaginable model eventually stops; in this particular count model every path reaches the end after three words, so termination is guaranteed.

    3. A new sentence can use familiar pieces

    The exact sentence you drink coffee is absent from training. Yet every adjacent pair in it was observed. Our model assigns it:

    P(you drink coffee, end)
    = P(you | start) × P(drink | you)
    × P(coffee | drink) × P(end | coffee)
    = 1/4 × 1 × 1/3 × 1 = 1/12 ≈ 0.08333

    This is genuine recombination. The model borrows you drink from one sentence and drink coffee from another. Its limited memory makes that possible. The same limitation can produce awkward text: locally plausible pairs need not form a globally sensible argument.

    To sample, start at <s>, choose i with probability 3/4 or you with probability 1/4, and repeat using the chosen token’s row. Stop at </s>. Random sampling represents the distribution. Always taking the largest probability is greedy decoding, which repeatedly favors the same high-probability route. Neither procedure creates new knowledge about which drink a particular person actually wants.

    4. A zero count is too strong a claim

    Now score i like coffee. All three words are familiar, but like coffee never occurred together. MLE gives P(coffee | like) = 0; the entire product becomes zero. Our dataset’s silence has become a claim of impossibility.

    Smoothing reserves some probability for missing events. Add-k smoothing inserts the same positive pseudo-count into every possible continuation:

    Pk(w | h) = [C(h, w) + k] / [C(h) + k|V|]

    Here C(h) means the row total. The denominator increases by k|V|, because there are |V| additions. Without that adjustment, the row would no longer sum to one. With k = 0.5 and seven outputs, the unseen continuation gets P(coffee | like) = 0.5/(1 + 3.5) = 1/9.

    That repair has a cost. After drink, smoothing now assigns probability to odd continuations such as drink drink. Add-one smoothing is the special case k = 1; with a large vocabulary, its many pseudo-counts can overwhelm a small observed row. As k grows very large, all seven outcomes approach probability 1/7. More smoothing is not automatically better.

    A missing history is another edge case. If its total count is zero, MLE gives 0/0 and is undefined. Positive add-k yields the uniform distribution 1/|V|. Our interactive histories all occur in training, so its zero-smoothing setting avoids that undefined case.

    5. Borrow evidence from a simpler model

    Add-k treats unseen outputs equally. Interpolation can borrow the preferences of a lower-order model instead. Mix a bigram estimate with a unigram estimate:

    Pmix(w | h) = λ Pbigram(w | h) + (1 − λ) Punigram(w),   0 ≤ λ ≤ 1

    Our unigram model counts the sixteen targets, including four end markers. Coffee occurs once, so Punigram(coffee) = 1/16. If λ = 0.8, then the missing pair receives Pmix(coffee | like) = 0.8 × 0 + 0.2 × 1/16 = 0.0125. Unlike uniform pseudo-counts, unigram evidence allocates more of its contribution to frequent words.

    Both component distributions sum to one and their nonnegative weights sum to one, so the mixture does too. In a higher-order model, combine trigram, bigram, and unigram components in the same way. A rare history may deserve less trust than a frequent one, so practical interpolation weights can depend on the context.

    Backoff instead uses a shorter context when longer-context evidence is missing or discounted. A probability-based backoff scheme must allocate the leftover mass carefully. A heuristic backoff score is not automatically a normalized probability; inserting arbitrary scores into the perplexity formula does not produce a valid perplexity comparison.

    Smoothing also cannot make an output token appear in a vocabulary that excludes it. A word-level system needs an explicit unknown-word policy, usually learned by mapping selected training words to an unknown token. A subword or byte-based tokenizer provides another route, with coverage determined by its token inventory and fallback rules. Decide this before evaluating new text.

    6. Keep the exam away from the answer key

    Increasing the context length or adjusting k changes the model. To decide whether those changes help, separate three jobs:

    • Training data: build the vocabulary policy and estimate counts.
    • Development data: choose the order, smoothing strength, or interpolation weights.
    • Test data: report performance after those decisions are fixed.

    The four-sentence corpus is a teaching tool, far too small for a trustworthy benchmark. Its novel examples are demonstrations, not evidence that one model generalizes well. In a real experiment, keep documents or other relevant groups separate so near-duplicate passages do not leak across the split. Match the evaluation text to the intended use: a model of chat messages may struggle with biology lectures.

    Repeatedly selecting settings based on the test score turns the test set into development data. Similarly, fitting a tokenizer on the test corpus gives the pipeline information it was meant to discover only at evaluation. Record the split, tokenizer, vocabulary, boundary convention, and chosen settings so a lower score has a clear interpretation.

    7. Perplexity puts surprise on a per-token scale

    A long sequence usually has a smaller probability than a short one because it multiplies more factors. To compare predictions on a fixed evaluation task, average the negative log probability per target. With base-two logarithms, this is the model’s empirical cross-entropy, measured in bits per token:

    H = −(1/N) ∑t=1N log₂ P(wₜ | historyₜ)
    Perplexity = 2H = P(sequence)−1/N

    Return to you drink coffee, whose probability is 1/12. There are four predictions: three words and the end marker. The start marker contributes context, not a target. Therefore H = log₂(12)/4 ≈ 0.8962 bits per token and perplexity is 121/4 ≈ 1.8612.

    If a model assigns probability 1/4 to each observed next token, its perplexity is four. That makes perplexity resemble an effective number of equally plausible choices. The general result need not be an integer and is not a literal count of candidates. It measures surprise at the tokens that actually occurred.

    For i like coffee, the unsmoothed zero factor gives infinite negative log loss and infinite perplexity. With k = 0.5, its four factors are 7/15, 3/13, 1/9, and 1/3. Their product is 7/1755, producing perplexity about 3.9792. Smoothing makes this previously impossible sequence scoreable.

    For numerical stability, add log probabilities instead of multiplying thousands of tiny values. Across multiple sentences, reset the history at each start, sum the losses for every target, and divide by the total number of targets. Averaging sentence perplexities directly would weight sentences incorrectly and also average after a nonlinear transformation.

    # probabilities includes each sentence's end-token prediction
import math

if any(p == 0 for p in probabilities):
    perplexity = math.inf
else:
    mean_nll = -sum(math.log(p) for p in probabilities) / len(probabilities)
    perplexity = math.exp(mean_nll)

    Compare like with like. Use the same evaluation text, tokenization, vocabulary/unknown-token policy, and end-token convention. Splitting one word into three tokens changes both the events and the denominator. A lower per-token perplexity from a different tokenizer does not by itself establish a better model. Even a fair perplexity improvement measures predictive fit; verify the downstream result if your actual goal is better transcription or suggestions.

    9. Check your understanding

    Why is P(coffee | drink) = 1/3 rather than 1/16?

    We are conditioning on the history drink. It has three continuations in training, one of which is coffee. The denominator 16 belongs to the unigram question “How often is any target coffee?”

    How can an unseen sentence have positive probability?

    The model scores local transitions. you drink coffee never occurred as a complete sentence, but all of its bigrams, including the start and end transitions, did. Their probabilities multiply to 1/12.

    With k = 1, what is P(coffee | like)?

    The numerator is 0 + 1. The row total is 1, and seven output tokens each receive a pseudo-count, so the denominator is 1 + 7 = 8. The answer is 1/8. The start marker is not an eighth output.

    Does adding a tiny constant only to zero probabilities fix the model?

    It makes the affected row sum to more than one unless you also redistribute or renormalize its mass. A smoothing rule must define a valid full distribution, not just patch the factor you happened to evaluate.

    Two systems report perplexities of 12 and 9. Which is better?

    First check that they scored the same data with the same tokenization, vocabulary policy, and boundary convention. Under those conditions, 9 means higher average predictive probability on that test set. It does not prove better factual accuracy, reasoning, or performance on a different application.
