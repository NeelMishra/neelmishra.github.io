    Classification & Losses: From Scores to Probabilities

    September 2026 · 11 min read

    In regression boosting, we fit residuals because they were the negative derivatives of squared loss. We now keep the same sequence—predict, differentiate, fit, add—but change what the model predicts and which loss it minimizes.

    A score is the quantity the trees add to

    For binary labels $y\in\{0,1\}$, maintain a real score $F$. Convert it to a probability by $p=1/(1+e^{-F})$. The inverse relationship is the log odds: $F=\ln[p/(1-p)]$. A score of zero means probability 0.5. A score of $\ln3$ means probability 0.75. Trees add to the score because scores can increase or decrease without leaving a valid probability range.

    The loss for one observed label is $-y\ln p-(1-y)\ln(1-p)$. Substituting the sigmoid gives a convenient score-space form:

    $$\ell(y,F)=\ln(1+e^F)-yF.$$

    Differentiate with respect to $F$: the derivative of $\ln(1+e^F)$ is $e^F/(1+e^F)=p$, and the derivative of $-yF$ is $-y$. Thus the gradient is $g=p-y$, and the negative-gradient target is $r=y-p$. These are continuous numbers even though the labels are categories, so the correction learner is a regression tree.

    10.2+0.8Raise this score substantially.
    00.2−0.2Lower it a little.
    10.9+0.1Already plausible; a small upward direction remains.

    Choose a baseline that minimizes the loss

    Before the first tree, use one constant probability. If the training positive fraction is $\pi$, minimizing summed log loss gives $p_0=\pi$, hence $F_0=\ln[\pi/(1-\pi)]$. With three positives among four rows, $F_0=\ln3\approx1.0986$, not 0.75. In a weighted dataset, use the weighted positive fraction.

    If every label is the same, the unconstrained optimal logit is infinite. A real implementation needs an explicit smoothing, clipping, or constant-class policy. Keep the numeric guard separate from the theoretical optimum.

    One complete binary update

    Take $x=[1,2,3,4]$ and $y=[0,0,1,1]$. The baseline is $F_0=0$ because $\pi=0.5$, so every initial probability is 0.5. The negative gradients are $[-0.5,-0.5,+0.5,+0.5]$. A regression stump splits at 2.5, grouping the signs perfectly.

    For a first-order update using the mean negative gradient directly, the leaf corrections are −0.5 and +0.5. With $\eta=0.5$, the new scores are −0.25 and +0.25, and the probabilities are about 0.4378 and 0.5622. They are not 0.25 and 0.75: the addition happened before sigmoid.

    Why a Newton leaf can have a different value

    The second derivative is $h=p(1-p)$. It measures curvature: how the loss slope changes as the score moves. A quadratic approximation chooses leaf correction $w=-G/H$, where $G$ and $H$ sum the row gradients and curvatures in that leaf. We will derive and regularize this rule in the next chapter.

    For the left leaf above, $G=0.5+0.5=1$ and $H=0.25+0.25=0.5$, giving $w=-2$. The right leaf gets +2. With rate 0.5, scores become −1 and +1, probabilities become 0.2689 and 0.7311, and mean log loss is about 0.3133. This is a different update from the first-order leaf means. Both use the same initial gradients; curvature changes the step size.

    The pure leaves' exact unregularized log-loss minima are at infinite scores. The finite Newton update is a local approximation, not that exact optimum. More complex losses, constraints, and regularization can further alter leaf values. Avoid memorizing “classification leaf equals mean residual” as a universal implementation rule.

    Check: a positive row has probability 0.8. What are its gradient, negative gradient, and curvature?

    The gradient is $0.8-1=-0.2$, the desired direction is +0.2, and curvature is $0.8(0.2)=0.16$. For a one-row unregularized Newton leaf, the correction is $-(-0.2)/0.16=1.25$ on the score scale, before learning-rate shrinkage.

    Other targets require other losses

    The loss determines the best constant start, the derivative targets, and the optimal leaf correction. Changing only the label you put on an ordinary residual algorithm is not enough.

    Half squared errorMean targetNegative gradient y − F; mean residual in each leaf.
    Absolute errorA median targetUse a residual sign as a negative subgradient away from zero; leaf optimization uses a residual median.
    Quantile loss at τThe τ quantileDirection τ for underprediction, τ − 1 for overprediction; refine leaves with a residual τ quantile.
    Huber lossMinimizer of the Huber objectiveResidual direction near zero, capped magnitude for large residuals; refine for that loss.

    For residuals $[-4,1,9]$, a squared-loss leaf uses their mean 2, while an absolute-loss leaf uses their median 1. At quantile level $\tau=0.8$, predicting a high quantile charges an underprediction four times as much per unit as an overprediction: weights 0.8 versus 0.2. A residual 0.8 quantile can be 9 for this three-row example under the empirical pinball-loss objective. State the quantile convention if reporting interpolated software quantiles.

    Absolute and quantile losses have a kink at zero; use a subgradient convention there. Quantile intervals need coverage evaluation on held-out data, and independently trained upper and lower quantiles can cross. For nonnegative counts, a Poisson loss uses a log-scale score and positive mean $\mu=e^F$, with gradient $\mu-y$; zero and exposure handling belong to the model specification.

    Extend the score idea, not the numeric class IDs

    For $K$ classes, keep scores $F_1,\ldots,F_K$ and use softmax: $p_k=e^{F_k}/\sum_j e^{F_j}$. If $y_k$ is the one-hot label indicator, the negative gradient for class $k$ is $y_k-p_k$. Common implementations fit a correction tree per class per round; other structures are possible. The softmax probabilities are coupled, so binary formulas cannot simply be reused as if every class were an unrelated problem.

    Never regress on IDs such as 0, 1, and 2 to represent unordered classes: it would make class 1 artificially intermediate. For stable computation, subtract the maximum score before exponentiating softmax, and use a stable softplus/log-sum-exp implementation for log loss.

    From directions to optimized trees

    You now know what score a tree modifies and how the loss creates its training signal. The Newton and XGBoost chapter turns these row-level gradients and curvatures into leaf values and split gains. That will make the library hyperparameters meaningful rather than a list of names.
