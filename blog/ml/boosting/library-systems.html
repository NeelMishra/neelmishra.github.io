    XGBoost, LightGBM & CatBoost: How the Systems Differ

    September 2026 · 10 min read

    We can now score a split. A fast boosting system must also decide which candidates are worth evaluating and how to represent the data. Keep three questions separate: how a split is scored, how it is searched, and how the resulting tree grows.

    Histograms compress a search, not the target labels

    Assign each numeric feature value to a bin and accumulate gradient and Hessian sums per bin. A prefix scan gives the statistics to the left of each candidate bin boundary; subtract from the totals to obtain the right statistics. Binning removes many thresholds from consideration but allows repeated rounds to reuse a compact feature representation.

    The four-row example shows the accuracy tradeoff explicitly: with a useful split inside a coarse bin, gain falls from 11.5 to 2.875 because that boundary cannot be searched. The bins are learned from training data; avoid adapting them to a held-out test distribution during a supposedly untouched evaluation.

    Leaf-wise growth spends the next split where gain is largest

    Imagine current leaves A and B with best eligible gains 8 and 3. A leaf-wise procedure splits A first. If one new child offers gain 6, it can split again before B. The result can have uneven path lengths. A level-wise schedule instead considers leaves at the current level before descending to the next one.

    LightGBM is known for leaf-wise growth. Restricting leaf count, maximum depth, and minimum leaf support helps prevent an attractive but tiny branch from becoming too specific. A depth-$d$ binary tree has at most $2^d$ leaves; this is an upper bound, not a promise about the shape of a leaf-wise tree. Tune leaf count with sample size and depth constraints rather than copying a depth value from a different library.

    GOSS and feature bundling solve different costs

    Gradient-based One-Side Sampling (GOSS) retains rows with large gradient magnitude and samples smaller-gradient rows. The sampled group is upweighted to account for the rows it represents. With 100 rows, suppose we retain the 20 largest-gradient rows and sample 10 of the other 80. Each sampled small-gradient row receives an inverse-sampling factor of 8 for the approximate statistics.

    The selection is based on the current training signal, not a permanent definition of which observations are important. The approximation introduces sampling variability; retaining large gradients is not guaranteed to improve every dataset. Specify the configured sampling strategy when describing a run.

    Exclusive feature bundling targets another expense: many sparse features that are rarely nonzero together. Suitable features can share a compact representation using distinct bin ranges, reducing effective feature count during histogram construction. For example, one-hot indicators of mutually exclusive categories do not activate simultaneously. Bundling is a storage/search technique, not an instruction to add arbitrary correlated feature columns together.

    A category encoding must not reveal the row's answer

    If category A appears only once, encoding it as its observed positive rate gives that row its own label. A flexible model can exploit this during training while failing on new rows. Even categories with multiple rows can leak some information when a row contributes to the statistic used to predict itself.

    An ordered target statistic follows a permutation of the training rows. For the current row, use only earlier rows of its category, together with a prior. With earlier category-label sum $s$, earlier count $c$, fixed prior mean $p_0=0.5$, and prior strength $a=2$, our illustrative encoding is $(s+ap_0)/(c+a)$.

    Permutation positionCategory / labelEarlier matching labelsEncoding
    1A / 1None1/2 = 0.5
    2A / 0[1]2/3 ≈ 0.6667
    3B / 1None1/2 = 0.5
    4A / 1[1, 0]2/4 = 0.5

    At position 2, the encoding is $(1+2\times0.5)/(1+2)=2/3$, even though that row's own label is 0. At position 4, its current label 1 has not yet entered the sum. For an unseen category at prediction time, this simple scheme falls back to the prior; a new A after all training rows would use $(2+1)/(3+2)=0.6$. These calculations illustrate the principle, not every detail of CatBoost's category combinations or statistics.

    A learned prior also has to respect the allowed training boundary. Internal permutations do not create an external future-time or unseen-customer test. If the deployment problem is chronological, evaluate chronologically regardless of the model's internal category handling.

    Ordered boosting is a separate idea

    The categorical statistic above concerns the feature representation. Ordered boosting addresses a related training-versus-test mismatch in gradient estimation: a model's in-sample prediction has already depended on that example's target. Conceptually, use a model built from an earlier permutation prefix to obtain the prediction used for the current row's residual. CatBoost implements efficient variants of this idea; it is more than shuffling the data once.

    CatBoost also commonly uses symmetric trees: at each depth the same split question is applied to all nodes at that depth. A three-question path can be represented by three Boolean outcomes, identifying one of up to eight leaves. This structural constraint can make evaluation efficient, while limiting the shapes available. Other growth policies exist; state the selected policy instead of treating every CatBoost configuration as identical.

    Check: does native categorical handling eliminate the need for validation?

    No. Internal encoding and gradient procedures address specific sources of training bias. They do not enforce your external time/group boundaries, choose the right deployment metric, detect unavailable features, or reserve an untouched final test.

    Compare systems by a concrete experiment

    SystemA useful emphasis to understandWhat to record in a comparison
    XGBoostRegularized objective, constraints, and alternative search methodsTree method, penalties, categorical settings, hardware.
    LightGBMHistogram efficiency, leaf-wise growth, optional sampling/bundling techniquesLeaf/depth/support limits and active sampling strategy.
    CatBoostCategorical statistics, ordered techniques, symmetric-tree workflowsCategory specification, boosting mode, and growth policy.

    These capabilities overlap. A categorical workflow may make CatBoost convenient; a constraint requirement or deployment integration may favor another system. Choose by valid held-out quality, training cost, and serving constraints under comparable tuning budgets. A library name alone does not specify a reproducible model.
