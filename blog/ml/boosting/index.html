    Boosting: Build a Model One Correction at a Time

    October 2026 · 12 min read

    Imagine practicing golf shots toward a pin 180 yards away. Bagging asks 100 golfers to tee off simultaneously from the exact same tee box and averages where their balls land—excellent if each golfer already hits the right distance on average (low bias) and only sprays left or right (high variance). Boosting plays golf the way an actual round works: one player hits a conservative 110-yard iron toward the green, walks up to where the ball landed, measures the remaining 70 yards, chips to within 8 feet, and taps in. Each swing does not replace the previous shot; it adds a targeted correction to the current position.

    Mathematically, boosting constructs a forward stagewise additive expansion from a family $\mathcal H$ of intentionally simple "weak" learners (typically shallow CART trees of depth $1$ to $6$):

    $$F_M(x) = F_0(x) + \sum_{m=1}^{M} \eta\,h_m(x), \qquad h_m \in \mathcal H.$$

    Define every symbol before going further:

    • $F_0(x)$: the initial constant prediction before any trees are grown (for example, the sample mean $\bar y$ under squared error, or initial log-odds $\ln\frac{\bar y}{1 - \bar y}$ under binary log-loss).
    • $M$: the total number of sequential boosting rounds (trees). Unlike bagging—where larger $B$ merely reduces Monte Carlo variance—larger $M$ directly increases model capacity and must be tuned or early-stopped on validation data.
    • $h_m(x) \in \mathcal H$: the $m$-th weak learner, fit specifically to repair the errors left behind by $F_{m-1}(x)$ without modifying $F_0, h_1, \dots, h_{m-1}$.
    • $\eta \in (0, 1]$ (also written $\nu$, or replaced by a round-specific vote $\alpha_m$ in AdaBoost): the learning rate (shrinkage) that damps each tree's step so many small, complementary trees share the work.

    1. Bagging vs. Boosting: Parallel Variance Reduction vs. Sequential Bias Reduction

    Because bagging averages identically distributed trees, $\mathbb E[\bar T(x)] = \mathbb E[\hat T_1(x)]$: it shrinks variance toward the correlation floor $\rho\sigma^2$ while leaving single-tree bias untouched, which is why Random Forests grow deep, unpruned trees. Boosting inverts every design choice: it starts with shallow, high-bias, low-variance trees (a depth-$d$ tree can model feature interactions of order at most $d$) and drives bias down sequentially by greedily solving

    $$h_m = \arg\min_{h \in \mathcal H} \sum_{i=1}^{n} L\Bigl(y_i,\, F_{m-1}(x_i) + \eta\,h(x_i)\Bigr)$$

    via local first-order gradients (GBM) or second-order quadratic approximations (XGBoost / LightGBM).

    2. Concrete Two-Round Residual Trace (Exact Mental Arithmetic)

    Take four training rows $(x_i, y_i) = (1, 10), (2, 12), (3, 23), (4, 35)$ under squared-error loss with learning rate $\eta = 0.5$ and depth-1 regression stumps ($h_m$ makes a single split $x < t$).

    1. Initialize ($m = 0$): $F_0(x) = \bar y = \frac{10 + 12 + 23 + 35}{4} = 20.0$ for all four rows (MSE $= 99.50$).
    2. Round 1 ($m = 1$): Residuals are $r_{i1} = y_i - F_0(x_i) = [-10, -8, +3, +15]$. Scanning midpoints $t \in \{1.5, 2.5, 3.5\}$, the split $x < 2.5$ wins, predicting $(-10 - 8)/2 = -9.0$ on $\{1, 2\}$ and $(+3 + 15)/2 = +9.0$ on $\{3, 4\}$. Damping by $\eta = 0.5$ updates $F_1 = 20.0 + 0.5\,h_1 = [15.5, 15.5, 24.5, 24.5]$, slashing MSE from $99.50$ to $38.75$.
    3. Round 2 ($m = 2$): New residuals are $r_{i2} = y_i - F_1(x_i) = [-5.5, -3.5, -1.5, +10.5]$. Now Row 4 ($+10.5$) stands out sharply, so the second stump shifts its split to $x < 3.5$, predicting $(-5.5 - 3.5 - 1.5)/3 = -3.5$ on $\{1, 2, 3\}$ and $+10.5$ on $\{4\}$. Updating $F_2 = F_1 + 0.5\,h_2$ gives $[13.75, 13.75, 22.75, 29.75]$ and drops MSE to $11.1875$!

    Verify the two-round boosting table in Python 
    import numpy as np
from sklearn.ensemble import GradientBoostingRegressor

X = np.array([[1.0], [2.0], [3.0], [4.0]])
y = np.array([10.0, 12.0, 23.0, 35.0])
gbr = GradientBoostingRegressor(
    loss="squared_error", learning_rate=0.5, n_estimators=2, max_depth=1, random_state=0
).fit(X, y)

for m, pred in enumerate(gbr.staged_predict(X), start=1):
    print(f"After Round {m}: F_{m} = {pred}, MSE = {np.mean((y - pred)**2):.4f}")
