    The 30-Second Playbook

    1. Confirm the elements must be contiguous. If not, this series doesn't apply — look at DP or subsequence techniques instead.
    2. Decide fixed vs. variable size. Fixed size is given directly; variable size is implied by "longest/shortest/count."
    3. Identify what the window needs to track: a scalar (sum/count), a frequency table, one extremum, or two extrema simultaneously. This picks the supporting data structure (nothing, hash map, one deque, two deques).
    4. Check the monotonicity precondition explicitly: are all values positive (for sums/products)? Is the validity condition prefix-monotonic (for counting)? If the precondition fails, stop and look for the Part 12 alternative instead of forcing the template.
    5. If it's a "transform first" problem (budgeted replacement, complement counting), restate the condition as a direct arithmetic check before reaching for a template.
    6. If nothing above fits cleanly, it's probably one of Part 12's false positives — re-read the problem for signs of mixed-sign sums, global feasibility checks, or a two-pointer scan on sorted data.

    Series Summary

    • Sliding window converts $O(n^2)$/$O(nk)$ brute force into $O(n)$ by reusing overlapping computation between consecutive windows, formalized as an amortized bound in Part 1.
    • Fixed-size (Parts 2–3) and variable-size (Parts 4–6) windows share one invariant but differ in whether both pointers move together or the left pointer moves conditionally.
    • Extrema need a monotonic deque (Part 7), not a scalar; range constraints need two of them at once (Part 8).
    • Positivity is the load-bearing assumption behind every shrink-based sum/product window (Part 9); budgeted and complement transformations (Part 10) restate awkward conditions as direct checks.
    • Real contest problems often compose these tools with order statistics or DP (Part 11).
    • Knowing when not to use sliding window (Part 12) is as valuable as knowing the templates themselves.
