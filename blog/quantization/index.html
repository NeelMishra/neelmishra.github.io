    Quantization: shrink the weights, then repair the layer

    October 2026 · 4 min read

    This series is about post-training weight quantization: take a trained network, store its weights with fewer bits, and keep the layer outputs close to the full-precision outputs. The first path is GPTQ.

    The notes follow Oscar Savolainen's GPTQ Quantization Explained and check the claims against the GPTQ paper and the authors' implementation. Frames from the video are included where the picture carries the point. The equations to compute with are the ones written in the notes.

    What this series is for

    A floating-point LLM is often slow because the weights have to move. Shrinking the weight tensor cuts that traffic. On current GPUs the matmul itself may still run in floating point after the values are unpacked. GPTQ is a way to choose the low-bit weights so that unpacking does not wreck the layer.

    The method does not retrain the network. It runs a small calibration set through one layer at a time, quantizes weights in a fixed order, and updates the weights that are still full precision so the layer output stays close to the original. That update is a second-order correction, inherited from pruning, then rewritten so a 175-billion-parameter model finishes in hours rather than a cubic greedy pass.

    Each method has its own folder and guide: quantization fundamentals, LLM.int8, SmoothQuant, AWQ, NF4/QLoRA, and QAT. The method-selection guide compares their goals. GPTQ has its own three-chapter derivation path. GGUF and the shorter serving notes stay in the MLOps comparison. The existing GPTQ inference note is the deployment-side summary.

    Reading route

    Read them in that order. The third chapter uses the column index and the inverse-Hessian update from the second, and the second uses the layer objective from the first.

    Choose a method-specific guide

    Start with Quantization: choose the method for the job when your question is about activation outliers, weight-only scaling, four-bit adapter training, or training with simulated quantization. It links to the dedicated method guides, their primary-paper references, and a shared evaluation contract. The reader-suggested video remains companion viewing.

    Boundaries

    GPTQ quantizes weights. The paper does not quantize activations, and it says the method does not speed up the multiplications themselves on hardware without mixed-precision operands. A smaller file and a faster load are different claims from a faster tensor core instruction.

    Group size and the lazy-update block size are also different knobs. A group of input channels can share a scale and a zero. The number 128 in Algorithm 1 is how many columns are quantized before the rest of the matrix is updated. The notes keep those apart.
