    AWQ: protect weights with activation-aware scaling

    October 2026 · 5 min read

    AWQ is a weight-only quantization method. Its key observation is that weight magnitude alone is a poor guide to importance: a small weight multiplied by a frequently large activation can matter greatly to the output. Activation statistics identify channels worth protecting, while equivalent scaling provides that protection without requiring a separate high-precision weight subset at inference.

    Change coordinates before rounding

    Use $y=Wx$, with one input feature per column of $W$. For a positive diagonal matrix $S$, the unquantized computation satisfies

    $$Wx=(WS)(S^{-1}x).$$

    AWQ quantizes the scaled weights $WS$ and retains the compensating inverse transformation on the input. Its approximate layer is $Q(WS)S^{-1}x$. In a network implementation, compatible adjacent operations can absorb the scaling, but every affected branch must preserve the equivalence. Multiplying weights by a scale and forgetting to divide their inputs changes the model before quantization even begins.

    If the scaled-weight rounding error is $E=Q(WS)-WS$, then the output error is $ES^{-1}x$. Increasing one channel's scale can reduce that channel's effective error after division. However, it can also enlarge the quantizer's shared range, increasing errors for neighboring weights. This tradeoff is why the scale needs a search rather than “make important channels arbitrarily large.”

    A fixed-grid illustration

    Consider one output with $w=(0.26,0.74)$ and $x=(10,1)$. The exact output is $3.34$. A toy grid with step $0.25$ rounds the weights to $(0.25,0.75)$, giving $3.25$: the output error is $-0.09$ even though each weight moved by only $0.01$.

    Choose $S=\operatorname{diag}(4,1)$. The transformed weights are $(1.04,0.74)$ and the transformed input is $(2.5,1)$. On the same nonclipping grid, they round to $(1,0.75)$ and still produce $3.25$. So scaling by four did not help this particular sample. Choose instead $S=\operatorname{diag}(2,1)$: $(0.52,0.74)$ rounds to $(0.5,0.75)$, again giving $3.25$. Favorable scales are not guaranteed by the identity alone.

    A scale of $25/13\approx1.92308$ instead sends the first weight to exactly $0.5$, and its input to $5.2$. The reconstructed output is $0.5(5.2)+0.75=3.35$, only $0.01$ above the original. Conversely, a scale of $2.5$ sends $0.26$ to $0.65$, rounds it to $0.75$, and produces $3.75$, which is worse. This example shows both effective protection and why scaling must be evaluated against reconstruction error. Real groupwise grids also recompute their range after scaling, creating another tradeoff.

    What calibration chooses

    The paper uses activation magnitudes to guide a channel-scaling search, with calibration forwards evaluating quantization error. This is not GPTQ's sequential inverse-Hessian compensation, nor ordinary gradient-based fine-tuning. Scaling and clipping choices depend on the calibration inputs; preserving the original floating-point weights before conversion lets you repeat that search when the target distribution changes.

    Use inputs resembling the intended workload, including the tokenizer and prompt formatting. Then validate held-out perplexity and downstream tasks. A low local reconstruction error on calibration examples does not prove multilingual, coding, or long-context behavior is unchanged.

    Protection must survive the kernel

    AWQ commonly targets low-bit groupwise weights with floating-point activations, rather than W8A8. Deployment needs a compatible packed layout, group size, scale handling, and fused dequantization path. A checkpoint produced by an AWQ converter is not automatically usable by every four-bit runtime. Benchmark batch and context settings explicitly; numerical protection and execution performance are separate results.

    Does “protect” mean keeping selected weights in FP16?

    Not in the scaling approach taught here. The goal is to improve their effective precision while keeping the low-bit representation uniform enough for efficient execution.
