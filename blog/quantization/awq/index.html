    AWQ: protect important weight channels

    October 2026 · 7 min read

    When we compress a language model's weights from 16-bit floats (FP16) down to 4-bit or 3-bit integers for fast inference (W4A16: low-bit weights, 16-bit activations), plain round-to-nearest (RTN) quantization badly hurts accuracy.

    Activation-aware Weight Quantization (AWQ) (Lin et al., MLSys 2024) starts from a simple empirical discovery: only $0.1\%$ to $1\%$ of the weights in a layer drive almost all of the quantization error, and those critical weights are not the largest weights in the matrix—they are the weight channels multiplied by the largest input activations.

    Keeping that $1\%$ of channels in FP16 and the other $99\%$ in INT4 restores model quality, but mixing FP16 and INT4 columns inside one matrix breaks contiguous GPU memory access. AWQ achieves the same protection while keeping every weight in uniform 4-bit integers in four steps:

    1. Measure channel importance from activations: pass a small calibration dataset through the layer and record the average absolute activation $s_X$ for each input channel.
    2. Scale up important weight channels before rounding: multiply weight channel $c$ by a scale factor $s_c > 1$ and divide input channel $c$ by $s_c$. This leaves the exact floating-point product $w_c x_c = (s_c w_c)(x_c / s_c)$ unchanged, while shrinking channel $c$'s rounding error by roughly $1/s_c$.
    3. Grid-search a single exponent $\alpha \in [0, 1]$ per layer: parameterize all channel scales as $s = s_X^{\,\alpha}$ and test $20$ evenly spaced values of $\alpha \in [0, 1]$ to minimize the layer's output reconstruction error on calibration data.
    4. Fuse $s^{-1}$ into the previous layer and pack uniform INT4 weights: fold the $1/s_c$ division into the preceding LayerNorm/RMSNorm or linear layer so inference has zero extra runtime cost.

    1. Motivation: why $1\%$ of activation channels dominate output error

    Consider one output neuron computing a dot product across $C$ input channels:

    $$y = \sum_{c=1}^{C} w_c x_c.$$

    When we quantize each weight $w_c$ to $\widehat{w}_c = w_c + \delta w_c$ (where $\delta w_c = \widehat{w}_c - w_c$ is the weight rounding error), the error in the layer's output $y$ is

    $$\widehat{y} - y = \sum_{c=1}^{C} (\widehat{w}_c - w_c)\,x_c = \sum_{c=1}^{C} \delta w_c \cdot x_c.$$

    Every weight's rounding error $\delta w_c$ is amplified by its input activation $x_c$. In large language models, specific hidden dimensions consistently carry huge activation outliers across tokens:

    • Suppose a neuron has two weights $w = [0.14,\, 0.70]$ and typical input activations $x = [20.0,\, 0.5]$.
    • Even though $w_1 = 0.14$ is five times smaller than $w_2 = 0.70$, a rounding error of $\delta w = 0.04$ on channel $1$ changes the output by $0.04 \times 20.0 = 0.80$.
    • The exact same rounding error $\delta w = 0.04$ on channel $2$ changes the output by only $0.04 \times 0.5 = 0.02$—forty times smaller.

    Selecting the $1\%$ of channels with the largest weight magnitudes $|w_c|$ does no better than picking channels at random. Selecting the $1\%$ of channels with the largest input activations $\mathbb{E}[|x_c|]$ cuts perplexity from $43.20$ down to $11.39$. Our goal now is to get that same protection without storing a separate FP16 tensor.

    2. Mathematical build-up: how scaling protects a channel

    Before quantizing weight $w_c$, multiply it by a positive scale factor $s_c > 1$ and divide the corresponding activation $x_c$ by $s_c$. In exact floating-point arithmetic, the product does not change:

    $$w_c x_c = (s_c w_c)\!\left(\frac{x_c}{s_c}\right).$$

    Now apply groupwise $b$-bit symmetric quantization to the weights. A group $g$ of $G$ contiguous weights (typically $G = 128$) shares a single step size $\Delta$. Define $Q(\cdot)$ as the quantize-and-reconstruct operator that snaps a weight to the nearest integer code in $\{-2^{b-1}+1, \dots, 2^{b-1}-1\}$ and multiplies back by $\Delta$:

    $$Q(w) = \Delta \cdot \operatorname{round}\!\left(\frac{w}{\Delta}\right), \qquad \Delta = \frac{\max_{j \in g}|w_j|}{2^{b-1}-1}.$$

    Compare the output error of channel $c$ with and without scaling:

    1. Without scaling ($s_c = 1$): Rounding $w_c / \Delta$ to the nearest integer produces a fractional grid offset $\varepsilon_c = \operatorname{round}(w_c/\Delta) - w_c/\Delta \in [-1/2, 1/2]$. Multiplying by $\Delta$ and by $x_c$, the output error from channel $c$ is
      $$\operatorname{Err}_{\mathrm{unscaled}}(w_c x_c) = \bigl|Q(w_c)x_c - w_c x_c\bigr| = \Delta \cdot |\varepsilon_c| \cdot |x_c| \le \frac{\Delta}{2}|x_c|.$$
    2. With scaling ($s_c > 1$): We quantize $s_c w_c$ on the group's new grid of step size $\Delta'$ and multiply by the inverse-scaled activation $x_c / s_c$:
      $$Q(s_c w_c)\,\frac{x_c}{s_c} = \Delta' \cdot \operatorname{round}\!\left(\frac{s_c w_c}{\Delta'}\right)\frac{x_c}{s_c}.$$
      The integer rounding $\operatorname{round}(s_c w_c / \Delta')$ still lands within half a step ($\varepsilon'_c \in [-1/2, 1/2]$), so the error in the scaled weight $Q(s_c w_c) - s_c w_c$ is bounded by $\Delta'/2$. Dividing the activation by $s_c$ divides that error by $s_c$:
      $$\operatorname{Err}_{\mathrm{scaled}}(w_c x_c) = \left|Q(s_c w_c)\frac{x_c}{s_c} - w_c x_c\right| = \frac{\Delta'}{s_c} \cdot |\varepsilon'_c| \cdot |x_c| \le \frac{\Delta'}{2s_c}|x_c|.$$

    Taking the ratio of the two error bounds gives the relative error factor for channel $c$:

    $$\text{Relative error factor} = \frac{\Delta'}{\Delta}\cdot\frac{1}{s_c}.$$

    This ratio reveals the exact trade-off that governs AWQ:

    • Why moderate $s_c$ reduces error ($\Delta' \approx \Delta$): Salient channels are identified by their large activations $|x_c|$, so their weight $|w_c|$ usually sits well below the group's largest weight $\max_{j \in g}|w_j|$. For a moderate scale $s_c > 1$, the scaled weight $s_c|w_c|$ still stays below (or barely nudges) the group maximum. That leaves $\Delta' \approx \Delta$, so the error factor drops to $\approx 1/s_c < 1$ with zero penalty to the other channels in the group.
    • Why huge $s_c$ ruins the group ($\Delta' \propto s_c$): Once $s_c$ is pushed large enough that $s_c|w_c| > \max_{j \neq c}|w_j|$, channel $c$ becomes the new group maximum and $\Delta' = s_c|w_c| / (2^{b-1}-1)$ grows linearly with $s_c$. At that point, $\Delta' / (\Delta s_c)$ stops shrinking for channel $c$, while every unscaled neighbor $j \neq c$ ($s_j = 1$) in the same group sees its grid step coarsened by $\Delta'/\Delta > 1$.

    3. The AWQ Algorithm

    Let $W \in \mathbb{R}^{D \times C}$ be a linear layer's weight matrix ($D$ output channels, $C$ input channels) and let $X \in \mathbb{R}^{C \times N}$ be the input activations recorded across $N$ calibration tokens. Writing $S = \operatorname{diag}(s_1, \dots, s_C)$, we want to choose positive channel scales $s \in \mathbb{R}^C$ that minimize the layer's output reconstruction error:

    $$s^* = \arg\min_{s \in \mathbb{R}_{>0}^C} \bigl\lVert Q(W S)\,S^{-1} X - W X \bigr\rVert_F^2.$$

    Because the rounding operation $\operatorname{round}(\cdot)$ inside $Q(\cdot)$ has zero gradient almost everywhere, we cannot optimize $s \in \mathbb{R}^C$ with gradient descent, and searching $C = 4096$ independent numbers by brute force is intractable.

    AWQ's 1D parameterization. Because a channel's importance is driven by its average activation magnitude, AWQ first computes the per-channel mean absolute activation across the $N$ calibration tokens:

    $$s_X = \frac{1}{N}\sum_{n=1}^{N} |X_{:, n}| \;\in\; \mathbb{R}^C, \qquad (s_X)_c = \frac{1}{N}\sum_{n=1}^{N} |X_{c, n}|.$$

    It then ties all $C$ channel scales to a single exponent $\alpha \in [0, 1]$:

    $$s(\alpha) = s_X^{\,\alpha} \quad \Longleftrightarrow \quad s_c(\alpha) = \bigl((s_X)_c\bigr)^\alpha, \qquad \alpha \in [0, 1].$$

    Here $\alpha = 0$ gives $s = \mathbf{1}$ (plain round-to-nearest, no scaling), $\alpha = 1$ gives $s = s_X$ (full activation-proportional scaling), and intermediate values $\alpha \in (0, 1)$ balance protecting salient channels against inflating the group step size $\Delta'$. This reduces a $C$-dimensional optimization problem to a fast 20-point grid search over $\alpha \in [0, 1]$.

    Algorithm 1: Activation-aware Weight Quantization (AWQ) for a Linear Layer
    1. Inputs: weight matrix $W \in \mathbb{R}^{D \times C}$, calibration activations $X \in \mathbb{R}^{C \times N}$, bit-width $b$ (e.g., $4$), group size $G$ (e.g., $128$), grid steps $K = 20$.
    2. Compute activation importance: for each input channel $c \in \{1, \dots, C\}$, compute $$s_{X,c} = \frac{1}{N}\sum_{n=1}^{N} |X_{c,n}|.$$
    3. Grid-search the optimal scaling exponent $\alpha^*$: initialize $\mathcal{L}_{\min} = \infty$. For $k = 0, 1, \dots, K$:
      • Set $\alpha = k / K \in [0, 1]$ and compute raw scales $\tilde{s} = s_X^{\,\alpha}$.
      • Normalize $\tilde{s}$ by its geometric range so scales stay centered around $1$: $$s(\alpha) = \frac{\tilde{s}}{\sqrt{\max(\tilde{s})\cdot\min(\tilde{s})}}.$$
      • Quantize and reconstruct the scaled weights group-by-group: $\widehat{W}(\alpha) = Q\bigl(W \operatorname{diag}(s(\alpha))\bigr)\operatorname{diag}(s(\alpha))^{-1}$.
      • Evaluate the layer output MSE on calibration tokens: $$\mathcal{L}(\alpha) = \bigl\lVert \widehat{W}(\alpha) X - W X \bigr\rVert_F^2.$$
      • If $\mathcal{L}(\alpha) < \mathcal{L}_{\min}$, update $\mathcal{L}_{\min} \leftarrow \mathcal{L}(\alpha)$ and $s^* \leftarrow s(\alpha)$.
    4. Apply optimal scaling: replace $W \leftarrow W \operatorname{diag}(s^*)$ and fold $(s^*)^{-1}$ into the preceding operation (for example, updating the preceding RMSNorm/LayerNorm weight vector $\gamma \leftarrow \gamma \oslash s^*$).
    5. Optional per-group weight clipping search: for each group $g$ of $G$ weights in the scaled matrix, test clipping ratios $\gamma \in \{1.00, 0.95, \dots, 0.50\}$ (clamping $|w| \le \gamma \max_{j \in g}|w_j|$ before setting $\Delta_g$) and pick $\gamma_g^*$ that minimizes $\lVert Q_\gamma(w_g) X_g - w_g X_g \rVert_2^2$.
    6. Output: packed $b$-bit integer weights $W_q$ and per-group FP16 step sizes $\Delta_g$ (plus zero-points for asymmetric grids).

    4. Step-by-step numerical walk-through of the algorithm

    Let's run Algorithm 1 by hand on a single 4-bit symmetric group ($b = 4$, integer codes $-7$ to $7$) with $C = 4$ input channels:

    $$w = [0.14,\; 0.15,\; -0.40,\; 0.70].$$

    Suppose our calibration pass reveals that channel $1$ is an outlier activation channel with mean magnitude $9.0$, while channels $2, 3, 4$ have mean magnitude $1.0$:

    $$s_X = [9.0,\; 1.0,\; 1.0,\; 1.0], \qquad x = [9.0,\; 1.0,\; 1.0,\; 1.0]^{\mathsf T}.$$

    First compute the exact floating-point output $y = w x$:

    $$y = 0.14(9.0) + 0.15(1.0) - 0.40(1.0) + 0.70(1.0) = 1.26 + 0.15 - 0.40 + 0.70 = 1.71.$$

    Now evaluate the grid search over three representative candidates $\alpha \in \{0.0,\, 0.5,\, 1.0\}$ (leaving out the scalar geometric-mean normalization factor since uniform rescaling of a group cancels in $\Delta$):

    • Candidate $\alpha = 0.0$ (No scaling, standard RTN):
      • Scale vector: $s(0) = s_X^{\,0} = [1,\, 1,\, 1,\, 1]$.
      • Group maximum is $0.70$, so the shared step size is $\Delta = 0.70 / 7 = 0.10$.
      • Quantized weights: $Q(w) = [0.10,\; 0.20,\; -0.40,\; 0.70]$.
      • Reconstructed output: $\widehat{y}(0) = 0.10(9.0) + 0.20(1.0) - 0.40(1.0) + 0.70(1.0) = 1.40$.
      • Squared output error: $\mathcal{L}(0) = (1.40 - 1.71)^2 = (-0.31)^2 = \mathbf{0.0961}$. Rounding $w_1 = 0.14$ to $0.10$ created a $-0.04$ weight error that got multiplied by $x_1 = 9.0$ into a $-0.36$ output shift.
    • Candidate $\alpha = 0.5$ (Moderate AWQ scaling — the sweet spot):
      • Scale vector: $s(0.5) = s_X^{\,0.5} = [\sqrt{9},\, 1,\, 1,\, 1] = [3,\, 1,\, 1,\, 1]$.
      • Scaled weights: $w \odot s = [0.42,\; 0.15,\; -0.40,\; 0.70]$.
      • Because $0.42 < 0.70$, the group maximum is still $0.70$, so $\Delta' = 0.70 / 7 = 0.10$ is completely unchanged.
      • On the $\Delta' = 0.10$ grid, $0.42$ rounds to $0.40$, giving $Q(w \odot s) = [0.40,\; 0.20,\; -0.40,\; 0.70]$ and effective weights $Q(w \odot s) \oslash s = [0.1333,\; 0.20,\; -0.40,\; 0.70]$.
      • Reconstructed output: $\widehat{y}(0.5) = 0.40(9.0/3) + 0.20(1.0) - 0.40(1.0) + 0.70(1.0) = 1.20 + 0.20 - 0.40 + 0.70 = 1.70$.
      • Squared output error: $\mathcal{L}(0.5) = (1.70 - 1.71)^2 = (-0.01)^2 = \mathbf{0.0001}$—a $961\times$ reduction in squared error over RTN.
    • Candidate $\alpha = 1.0$ (Over-scaling):
      • Scale vector: $s(1.0) = s_X^{\,1.0} = [9,\, 1,\, 1,\, 1]$.
      • Scaled weights: $w \odot s = [1.26,\; 0.15,\; -0.40,\; 0.70]$.
      • Channel $1$ now overtakes $0.70$ as the group maximum ($1.26$), inflating the shared step size by $1.8\times$ to $\Delta' = 1.26 / 7 = 0.18$.
      • On the coarser $\Delta' = 0.18$ grid, the scaled weights round to $[7,\, 1,\, -2,\, 4] \times 0.18 = [1.26,\; 0.18,\; -0.36,\; 0.72]$.
      • Reconstructed output: $\widehat{y}(1.0) = 1.26(9.0/9) + 0.18(1.0) - 0.36(1.0) + 0.72(1.0) = 1.80$.
      • Squared output error: $\mathcal{L}(1.0) = (1.80 - 1.71)^2 = (+0.09)^2 = \mathbf{0.0081}$—$81\times$ worse than $\alpha = 0.5$ because inflating $\Delta'$ degraded channels $2$, $3$, and $4$.

    5. How AWQ compares to LLM.int8, SmoothQuant, and GPTQ

    Comparison of post-training quantization methods for large language models.
    MethodTarget formatHow it handles outlier channelsCalibration procedure
    LLM.int8W8A8 + FP16 branchSplits outlier activation channels into a separate FP16 matrix multiplication at runtime.None (dynamic runtime threshold).
    SmoothQuantW8A8Rescales $s_c = (\max|X_c|)^\alpha / (\max|W_c|)^{1-\alpha}$ so both weights and activations fit in INT8.Records peak activation and weight ranges ($\alpha \approx 0.5$).
    GPTQW4A16 / W3A16Quantizes columns sequentially and adjusts remaining weights via the inverse Hessian $H^{-1} = (2XX^{\mathsf T})^{-1}$.Solves sequential Cholesky/Hessian updates across columns.
    AWQW4A16 / W3A16Scales weights by $s = s_X^{\,\alpha}$ before uniform low-bit rounding so salient channels get a finer effective step $\Delta'/s_c$.20-point 1D grid search over $\alpha \in [0, 1]$ + optional clipping ratio $\gamma$.
    Why does AWQ often generalize better to out-of-distribution tasks than GPTQ at very low bit-widths?

    GPTQ actively modifies unquantized weights using $H^{-1} = (2XX^{\mathsf T})^{-1}$ to cancel rounding errors on the calibration set, which can overfit if the calibration set is small or narrow. AWQ never alters the underlying floating-point function $W X = (W S)(S^{-1} X)$ before rounding; it only searches two scalar parameters ($\alpha$ and $\gamma$) to choose a better quantization grid.
