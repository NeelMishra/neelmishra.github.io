    RTN: Round-to-Nearest quantization from first principles

    October 2026 · 5 min read

    Round-to-Nearest (RTN) is the foundational baseline of neural network quantization. Every more sophisticated post-training quantizer—whether GPTQ, AWQ, or SmoothQuant—still uses an RTN step under the hood; they differ only in how they rescale or compensate weights before and after calling RTN.

    Given a block of floating-point weights $w \in \mathbb{R}^G$ and a target bit-width $b$, RTN requires zero calibration data, zero forward passes, and zero optimization loops. It maps each weight independently to its closest point on a uniform integer grid in four steps:

    1. Select the scale granularity: partition the weight matrix $W \in \mathbb{R}^{m \times d}$ into independent scaling blocks—per-tensor, per-output-channel (row), or per-group of $G$ consecutive weights (typically $G = 128$).
    2. Compute the grid step $\Delta$ (and zero-point $z$): measure the block's extreme values ($M = \max_i |w_i|$ for symmetric grids, or $[w_{\min}, w_{\max}]$ for asymmetric grids) and divide that span into $2^b - 1$ (or $2^{b-1}-1$) equal intervals of width $\Delta$.
    3. Divide, round to nearest integer, and clip: map each weight $w_i$ independently to the integer code $q_i = \operatorname{clip}\!\bigl(\lfloor w_i / \Delta \rceil + z,\, q_{\min},\, q_{\max}\bigr)$ using round-half-to-even tie-breaking.
    4. Dequantize during inference: reconstruct $\widehat{w}_i = \Delta\cdot(q_i - z)$ on the fly inside the matrix-multiply kernel (for W4A16/W8A16) or accumulate directly in INT32 (for W8A8).

    What problem does RTN actually solve?

    Once a block's step size $\Delta > 0$ and integer zero-point $z$ are fixed, the allowed reconstruction levels form a discrete uniform grid:

    $$\mathcal{G}_{\Delta, z} = \bigl\{\,\Delta\cdot(q - z) \;\big|\; q \in \{q_{\min}, \dots, q_{\max}\}\,\bigr\}.$$

    RTN chooses the quantized matrix $\widehat{W} \in \mathcal{G}_{\Delta, z}^{m \times d}$ that minimizes the elementwise Frobenius reconstruction error of the weights themselves:

    $$\widehat{W}_{\mathrm{RTN}} = \operatorname*{arg\,min}_{\widehat{W} \in \mathcal{G}_{\Delta, z}^{m \times d}} \lVert W - \widehat{W} \rVert_F^2 = \sum_{r=1}^{m}\sum_{c=1}^{d} \bigl(W_{r,c} - \widehat{W}_{r,c}\bigr)^2.$$

    Because $\lVert W - \widehat{W} \rVert_F^2$ is a sum of independent per-weight squares $(W_{r,c} - \widehat{W}_{r,c})^2$, minimizing the sum is equivalent to snapping each scalar $W_{r,c}$ to its single closest grid point in $\mathcal{G}_{\Delta, z}$. That decoupling is both RTN's greatest strength (instant, embarrassingly parallel quantization) and its central weakness at 3–4 bits (it ignores which weights multiply large activations and cannot use unquantized columns to cancel rounding error).

    The distinctions to keep straight

    • Weight-space MSE (RTN) versus output-space MSE (GPTQ, AWQ): RTN minimizes $\lVert W - \widehat{W} \rVert_F^2$ without looking at activations $X$. GPTQ and AWQ minimize $\lVert WX - \widehat{W}X \rVert_F^2$ on a calibration set.
    • Symmetric absmax versus asymmetric min-max: symmetric RTN forces $z = 0$ so zero is exact and dequantization requires only a multiply ($\widehat{w} = \Delta\cdot q$), whereas asymmetric RTN shifts the grid by $z$ to fit skewed intervals $[w_{\min}, w_{\max}]$ tightly at the cost of storing $z$ and computing $\Delta(q - z)$.
    • Rounding error versus clipping error: inside the grid, $|\widehat{w} - w| \le \Delta/2$. If $\Delta$ is chosen smaller than the true min-max span to improve interior resolution, weights outside the grid saturate at $q_{\min}$ or $q_{\max}$ and incur unbounded clipping error.
    • Per-channel versus per-group RTN: per-channel RTN stores one scale per output row ($m$ scales total). Groupwise RTN stores one scale every $G$ input columns ($m \times d / G$ scales), trading a small metadata overhead ($0.125$ to $0.25$ bits/weight) for a dramatic drop in 4-bit quantization error.

    A quick numerical check

    In Part 2's 8-weight example, a single outlier weight $w_8 = 1.40$ sits alongside seven small weights in $[-0.21, +0.22]$. Under 4-bit symmetric per-row RTN ($\Delta = 1.40/7 = 0.20$), three of the seven small weights round to $0.00$. Splitting the row into two groups of $G = 4$ gives the first group $\Delta_1 = 0.21 / 7 = 0.03$, reconstructing all four weights in Group 1 with zero error.
