    Quantize one slice, then repair the rest

    October 2026 · 9 min read

    The setting in the video is an LLM that is already trained and already too slow. The weights are floating point. The job is inference on a GPU, not a requirement that the model run only on a CPU, and not a determinism constraint. Quantization is the tool because the weight tensor is what has to move.

    The wait is the bytes, not the multiply

    Savolainen's description matches the paper's limitation sentence. You quantize so fewer weight bytes cross into the part of the GPU that will use them. The multiply often happens after those values have been cast back to floating point. The paper is explicit that, on the hardware they targeted, there was no speedup for the multiplications themselves, because mainstream GPUs did not have mixed-precision operands such as FP16 times INT4. Their kernels still got about 3.25 times on an A100 and 4.5 times on an A6000, from loading the compressed weights faster.

    That split matters when you read a benchmark. A smaller checkpoint, a faster load, and a faster matmul are three measurements. GPTQ, as published, is evidence for the first two.

    The video also separates three waits inside a GPU program, with a paperwork analogy that is worth keeping in plain language:

    • Compute-bound: the arithmetic is the wait.
    • Memory-bound: moving values already on the machine is the wait.
    • I/O-bound: getting values to the machine and back is the wait.

    Large-language-model inference, in the regime this method targets, is memory-bound. The later speedups inside GPTQ itself, the lazy batch updates, are an answer to that same wait. They show up in the third note.

    One scale for the tensor is a coarse grid

    A quantizer needs a scale, and usually a zero, to turn a floating-point number into an integer and back. One scale for an entire weight tensor is legal. It is also blunt: a single range has to cover every row, including the outliers.

    Per-channel scales are the usual step up. Per-row scales, or scales shared by a group of input channels, spend a few extra parameters for a tighter fit. The video's point is that you may accept that extra tuple if the reconstruction error drops enough. Nothing in that choice requires the Hessian update. Round-to-nearest can be per-row too. The group size is a property of the grid, not of GPTQ's solver.

    Keep it separate from the other 128 you will see. Algorithm 1 quantizes 128 columns before it writes the update into the rest of the matrix. A group size of 128, which the authors' code exposes as groupsize, means 128 columns share a scale and a zero. Same number, different object. The basic algorithm in the paper fixes the grid before the solve and does not require those groups.

    The move that makes it GPTQ's ancestor

    Round-to-nearest snaps each weight and leaves the others alone. The idea the video builds toward is more ambitious, and it is older than GPTQ:

    1. Snap one slice of the weight matrix onto the grid.
    2. Move the weights that are still full precision so the layer output moves back toward the original output.
    3. Repeat until nothing full precision remains.

    Each snap adds error. The update spends the remaining degrees of freedom to buy some of that error back. When you reach the last slice, there is nothing left to move. That is why the order is not a cosmetic choice, and why the third note spends so long on it.

    The video says "row" for the slice you snap. The paper's layout is more specific. Under $Y=WX$, each row of $W$ is one output unit, and the Hessian of that row depends only on $X$, not on the row itself. OBQ therefore solves each row independently, and inside a row it picks a single weight, not the whole row. GPTQ then quantizes column $j$ across every row at the same step, because every row shares that Hessian. The next note uses "column" in that sense. If a sentence in the video says "row", read it as "the slice being snapped", and check the index against equation (2) before you implement it.

    Three papers, one objective, then a faster solve

    The compensation idea is not native to transformers. Hassibi, Stork, and Wolff's Optimal Brain Surgeon (1993) used it for pruning: set a weight to zero, then adjust the others so the training loss barely moves. The Hessian note derives that equation: drop the gradient at a local minimum, keep the quadratic, enforce $e_q^{\mathsf T}\delta w+w_q=0$ with a Lagrangian, and get both the update and the saliency $L_q=\tfrac12 w_q^2/[H^{-1}]_{qq}$. Quantization uses the same Lagrangian with the grid point in place of 0.

    Optimal Brain Compression, and the OBQ method inside it, keeps the second-order update and changes the target. A pruned weight has one legal value, zero. A quantized weight has one legal value per grid point. For 8-bit weights that is 256 possibilities; for 4-bit, 16. You snap to the nearest grid point, then compensate. You do not get to pick any real number.

    GPTQ does not replace that objective. The paper says so by building the section "The GPTQ Algorithm" as three modifications of OBQ. What changed is the cost. OBQ's runtime on a $d_{\text{row}}\times d_{\text{col}}$ matrix is $O(d_{\text{row}}\cdot d_{\text{col}}^3)$. The video's practical translation: hours or days on a large model. GPTQ's translation, measured: about four GPU hours for OPT-175B and BLOOM-176B, to 3 or 4 bits, with the perplexity curves in Figure 1 staying near FP16 where round-to-nearest blows up.

    The video says the speedup is as much as three orders of magnitude, and that a large model can finish in a couple of minutes. The paper's wording is "more than three orders of magnitude" against OBQ, and "approximately four GPU hours" for those two models. Use the paper's clock when you quote a runtime. The minutes figure is the video's, and it is looser than the experiment.

    The loop: feed, store the MSE, quantize the easiest row, adjust the rest

    At 06:56 the video stops the history and states the procedure. This is the loop. Do not skip ahead to Cholesky until these four steps are obvious.

    Step 2 is not a single MSE for the whole layer, and it is not a log you discard. A layer-level number cannot choose a row. You store an error for each row that is still full precision. The smallest of those stored errors is the row you quantize in step 3. Step 4 then changes the remaining floating-point weights, so the errors you stored are no longer the right ones. Measure again on the reduced problem, or update the stored errors with the same Hessian that did the adjustment. Then repeat.

    You do not have to quantize every remaining row, run a separate forward, and write those MSEs to disk. The error you store for a row is the extra output MSE of snapping that row and applying the optimal adjustment. With $H=2XX^{\mathsf T}$, that extra error is $(\operatorname{quant}(w)-w)^2/[H^{-1}]_{qq}$, up to the factor of one half explained in the next note. The inverse Hessian is how the measurement is stored in the algorithm. It is still the MSE the video is talking about: floating-point output against quantized output, after the compensation that step 4 will apply.

    The three-weight example in the next note is one pass of this loop. The stored errors are 0.020806, 0.006181, and 0.019105. The smallest is the middle weight, $-0.85$ snapping to $-1$. That is the row you quantize. The adjustment of the other two floating-point weights is $(1.20,-0.85,0.40)\rightarrow(1.305629,-1,0.382805)$. Output squared error falls from 0.05265, if you snap and do not adjust, to 0.00309 after the adjustment.

    The video says "row". Under $Y=WX$ the independent problem is one output row of $W$, and the entry you snap inside that row is one weight, a column of the row. GPTQ later snaps that same column in every output row at once, because every row shares $H$. If you are implementing from the video, the four steps are the spec. If you are implementing from equation (2), the index you minimize is a weight inside the row, and the video's "row" is that slice. Either reading still feeds data, stores an MSE per candidate, quantizes the minimum, and adjusts what is left.

    GPTQ's change comes after this loop, not instead of it. From 19:57 the video argues that always picking the smallest error leaves the hardest rows until the end, when almost no floating-point weights remain to adjust. That tail-loads the error. So GPTQ keeps steps 1, 2, and 4, and replaces the search in step 3 with a fixed order. You still adjust the remaining floating-point weights after every row you quantize. The third note is that replacement, and why a fixed order lets you precompute the inverse. It is not a different reconstruction problem.

    The grid, one scale for the tensor or one per row or group, is chosen before the loop. It decides what "quantize" snaps to. It is not a fifth step inside the loop.

    If the matmul is still in floating point, why quantize at all?

    Because the weights still have to be stored and loaded. The paper's speedups are from that load. If your profiler says the matmul is the wait, and the hardware cannot use the low-bit operands, weight-only GPTQ will not fix that wait.

    Is a per-row scale the same thing as GPTQ?

    No. A finer scale reduces rounding error before any compensation. GPTQ is the compensation, plus the schedule that makes the compensation cheap. You can round to nearest on a per-row grid and never touch a Hessian.

    Does the floating-point model have to be at a training minimum?

    For pruning, OBS assumed that, so the gradient of the training loss was treated as zero. For this quantization objective the full-precision weights already have zero reconstruction error. They are the minimum of $\lVert WX-\widehat W X\rVert^2$ by construction. You do not need a separate claim that training had fully converged.
