    Quantize one slice, then repair the rest

    October 2026 · 7 min read

    The setting in the video is an LLM that is already trained and already too slow. The weights are floating point. The job is inference on a GPU, not a requirement that the model run only on a CPU, and not a determinism constraint. Quantization is the tool because the weight tensor is what has to move.

    The wait is the bytes, not the multiply

    Savolainen's description matches the paper's limitation sentence. You quantize so fewer weight bytes cross into the part of the GPU that will use them. The multiply often happens after those values have been cast back to floating point. The paper is explicit that, on the hardware they targeted, there was no speedup for the multiplications themselves, because mainstream GPUs did not have mixed-precision operands such as FP16 times INT4. Their kernels still got about 3.25 times on an A100 and 4.5 times on an A6000, from loading the compressed weights faster.

    That split matters when you read a benchmark. A smaller checkpoint, a faster load, and a faster matmul are three measurements. GPTQ, as published, is evidence for the first two.

    The video also separates three waits inside a GPU program, with a paperwork analogy that is worth keeping in plain language:

    • Compute-bound: the arithmetic is the wait.
    • Memory-bound: moving values already on the machine is the wait.
    • I/O-bound: getting values to the machine and back is the wait.

    Large-language-model inference, in the regime this method targets, is memory-bound. The later speedups inside GPTQ itself, the lazy batch updates, are an answer to that same wait. They show up in lazy batches.

    One scale for the tensor is a coarse grid

    A quantizer needs a scale, and usually a zero, to turn a floating-point number into an integer and back. One scale for an entire weight tensor is legal. It is also blunt: a single range has to cover every row, including the outliers.

    Per-channel scales are the usual step up. Per-row scales, or scales shared by a group of input channels, spend a few extra parameters for a tighter fit. The video's point is that you may accept that extra tuple if the reconstruction error drops enough. Nothing in that choice requires the Hessian update. Round-to-nearest can be per-row too. The group size is a property of the grid, not of GPTQ's solver.

    Keep it separate from the other 128 you will see. Algorithm 1 quantizes 128 columns before it writes the update into the rest of the matrix. A group size of 128, which the authors' code exposes as groupsize, means 128 columns share a scale and a zero. Same number, different object. The basic algorithm in the paper fixes the grid before the solve and does not require those groups.

    The move that makes it GPTQ's ancestor

    Round-to-nearest snaps each weight and leaves the others alone. The idea the video builds toward is more ambitious, and it is older than GPTQ:

    1. Snap one slice of the weight matrix onto the grid.
    2. Move the weights that are still full precision so the layer output moves back toward the original output.
    3. Repeat until nothing full precision remains.

    Each snap adds error. The update spends the remaining degrees of freedom to buy some of that error back. When you reach the last slice, there is nothing left to move. That is why the order is not a cosmetic choice, and why the fixed-order note spends its time on it.

    The video says "row" for the slice you snap. The paper's layout is more specific. Under $Y=WX$, each row of $W$ is one output unit, and the Hessian of that row depends only on $X$, not on the row itself. OBQ therefore solves each row independently, and inside a row it picks a single weight, not the whole row. GPTQ then quantizes column $j$ across every row at the same step, because every row shares that Hessian. The Hessian note uses "column" in that sense. If a sentence in the video says "row", read it as "the slice being snapped", and check the index against equation (2) before you implement it.

    Three papers, one objective, then a faster solve

    The compensation idea is not native to transformers. Hassibi, Stork, and Wolff's Optimal Brain Surgeon (1993) used it for pruning: set a weight to zero, then adjust the others so the training loss barely moves. The OBS note derives that equation: drop the gradient at a local minimum, keep the quadratic, enforce $e_q^{\mathsf T}\delta w+w_q=0$ with a Lagrangian, and get both the update and the saliency $L_q=\tfrac12 w_q^2/[H^{-1}]_{qq}$. Quantization uses the same Lagrangian with the grid point in place of 0.

    Optimal Brain Compression, and the OBQ method inside it, keeps the second-order update and changes the target. A pruned weight has one legal value, zero. A quantized weight has one legal value per grid point. For 8-bit weights that is 256 possibilities; for 4-bit, 16. You snap to the nearest grid point, then compensate. You do not get to pick any real number.

    GPTQ does not replace that objective. The paper says so by building the section "The GPTQ Algorithm" as three modifications of OBQ. What changed is the cost. OBQ's runtime on a $d_{\text{row}}\times d_{\text{col}}$ matrix is $O(d_{\text{row}}\cdot d_{\text{col}}^3)$. The video's practical translation: hours or days on a large model. GPTQ's translation, measured: about four GPU hours for OPT-175B and BLOOM-176B, to 3 or 4 bits, with the perplexity curves in Figure 1 staying near FP16 where round-to-nearest blows up.

    The video says the speedup is as much as three orders of magnitude, and that a large model can finish in a couple of minutes. The paper's wording is "more than three orders of magnitude" against OBQ, and "approximately four GPU hours" for those two models. Use the paper's clock when you quote a runtime. The minutes figure is the video's, and it is looser than the experiment.
