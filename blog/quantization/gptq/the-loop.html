    Feed the layer, store the MSE, quantize the easiest row

    October 2026 · 6 min read

    The previous note is why you are quantizing a layer at all. This note is the procedure the video states at 06:56, before any of the speedups.

    The loop: feed, store the MSE, quantize the easiest row, adjust the rest

    At 06:56 the video stops the history and states the procedure. This is the loop. Do not skip ahead to Cholesky until these four steps are obvious.

    Step 2 is not a single MSE for the whole layer, and it is not a log you discard. A layer-level number cannot choose a row. You store an error for each row that is still full precision. The smallest of those stored errors is the row you quantize in step 3. Step 4 then changes the remaining floating-point weights, so the errors you stored are no longer the right ones. Measure again on the reduced problem, or update the stored errors with the same Hessian that did the adjustment. Then repeat.

    You do not have to quantize every remaining row, run a separate forward, and write those MSEs to disk. The error you store for a row is the extra output MSE of snapping that row and applying the optimal adjustment. With $H=2XX^{\mathsf T}$, that extra error is $(\operatorname{quant}(w)-w)^2/[H^{-1}]_{qq}$, up to the factor of one half explained in the OBS note. The inverse Hessian is how the measurement is stored in the algorithm. It is still the MSE the video is talking about: floating-point output against quantized output, after the compensation that step 4 will apply.

    The three-weight example in the worked update is one pass of this loop. The stored errors are 0.020806, 0.006181, and 0.019105. The smallest is the middle weight, $-0.85$ snapping to $-1$. That is the row you quantize. The adjustment of the other two floating-point weights is $(1.20,-0.85,0.40)\rightarrow(1.305629,-1,0.382805)$. Output squared error falls from 0.05265, if you snap and do not adjust, to 0.00309 after the adjustment.

    The video says "row". Under $Y=WX$ the independent problem is one output row of $W$, and the entry you snap inside that row is one weight, a column of the row. GPTQ later snaps that same column in every output row at once, because every row shares $H$. If you are implementing from the video, the four steps are the spec. If you are implementing from equation (2), the index you minimize is a weight inside the row, and the video's "row" is that slice. Either reading still feeds data, stores an MSE per candidate, quantizes the minimum, and adjusts what is left.

    GPTQ's change comes after this loop, not instead of it. From 19:57 the video argues that always picking the smallest error leaves the hardest rows until the end, when almost no floating-point weights remain to adjust. That tail-loads the error. So GPTQ keeps steps 1, 2, and 4, and replaces the search in step 3 with a fixed order. You still adjust the remaining floating-point weights after every row you quantize. The fixed-order note is that replacement, and why a fixed order lets you precompute the inverse. It is not a different reconstruction problem.

    The grid, one scale for the tensor or one per row or group, is chosen before the loop. It decides what "quantize" snaps to. It is not a fifth step inside the loop.

    If the matmul is still in floating point, why quantize at all?

    Because the weights still have to be stored and loaded. The paper's speedups are from that load. If your profiler says the matmul is the wait, and the hardware cannot use the low-bit operands, weight-only GPTQ will not fix that wait.

    Is a per-row scale the same thing as GPTQ?

    No. A finer scale reduces rounding error before any compensation. GPTQ is the compensation, plus the schedule that makes the compensation cheap. You can round to nearest on a per-row grid and never touch a Hessian.

    Does the floating-point model have to be at a training minimum?

    For pruning, OBS assumed that, so the gradient of the training loss was treated as zero. For this quantization objective the full-precision weights already have zero reconstruction error. They are the minimum of $\lVert WX-\widehat W X\rVert^2$ by construction. You do not need a separate claim that training had fully converged.
