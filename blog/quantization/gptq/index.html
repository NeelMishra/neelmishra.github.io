    GPTQ: quantize the layer, then compensate

    October 2026 · 6 min read

    GPTQ solves the same layer problem as Optimal Brain Quantization. The loop the video states, before any of the speedups, is the thing to keep in front of you. For one layer, and then again until nothing full precision remains:

    1. Feed calibration data through the layer.
    2. Measure the quantization error as the mean squared error between the floating-point output and the quantized output, and store that error. The next step reads it.
    3. Quantize the row with the smallest stored error. That is the easiest row.
    4. Adjust the remaining floating-point weights so the layer output moves back toward the original output. The adjustment uses the Hessian.

    That is the procedure at 06:56 in the walkthrough. GPTQ does not replace it with a new objective. It later stops searching for the easiest row and quantizes in a fixed order, because a greedy pass is too slow and leaves the hard rows until nothing is left to adjust. It still feeds data, still uses that MSE, and still adjusts the remaining floating-point weights after each snap. The schedule around the loop is a fixed column order, a precomputed inverse Hessian, updates in blocks of 128 columns, and a Cholesky factor.

    That schedule is why the paper can quantize OPT-175B and BLOOM-176B in about four GPU hours. The video describes the same speedup more loosely, as minutes and as much as three orders of magnitude. The paper's measured figure for those two models is the four hours. The "more than three orders of magnitude" claim is the comparison with OBQ's cubic pass, not with round-to-nearest.

    One layer, stated once

    Take a weight matrix $W$ and the layer inputs $X$ from a calibration set. The paper writes the objective as

    $$\operatorname{argmin}_{\widehat W}\lVert WX-\widehat W X\rVert_2^2.$$

    That formula assumes the weights multiply on the left. A PyTorch linear layer multiplies on the right, $Y=XW_{\text{torch}}$. The two agree after you transpose the activation tensor. The Hessian note is where that orientation matters, because the wrong orientation builds a Hessian of the wrong shape.

    The stored error is one number per remaining row, not one number for the whole layer. A single layer MSE cannot tell you which row to quantize. In the method, the number you store for a row is the extra output MSE of snapping that row and applying the optimal adjustment. The inverse Hessian produces it, as $(\operatorname{quant}(w)-w)^2/[H^{-1}]_{qq}$, so you do not quantize every row, run a separate forward, and write those MSEs out by hand. The worked example stores three of them: 0.020806, 0.006181, and 0.019105. The smallest is the middle weight, so that is the row you quantize, and the other two are the floating-point weights you adjust.

    The video says "row" for the slice you snap. Under the paper's layout the independent problem is one output row, and the entry you snap inside it is one weight. GPTQ later snaps the same column of every output row together, because those rows share the Hessian. The four steps stay the same either way. The first note writes the loop out, including what changes after GPTQ drops the search in step 3.

    A check before the algebra

    Round-to-nearest picks each weight's grid point independently. GPTQ's update can move a weight that has not been quantized yet, so two weights that started on opposite sides of a grid boundary can end on different points than a naive snap would choose. The worked example in the Hessian note is small enough to recompute: five input rows, three weights, and a grid of halves. Compensating the other two weights cuts the output squared error from 0.05265 to 0.00309. tools/check_gptq_update.py checks that number against a constrained least-squares solve.

    Is GPTQ the same algorithm as the MLOps inference note?

    Same method, different job. The inference note is the serving summary. These notes are the derivation, including the layout transpose the paper's formula needs and the block update the paper actually runs.

    Does a good GPTQ reconstruction mean the generated text is unchanged?

    No. The objective is layer output error on the calibration inputs. Perplexity on WikiText-2, which the paper reports, is a further check. A task the calibration set never resembles can still move.
