    GPTQ: quantize the layer, then compensate

    October 2026 · 5 min read

    GPTQ solves the same layer problem as Optimal Brain Quantization. For each linear layer, find low-bit weights whose output on calibration inputs stays close to the full-precision output. The new part is the schedule: a fixed column order, a precomputed inverse Hessian, updates applied in blocks of 128 columns, and a Cholesky factor instead of repeated elimination.

    That schedule is why the paper can quantize OPT-175B and BLOOM-176B in about four GPU hours. The video describes the same speedup more loosely, as minutes and as much as three orders of magnitude. The paper's measured figure for those two models is the four hours. The "more than three orders of magnitude" claim is the comparison with OBQ's cubic pass, not with round-to-nearest.

    One layer, stated once

    Take a weight matrix $W$ and the layer inputs $X$ from a calibration set. The paper writes the objective as

    $$\operatorname{argmin}_{\widehat W}\lVert WX-\widehat W X\rVert_2^2.$$

    That formula assumes the weights multiply on the left. A PyTorch linear layer multiplies on the right, $Y=XW_{\text{torch}}$. The two agree after you transpose the activation tensor. The Hessian note is where that orientation matters, because the wrong orientation builds a Hessian of the wrong shape.

    OBQ then quantizes one weight at a time, always picking the weight whose snap to the grid adds the least error, and moves the remaining full-precision weights to absorb that error. GPTQ keeps the move and drops the search for the easiest weight.

GPTQ tracks FP16 on those plots where round-to-nearest does not. That is a perplexity result, not a claim that every downstream task is unchanged.

    A check before the algebra

    Round-to-nearest picks each weight's grid point independently. GPTQ's update can move a weight that has not been quantized yet, so two weights that started on opposite sides of a grid boundary can end on different points than a naive snap would choose. The worked example in the Hessian note is small enough to recompute: five input rows, three weights, and a grid of halves. Compensating the other two weights cuts the output squared error from 0.05265 to 0.00309. tools/check_gptq_update.py checks that number against a constrained least-squares solve.

    Is GPTQ the same algorithm as the MLOps inference note?

    Same method, different job. The inference note is the serving summary. These notes are the derivation, including the layout transpose the paper's formula needs and the block update the paper actually runs.

    Does a good GPTQ reconstruction mean the generated text is unchanged?

    No. The objective is layer output error on the calibration inputs. Perplexity on WikiText-2, which the paper reports, is a further check. A task the calibration set never resembles can still move.
