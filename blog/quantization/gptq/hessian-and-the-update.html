    GPTQ: derive and check the Hessian update

    October 2026 · 8 min read

    The question is precise: if one weight must land on a grid point, how should the remaining weights move to keep the outputs as close as possible? We will solve that constrained least-squares problem, check it with numbers, then prepare the next step.

    One layer, two ways to arrange the samples

    Use $A$ for a calibration table with $n$ samples as rows and $d$ input features as columns. The weight matrix $W$ has $m$ output rows and $d$ input columns. $Y$ is the output table, with one row per sample. These are the shapes used by PyTorch nn.Linear:

    $$A\in\mathbb R^{n\times d},\qquad W\in\mathbb R^{m\times d},\qquad Y=AW^{\mathsf T}\in\mathbb R^{n\times m}.$$

    Each output row of $W$ defines a separate reconstruction problem. Write that row as a length-$d$ column vector $w$ and its quantized perturbation as $\delta=\widehat w-w\in\mathbb R^d$. Since the output change is $A\delta$, the squared reconstruction error for that channel is the quadratic form $E(\delta)=\lVert A\delta\rVert_2^2=\delta^{\mathsf T}(A^{\mathsf T}A)\delta$. Differentiating twice with respect to $\delta$ yields the gradient and the $d\times d$ Hessian matrix $H$:

    $$\nabla_\delta E(\delta)=\frac{\partial\lVert A\delta\rVert_2^2}{\partial\delta}=2A^{\mathsf T}A\delta,\qquad H=\nabla_\delta^2 E(\delta)=\frac{\partial^2\lVert A\delta\rVert_2^2}{\partial\delta\,\partial\delta^{\mathsf T}}=2A^{\mathsf T}A.$$

    The paper instead puts samples in columns. Set $X=A^{\mathsf T}$, with shape $d\times n$. Its left-multiplied output is the transpose of our output table. The Hessian $H$ is the $d\times d$ second-derivative matrix of one output channel's squared reconstruction error:

    $$WX=WA^{\mathsf T}=Y^{\mathsf T},\qquad H=2A^{\mathsf T}A=2XX^{\mathsf T}\in\mathbb R^{d\times d},\qquad H_{ij}=2\sum_{s=1}^{n}A_{s,i}A_{s,j}.$$

    Both products for $H$ describe input-feature correlations (depending only on the calibration inputs, never on the weights of a specific row). Computing $2AA^{\mathsf T}$ instead would give an $n\times n$ sample-correlation matrix, not the weight-coordinate Hessian.

    The authors' add_batch transposes linear inputs before accumulating inp @ inp.T. It also normalizes its accumulation by the number of calibration examples seen. Below we use the unnormalized sum $H=2A^{\mathsf T}A$ so the numerical costs are easy to check.

    The quadratic is exact here

    GPTQ updates the weight vector $w \in \mathbb{R}^d$ in place, one column at a time. Let $y = Aw^{(0)} \in \mathbb{R}^n$ denote the original full-precision output target before any rounding, and let $w$ denote the current working weights (starting at $w = w^{(0)}$):

    • Output residual $r = Aw - y \in \mathbb{R}^n$: how far the current output $Aw$ has drifted from the original target $y$ across all $n$ calibration tokens.
    • Reconstruction cost $C(w) = \lVert Aw - y\rVert_2^2 = r^{\mathsf T}r$: the current sum of squared output errors.

    Let $F \subseteq \{1, \dots, d\}$ be the index set of unfixed (free) input columns that have not been quantized yet. At a single step, we write the updated weights as $w + \delta$ rather than the final quantized vector $\widehat w$: one step only snaps a single coordinate $q \in F$ onto the grid while nudging the other free weights in $F$ to continuous floating-point values, so $w + \delta$ is an intermediate working vector ($w \leftarrow w + \delta$) with $w$ fixed and $\delta$ as the step variable. Because already-quantized coordinates outside $F$ are frozen ($\delta_i = 0$ for $i \notin F$), the output change $A\delta$ depends only on the $|F|$ free columns:

    $$A\delta = A_F \delta_F, \qquad A_F \in \mathbb{R}^{n \times |F|}, \quad \delta_F \in \mathbb{R}^{|F|},$$

    where $A_F$ slices the free columns of $A$ and $\delta_F$ stacks the changes to those free weights. Substituting $A(w+\delta) - y = (Aw - y) + A\delta = r + A_F\delta_F$ into $C(w+\delta)$ and expanding the square step by step gives:

    $$\begin{aligned} C(w+\delta) &= \lVert r + A_F\delta_F \rVert_2^2 = (r + A_F\delta_F)^{\mathsf T}(r + A_F\delta_F) \\ &= \underbrace{r^{\mathsf T}r}_{C(w)} + r^{\mathsf T}A_F\delta_F + \delta_F^{\mathsf T}A_F^{\mathsf T}r + \delta_F^{\mathsf T}(A_F^{\mathsf T}A_F)\delta_F \\ &= C(w) + 2r^{\mathsf T}A_F\delta_F + \delta_F^{\mathsf T}(A_F^{\mathsf T}A_F)\delta_F, \end{aligned}$$

    since the scalar $r^{\mathsf T}A_F\delta_F$ equals its own transpose $\delta_F^{\mathsf T}A_F^{\mathsf T}r$. Defining the restricted $|F|\times|F|$ Hessian on the free coordinates as $H_F = 2A_F^{\mathsf T}A_F$ (so $A_F^{\mathsf T}A_F = \tfrac12 H_F$) and subtracting $C(w)$ yields:

    $$C(w+\delta)-C(w) =2r^{\mathsf T}A_F\delta_F+\frac12\delta_F^{\mathsf T}H_F\delta_F, \qquad H_F=2A_F^{\mathsf T}A_F \in \mathbb{R}^{|F|\times|F|}.$$

    No higher-order Taylor remainder was dropped: because $Aw$ is linear in $w$, the squared error $C(w+\delta)$ is an exact quadratic in $\delta_F$.

    Furthermore, the linear term $2r^{\mathsf T}A_F\delta_F$ vanishes at the start of every step:

    • On Step 1 ($w = w^{(0)}$): no weight has been rounded yet, so $r = Aw^{(0)} - y = 0$ and $2r^{\mathsf T}A_F\delta_F = 0$.
    • On every subsequent step: even though $r \ne 0$, the previous step chose the free weights $w_F$ to minimize $C(w) = \lVert Aw - y\rVert_2^2$ over all remaining free coordinates $F$. By the least-squares normal equations, the gradient with respect to the free coordinates is zero at that optimum: $$\frac{\partial C(w)}{\partial w_F} = 2A_F^{\mathsf T}r = 0 \quad \Longrightarrow \quad r^{\mathsf T}A_F = 0^{\mathsf T}.$$ Geometrically, the residual $r$ from earlier roundings is already orthogonal to the column space of the remaining free features $A_F$.

    With $2r^{\mathsf T}A_F\delta_F = 0$, the incremental squared error added by rounding the next column in $F$ and compensating the rest of $F$ simplifies to a pure quadratic form:

    $$\Delta C(\delta_F) = C(w+\delta) - C(w) = \frac12\delta_F^{\mathsf T}H_F\delta_F.$$

    Derive the quantization constraint

    Choose an unfixed column index $q\in F$. Let $t=\operatorname{quant}(w_q)$ be its target grid value and $a=w_q-t$ its current rounding discrepancy. The coordinate must change by $-a$.

    Define $e_q$ as a length-$|F|$ vector with 1 at the position of column $q$ and 0 elsewhere. It selects that coordinate. The constrained problem is:

    $$\min_{\delta_F}\frac12\delta_F^{\mathsf T}H_F\delta_F \quad\text{subject to}\quad e_q^{\mathsf T}\delta_F+a=0.$$

    The constraint says $w_q+\delta_q=t$. All other entries of $\delta_F$ can vary. For now assume $H_F$ is positive definite, so its inverse exists; Part 3 handles damping when the inputs make it poorly conditioned.

    Introduce a scalar multiplier $\mu$ to enforce this one constraint. The Lagrangian is the quadratic plus the multiplier times the constraint residual:

    $$\mathcal L(\delta_F,\mu)= \frac12\delta_F^{\mathsf T}H_F\delta_F+ \mu(e_q^{\mathsf T}\delta_F+a).$$

    Setting both derivatives to zero will give a minimum that lands exactly on the grid.

    Two derivatives determine the correction

    Because $H_F$ is symmetric, differentiating the quadratic gives $H_F\delta_F$. The factor $\tfrac12$ cancels the two equal contributions from the left and right copies of $\delta_F$. Differentiating with respect to $\mu$ gives the constraint itself:

    $$\frac{\partial\mathcal L}{\partial\delta_F} =H_F\delta_F+\mu e_q=0,\qquad \frac{\partial\mathcal L}{\partial\mu}=e_q^{\mathsf T}\delta_F+a=0.$$

    Write $G_F = H_F^{-1}$ for the inverse of the active Hessian. Left-multiplying the first equation $H_F\delta_F = -\mu e_q$ by $G_F$ isolates $\delta_F = -\mu G_F e_q$.

    To see what $G_F e_q$ means without index clutter, picture $3$ free weights and pick the middle coordinate $q=1$ (so $e_1 = [0, 1, 0]^{\mathsf T}$). Multiplying $G_F$ by $e_1$ simply picks out column $1$ of $G_F$:

    $$\underbrace{\begin{bmatrix} G_{00} & \mathbf{G_{01}} & G_{02} \\ G_{10} & \mathbf{G_{11}} & G_{12} \\ G_{20} & \mathbf{G_{21}} & G_{22} \end{bmatrix}}_{G_F} \underbrace{\begin{bmatrix} 0 \\ \mathbf{1} \\ 0 \end{bmatrix}}_{e_1} = \underbrace{\begin{bmatrix} \mathbf{G_{01}} \\ \mathbf{G_{11}} \\ \mathbf{G_{21}} \end{bmatrix}}_{\text{column } 1\text{ of } G_F = (G_F)_{:,1}}.$$

    Why is column $q$ of $G_F$ the exact direction of compensation? Because $H_F$ and $G_F$ are inverses ($H_F G_F = I$), multiplying $H_F$ by each column of $G_F$ produces the matching column of the identity matrix:

    $$H_F \begin{bmatrix} G_{00} & \mathbf{G_{01}} & G_{02} \\ G_{10} & \mathbf{G_{11}} & G_{12} \\ G_{20} & \mathbf{G_{21}} & G_{22} \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & \mathbf{0} & 0 \\ 0 & \mathbf{1} & 0 \\ 0 & \mathbf{0} & 1 \end{bmatrix} \quad\Longrightarrow\quad H_F \begin{bmatrix} \mathbf{G_{01}} \\ \mathbf{G_{11}} \\ \mathbf{G_{21}} \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} \mathbf{0} \\ \mathbf{1} \\ \mathbf{0} \end{bmatrix} = e_1.$$

    Moving the weights along column $q$ of $G_F$ keeps the error gradient $H_F\delta_F$ at $0$ on every unconstrained weight ($j \ne q$) and non-zero only on the constrained weight $q$.

    Next, substitute $\delta_F = -\mu G_F e_q$ into the second equation ($e_q^{\mathsf T}\delta_F + a = 0$) to find the scalar $\mu$. Left-multiplying column $q$ by the row vector $e_q^{\mathsf T}$ picks out its $q$-th entry—the diagonal entry $(G_F)_{qq}$:

    $$e_1^{\mathsf T}(G_F e_1) = \begin{bmatrix} 0 & \mathbf{1} & 0 \end{bmatrix} \begin{bmatrix} G_{01} \\ \mathbf{G_{11}} \\ G_{21} \end{bmatrix} = \mathbf{G_{11}}, \qquad -\mu(G_F)_{qq} + a = 0 \;\Longrightarrow\; \mu = \frac{a}{(G_F)_{qq}}.$$

    Plugging $\mu = \frac{a}{(G_F)_{qq}}$ back into $\delta_F = -\mu G_F e_q$ gives the closed-form GPTQ update:

    $$\delta_F = -\frac{a}{(G_F)_{qq}}(G_F)_{:,q} \qquad\text{(for } q=1\text{: }\; \begin{bmatrix} \delta_0 \\ \delta_1 \\ \delta_2 \end{bmatrix} = -\frac{a}{G_{11}} \begin{bmatrix} G_{01} \\ G_{11} \\ G_{21} \end{bmatrix}\text{)}.$$

    Check what happens on each coordinate of $\delta_F$:

    • On the rounded coordinate $q$: the $q$-th entry of $(G_F)_{:,q}$ is $(G_F)_{qq}$, which cancels the denominator $(G_F)_{qq}$ and gives $\delta_q = -a = t - w_q$, landing $w_q + \delta_q = t$ on the grid. (The formula already includes the snap on coordinate $q$; do not snap $w_q$ first and then add $\delta_q$ again.)
    • On every other free coordinate $j \in F \setminus \{q\}$: the weight shifts by $\delta_j = -a\frac{(G_F)_{jq}}{(G_F)_{qq}}$, compensating for the output error caused by snapping $w_q$.

    Finally, substituting $\delta_F = -\frac{a}{(G_F)_{qq}}G_F e_q$ back into the quadratic objective $\Delta C_q = \frac12\delta_F^{\mathsf T}H_F\delta_F$ (and using $G_F^{\mathsf T} = G_F$ and $G_F H_F G_F = H_F^{-1}H_F G_F = G_F$) gives the exact incremental error after compensation:

    $$\Delta C_q =\frac12\frac{a^2}{(G_F)_{qq}^2} e_q^{\mathsf T}\underbrace{G_FH_FG_F}_{G_F}e_q =\frac12\frac{a^2}{(G_F)_{qq}^2}(G_F)_{qq} =\frac12\frac{a^2}{(G_F)_{qq}}.$$

    How does the off-diagonal correlation $H_{01}$ affect the diagonal entry $(G_F)_{11}$? Picture two free features ($0$ and $1$) and invert their $2\times 2$ Hessian $H_F$, multiplying the determinant $\det(H_F) = H_{00}H_{11} - H_{01}^2$ inside the matrix:

    $$H_F = \begin{bmatrix} H_{00} & H_{01} \\ H_{01} & H_{11} \end{bmatrix} \quad\Longrightarrow\quad G_F = H_F^{-1} = \begin{bmatrix} \dfrac{H_{11}}{H_{00}H_{11} - \mathbf{H_{01}^2}} & \dfrac{-H_{01}}{H_{00}H_{11} - \mathbf{H_{01}^2}} \\[1.1em] \dfrac{-H_{01}}{H_{00}H_{11} - \mathbf{H_{01}^2}} & \dfrac{H_{00}}{H_{00}H_{11} - \mathbf{H_{01}^2}} \end{bmatrix}.$$

    In $H_F$, the diagonal $H_{11} = 2\sum_s A_{s,1}^2$ only contains feature $1$'s own variance, while the off-diagonal $H_{01} = 2\sum_s A_{s,0}A_{s,1}$ is the correlation between features $0$ and $1$. When we invert $H_F$, dividing by the determinant $H_{00}H_{11} - H_{01}^2$ puts $\mathbf{H_{01}^2}$ directly into the denominator of the diagonal entry $(G_F)_{11}$:

    $$(G_F)_{11} = \frac{H_{00}}{H_{00}H_{11} - \mathbf{H_{01}^2}} \quad\Longrightarrow\quad \Delta C_1 = \frac12\frac{a^2}{(G_F)_{11}} = \frac12 a^2\Bigl(\underbrace{H_{11}}_{\text{uncompensated}} - \underbrace{\frac{\mathbf{H_{01}^2}}{H_{00}}}_{\text{cancelled by feature }0}\Bigr).$$
    • Zero correlation ($H_{01} = 0$): the $\mathbf{H_{01}^2}$ term in the denominator disappears, so $(G_F)_{11} = \frac{H_{00}}{H_{00}H_{11}} = \frac{1}{H_{11}}$ is just the reciprocal of the single-feature variance, and the error stays at $\frac12 a^2 H_{11}$.
    • Strong correlation ($H_{01}$ is large): subtracting a large $\mathbf{H_{01}^2}$ makes the determinant denominator $H_{00}H_{11} - H_{01}^2$ small, which makes the fraction $(G_F)_{11}$ much larger than $\frac{1}{H_{11}}$ and makes the compensated error $\Delta C_1$ much smaller.

    A grid target; zero is only a special case

    Suppose our quantization grid of allowed low-bit values is $\mathcal{G} = \{-1.0, -0.5, 0, 0.5, 1.0\}$ and the current weight is $w_1 = -0.85$. Its nearest grid point is simply the closest allowed number on that list ($t = -1.0$, at distance $0.15$, rather than $t = -0.5$, at distance $0.35$).

    Since GPTQ adjusts the other weights to compensate, could picking a farther grid value (like $t = -0.5$) ever end up with less compensated error than picking the closest one ($t = -1.0$)? Write $\Delta C_q$ as a function of any candidate grid value $t \in \mathcal{G}$ (using $a = w_q - t$):

    $$\Delta C_q(t) = \frac12\frac{(w_q - t)^2}{(G_F)_{qq}} \quad\Longrightarrow\quad \operatorname*{argmin}_{t \in \mathcal{G}}\;\Delta C_q(t) = \operatorname*{argmin}_{t \in \mathcal{G}}\;(w_q - t)^2 = \operatorname{quant}(w_q).$$

    For a given column $q$, the denominator $(G_F)_{qq} > 0$ is fixed by the calibration inputs—it stays the same whether you test $t = -1.0$ or $t = -0.5$. Only the numerator $(w_q - t)^2$ changes ($0.15^2$ vs. $0.35^2$). That gives two practical rules:

    • Always round $w_q$ to its closest allowed grid value $\operatorname{quant}(w_q)$: compensation never makes a farther grid point better (and values outside the grid's range clip to the nearest endpoint, such as $1.20 \to 1.0$).
    • Round the current weight $w_q$, not the original $w_q^{(0)}$: because earlier steps already added compensation $\delta_q$ to $w_q$, $\operatorname{quant}(w_q)$ rounds the updated working weight, which can land on a different grid point than the initial unquantized weight $w_q^{(0)}$ would have.
    The optional pruning connection: Optimal Brain Surgeon ($t = 0$)

    GPTQ's update formula originally came from network pruning—specifically Optimal Brain Surgeon (OBS), which deletes a weight by forcing it to zero instead of rounding it to a grid point:

    • Quantization (OBQ / GPTQ): snaps $w_q$ to the nearest grid point $t = \operatorname{quant}(w_q)$, so the discrepancy to remove is $a = w_q - t$ (and $\delta_q = -a = t - w_q$).
    • Pruning (OBS): forces $w_q$ all the way to $t = 0$ (so $w_q + \delta_q = 0$, meaning $\delta_q = -w_q$). Substituting $t = 0$ into $a = w_q - t$ gives $a = w_q - 0 = w_q$.

    Comparing the four quantization equations ($a = w_q - t$) directly against the OBS pruning equations ($t = 0 \implies a = w_q$) shows the exact substitution:

    $$\begin{aligned} \text{\textbf{Quantization ($a = w_q - t$):}}\quad & e_q^{\mathsf T}\delta_F + a = 0,\qquad \mu = \frac{a}{(G_F)_{qq}},\qquad \delta_F = -\frac{a}{(G_F)_{qq}}(G_F)_{:,q},\qquad \Delta C_q = \frac12\frac{a^2}{(G_F)_{qq}}, \\[0.6em] \text{\textbf{OBS Pruning ($t = 0, a = w_q$):}}\quad & e_q^{\mathsf T}\delta_F + w_q = 0,\qquad \mu = \frac{w_q}{(G_F)_{qq}},\qquad \delta_F = -\frac{w_q}{(G_F)_{qq}}(G_F)_{:,q},\qquad \Delta C_q = \frac12\frac{w_q^2}{(G_F)_{qq}}. \end{aligned}$$

    While the original 1993 OBS paper derived this on the full network training loss using a second-order Taylor approximation near a local minimum, our layer-output reconstruction cost $C(w) = \lVert Aw - y\rVert_2^2$ is already an exact quadratic—so quantization simply generalizes OBS from $t = 0$ to any grid target $t = \operatorname{quant}(w_q)$.

    Greedy OBQ chooses a column; GPTQ fixes the order

    The formulas above tell us how to compensate the remaining weights once a column $q \in F$ is chosen. But which free column $q \in F$ should we quantize next? That choice is the exact dividing line between OBQ and GPTQ:

    • Greedy OBQ (picks the cheapest column per row): within a single output row $w$, OBQ evaluates the compensated error $\Delta C_q = \frac12\frac{(w_q - \operatorname{quant}(w_q))^2}{(G_F)_{qq}}$ for every remaining column $q \in F$ (dropping the common factor $\tfrac12$ to define the score $S_q = 2\Delta C_q$) and greedily rounds whichever column causes the smallest error:
      $$S_q=\frac{(w_q-\operatorname{quant}(w_q))^2}{(G_F)_{qq}}, \qquad q_{\mathrm{OBQ}}=\operatorname*{argmin}_{q\in F}\;S_q.$$
      Why OBQ is slow on a full layer $W \in \mathbb{R}^{m \times d}$: the numerator $(w_q - \operatorname{quant}(w_q))^2$ depends on the weights of that specific row. Row $0$ might pick $q = 1$ first (leaving free set $F = \{0, 2\}$), while Row $1$ picks $q = 2$ first (leaving $F = \{0, 1\}$). Because every output row eliminates columns in a different order, every row ends up with a different free set $F$ and must maintain its own separate inverse matrix $G_F = H_F^{-1}$ ($O(md^3)$ total work).
    • GPTQ (uses the same fixed column order for every row): GPTQ skips the greedy search and rounds columns in a single predetermined order (for example, $q = 0, 1, \dots, d-1$) across all $m$ output rows at the same time. Look at what changes across rows versus what stays shared in the update formula for row $i$:
      $$\delta_F^{(\text{row } i)} = -\underbrace{\bigl(w_{i,q} - \operatorname{quant}(w_{i,q})\bigr)}_{\text{row } i\text{'s own scalar error } a_i} \;\cdot\; \underbrace{\frac{(G_F)_{:,q}}{(G_F)_{qq}}}_{\text{shared direction across all } m \text{ rows}}.$$
      Because all $m$ rows quantize column $q$ at the same step, every row has the same remaining free set $F$ and shares the same active inverse $G_F = H_F^{-1}$ ($O(d^3)$ matrix work once per layer instead of $m$ times).

    Convention note (sum vs. mean): under $H = 2A^{\mathsf T}A$, the score $S_q = 2\Delta C_q$ is twice the extra sum of squared output errors across the $n$ tokens (so the extra mean squared error is $\Delta C_q / n = S_q / (2n)$). Dividing $H$ by $n$ multiplies $G_F = H_F^{-1}$ by $n$, which cancels out in the ratio $\frac{(G_F)_{:,q}}{(G_F)_{qq}}$ and leaves the weight update $\delta_F$ unchanged.

    Remove a fixed coordinate without inverting again

    Once column $q$ has been snapped to the grid and compensated, its weight is frozen for the rest of the algorithm ($\delta_q = 0$ on all later steps). Let $R = F \setminus \{q\}$ be the remaining $|R| = |F| - 1$ free columns. On the next step, the active Hessian is the submatrix $H_{R,R}$ (formed by deleting row $q$ and column $q$ from $H_F$), so we need its inverse:

    $$G_R = (H_{R,R})^{-1} \in \mathbb{R}^{|R| \times |R|}.$$

    Why can't we just delete row $q$ and column $q$ from $G_F = H_F^{-1}$? Because the submatrix of an inverse is not the inverse of the submatrix ($G_{R,R} \ne (H_{R,R})^{-1}$). In our $2\times 2$ example above, deleting row $1$ and column $1$ from $H_F$ leaves $H_{00}$, whose inverse is $\frac{1}{H_{00}}$—whereas the top-left entry of $G_F$ is $G_{00} = \frac{H_{11}}{H_{00}H_{11}-H_{01}^2} \ne \frac{1}{H_{00}}$, because $G_{00}$ still includes the effect of allowing coordinate $1$ to move.

    To derive $(H_{R,R})^{-1}$ directly from the entries of $G_F$, partition $H_F$ and $G_F = H_F^{-1}$ into the remaining indices $R$ and the newly fixed index $q$:

    $$H_F = \begin{bmatrix} H_{R,R} & H_{R,q} \\[0.3em] H_{q,R} & H_{qq} \end{bmatrix}, \qquad G_F = H_F^{-1} = \begin{bmatrix} G_{R,R} & G_{R,q} \\[0.3em] G_{q,R} & G_{qq} \end{bmatrix},$$

    where $G_{R,R} \in \mathbb{R}^{|R|\times|R|}$ keeps the rows and columns in $R$, $G_{R,q} \in \mathbb{R}^{|R|\times 1}$ is column $q$ on rows $R$, $G_{q,R} \in \mathbb{R}^{1\times |R|}$ is row $q$ on columns $R$, and $G_{qq}$ is the scalar $(q,q)$ pivot. (For $3$ weights when fixing the middle coordinate $q=1$, $R=\{0,2\}$ consists of the four corner entries $G_{R,R}$, the column $G_{R,1} = [G_{01}, G_{21}]^{\mathsf T}$, the row $G_{1,R} = [G_{10}, G_{12}]$, and the center pivot $G_{11}$.)

    Since $G_F H_F = I_{|F|}$, multiply $G_F$ by the first block-column of $H_F$:

    $$\begin{bmatrix} G_{R,R} & G_{R,q} \\[0.3em] G_{q,R} & G_{qq} \end{bmatrix} \begin{bmatrix} H_{R,R} \\[0.3em] H_{q,R} \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} I_{|R|} \\[0.3em] 0^{\mathsf T} \end{bmatrix} \quad\Longrightarrow\quad \left\{ \begin{aligned} \text{(1)}\quad & G_{R,R} H_{R,R} + G_{R,q} H_{q,R} = I_{|R|}, \\[0.3em] \text{(2)}\quad & G_{q,R} H_{R,R} + G_{qq} H_{q,R} = 0^{\mathsf T}. \end{aligned} \right.$$

    Solve equation (2) for $H_{q,R}$ by dividing by the scalar pivot $G_{qq}$:

    $$H_{q,R} = -\frac{1}{G_{qq}} G_{q,R} H_{R,R}.$$

    Substitute that expression for $H_{q,R}$ into equation (1) and factor $H_{R,R}$ out on the right:

    $$G_{R,R} H_{R,R} - \frac{G_{R,q} G_{q,R}}{G_{qq}} H_{R,R} = I_{|R|} \quad\Longrightarrow\quad \Bigl(G_{R,R} - \frac{G_{R,q} G_{q,R}}{G_{qq}}\Bigr) H_{R,R} = I_{|R|}.$$

    Because multiplying $H_{R,R}$ by the bracketed matrix gives the identity $I_{|R|}$, that bracketed matrix is the exact reduced inverse $G_R = (H_{R,R})^{-1}$:

    $$G_{\mathrm{next}} = (H_{R,R})^{-1} = G_{R,R} - \frac{G_{R,q} G_{q,R}}{G_{qq}}.$$

    Check the shapes in this rank-one downdate (for $3$ weights after fixing $q=1$, so $R=\{0,2\}$ has size $|R|=2$):

    $$\underbrace{\begin{bmatrix} G_{00} & G_{02} \\ G_{20} & G_{22} \end{bmatrix}}_{G_{R,R}\;(2\times 2)} \;-\; \frac{1}{\underbrace{G_{11}}_{\text{scalar}}} \underbrace{\begin{bmatrix} G_{01} \\ G_{21} \end{bmatrix}}_{G_{R,1}\;(2\times 1)} \underbrace{\begin{bmatrix} G_{10} & G_{12} \end{bmatrix}}_{G_{1,R}\;(1\times 2)} \;=\; \underbrace{(H_{R,R})^{-1}}_{2\times 2}.$$

    Subtracting the outer product $\frac{G_{R,q}G_{q,R}}{G_{qq}}$ (one step of Gaussian elimination on the inverse) strips out coordinate $q$'s indirect pathway in $O(|F|^2)$ operations instead of a fresh $O(|F|^3)$ matrix inversion. Repeating this $O(|F|^2)$ downdate across all $d$ steps costs $O(d^3)$ total—which Part 3 shows how to precompute once for the entire layer via Cholesky factorization.
