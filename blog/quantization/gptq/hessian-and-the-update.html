    GPTQ: derive and check the Hessian update

    October 2026 · 8 min read

    The question is precise: if one weight must land on a grid point, how should the remaining weights move to keep the outputs as close as possible? We will solve that constrained least-squares problem, check it with numbers, then prepare the next step.

    One layer, two ways to arrange the samples

    Use $A$ for a calibration table with $n$ samples as rows and $d$ input features as columns. The weight matrix $W$ has $m$ output rows and $d$ input columns. $Y$ is the output table, with one row per sample. These are the shapes used by PyTorch nn.Linear:

    $$A\in\mathbb R^{n\times d},\qquad W\in\mathbb R^{m\times d},\qquad Y=AW^{\mathsf T}\in\mathbb R^{n\times m}.$$

    Each output row of $W$ defines a separate reconstruction problem. Write that row as a length-$d$ column vector $w$; its original outputs are $y=Aw$. We can solve for each output channel separately because the layer's total squared error is the sum of their errors.

    The paper instead puts samples in columns. Set $X=A^{\mathsf T}$, with shape $d\times n$. Its left-multiplied output is the transpose of our output table. The Hessian $H$ is the $d\times d$ second-derivative matrix of one output channel's squared reconstruction error:

    $$WX=WA^{\mathsf T}=Y^{\mathsf T},\qquad H=2A^{\mathsf T}A=2XX^{\mathsf T}\in\mathbb R^{d\times d}.$$

    Both products for $H$ describe input-feature correlations. Computing $2AA^{\mathsf T}$ instead would give an $n\times n$ sample-correlation matrix, not the weight-coordinate Hessian.

    The authors' add_batch transposes linear inputs before accumulating inp @ inp.T. It also normalizes its accumulation by the number of calibration examples seen. Below we use the unnormalized sum $H=2A^{\mathsf T}A$ so the numerical costs are easy to check.

    The quadratic is exact here

    Let $v$ be the current length-$d$ weight vector and keep $y=Aw$ as the original target. The output residual is $r=Av-y$, a length-$n$ vector. The reconstruction cost is $C(v)=r^{\mathsf T}r$, the sum of squared residuals.

    Let $F$ be the set of unfixed input columns. $A_F$ contains just those columns, and $\delta_F$ is a length-$|F|$ vector of changes to their weights. $H_F$ is the Hessian restricted to these free coordinates. Expanding the square gives:

    $$C(v+\delta)-C(v) =2r^{\mathsf T}A_F\delta_F+\frac12\delta_F^{\mathsf T}H_F\delta_F, \qquad H_F=2A_F^{\mathsf T}A_F.$$

    Here $\delta$ is zero outside $F$. The active Hessian $H_F$ has shape $|F|\times|F|$, not always $d\times d$. No Taylor remainder is being dropped: a linear layer with fixed inputs and squared error is exactly quadratic in its weights.

    Initially $v=w$, so $r=0$. After earlier weights are fixed, optimal compensation makes the remaining coordinates a least-squares optimum. That gives $A_F^{\mathsf T}r=0$, even though $r$ itself need not be zero.

    At such a point the linear term vanishes. The extra cost of the next constraint is therefore $\tfrac12\delta_F^{\mathsf T}H_F\delta_F$. It is an increment on top of error from earlier steps, not the layer's total error. This statement assumes the remaining coordinates have been optimized; it is not valid for an arbitrary partially rounded vector.

    Derive the quantization constraint

    Choose an unfixed column index $q\in F$. Let $t=\operatorname{quant}(v_q)$ be its target grid value and $a=v_q-t$ its current rounding discrepancy. The coordinate must change by $-a$.

    Define $e_q$ as a length-$|F|$ vector with 1 at the position of column $q$ and 0 elsewhere. It selects that coordinate. The constrained problem is:

    $$\min_{\delta_F}\frac12\delta_F^{\mathsf T}H_F\delta_F \quad\text{subject to}\quad e_q^{\mathsf T}\delta_F+a=0.$$

    The constraint says $v_q+\delta_q=t$. All other entries of $\delta_F$ can vary. For now assume $H_F$ is positive definite, so its inverse exists; Part 3 handles damping when the inputs make it poorly conditioned.

    Introduce a scalar multiplier $\mu$ to enforce this one constraint. The Lagrangian is the quadratic plus the multiplier times the constraint residual:

    $$\mathcal L(\delta_F,\mu)= \frac12\delta_F^{\mathsf T}H_F\delta_F+ \mu(e_q^{\mathsf T}\delta_F+a).$$

    Setting both derivatives to zero will give a minimum that lands exactly on the grid.

    Two derivatives determine the correction

    Because $H_F$ is symmetric, differentiating the quadratic gives $H_F\delta_F$. The factor $\tfrac12$ cancels the two equal contributions from the left and right copies of $\delta_F$. Differentiating with respect to $\mu$ gives the constraint itself:

    $$\frac{\partial\mathcal L}{\partial\delta_F} =H_F\delta_F+\mu e_q=0,\qquad \frac{\partial\mathcal L}{\partial\mu}=e_q^{\mathsf T}\delta_F+a=0.$$

    Write $G_F=H_F^{-1}$, also $|F|\times|F|$. Multiplying the first equation by $G_F$ gives $\delta_F=-\mu G_Fe_q$. The product $G_Fe_q$ is column $q$ of the inverse: this is the direction of the compensation.

    Substitute that direction into the second equation. Since $e_q^{\mathsf T}G_Fe_q$ selects the diagonal entry $(G_F)_{qq}$, the scale is:

    $$-\mu(G_F)_{qq}+a=0 \quad\Rightarrow\quad \mu=\frac{a}{(G_F)_{qq}}, \qquad \delta_F=-\frac{a}{(G_F)_{qq}}(G_F)_{:,q}.$$

    On coordinate $q$, the diagonal cancels, giving $\delta_q=-a=t-v_q$. Off-diagonal entries move the other weights. The formula includes the snap itself; do not snap coordinate $q$ and then add this same change to it again.

    To compute the extra squared output error, put the correction back into the quadratic. The identity $G_FH_FG_F=G_F$ reduces the matrix product to one diagonal entry:

    $$\Delta C_q =\frac12\frac{a^2}{(G_F)_{qq}^2} e_q^{\mathsf T}G_FH_FG_Fe_q =\frac12\frac{a^2}{(G_F)_{qq}}.$$

    For the same rounding discrepancy, a larger inverse diagonal means a cheaper compensated constraint. The inverse captures how the other input features can help; the diagonal of $H_F$ alone does not provide that correction.

    A grid target; zero is only a special case

    For a fixed column and fixed grid, the denominator does not depend on the target $t$. Minimizing the cost therefore chooses the nearest grid point to the current weight $v_q$, including clipping at the grid's limits. Earlier compensation may have changed that value.

    The optional pruning connection

    Optimal Brain Surgeon (OBS) sets a weight to zero. Setting $t=0$ in the derivation gives $a=v_q$, so the constraint, multiplier, update, and cost are:

    $$e_q^{\mathsf T}\delta_F+v_q=0,\qquad \mu=\frac{v_q}{(G_F)_{qq}},\qquad \delta_F=-\frac{v_q}{(G_F)_{qq}}(G_F)_{:,q},\qquad \Delta C_q=\frac12\frac{v_q^2}{(G_F)_{qq}}.$$

    The original OBS training-loss argument uses a second-order approximation near a stationary point. Our linear reconstruction derivation needs neither that training assumption nor an approximation. Quantization simply replaces the zero target with a legal grid value.

    For the example below, setting column 1 to zero gives weights approximately $(0.601437,0,0.497439)$ and squared output error $0.099238$. The existing check script verifies this special case too.

    Greedy OBQ chooses a column; GPTQ fixes the order

    OBQ compares the extra compensated costs for the remaining weights within one output row. It can omit the common factor $\tfrac12$ when ranking candidates. With $G_F=H_F^{-1}$, define the score and selected column as:

    $$S_q=\frac{(v_q-\operatorname{quant}(v_q))^2}{(G_F)_{qq}}, \qquad q_{\mathrm{OBQ}}=\operatorname*{argmin}_{q\in F}S_q.$$

    The score is twice the extra sum of squared output errors under our $H=2A^{\mathsf T}A$ convention. For one output channel, the extra mean squared error is $S_q/(2n)$. Scaling the Hessian for a mean changes the reported score, but not the compensation vector when the scaling is consistent.

    GPTQ does not make this greedy choice. It processes columns in a chosen fixed order, shared by every output row. Each row has its own current rounding discrepancy, but all rows use the same inverse-derived correction direction for that column.

    Remove a fixed coordinate without inverting again

    After fixing column $q$, let $R=F\setminus\{q\}$ be the remaining free set. The next update needs $(H_{RR})^{-1}$. Simply deleting row and column $q$ of $G_F$ is wrong: that submatrix of the inverse still includes the effect of allowing coordinate $q$ to move.

    A rank-one downdate removes that effect. Here write $G=G_F$ for the current active inverse. $G_{R,q}$ is a length-$|R|$ column, $G_{q,R}$ is its transpose, and $G_{qq}$ is a scalar pivot. Their outer product has the required $|R|\times|R|$ shape:

    $$G_{\mathrm{next}} =G_{R,R}-\frac{G_{R,q}G_{q,R}}{G_{qq}} =(H_{R,R})^{-1}.$$

    This is the same subtraction as one Gaussian-elimination step on the inverse, followed by dropping the fixed coordinate. It uses the current active inverse, not always the original full inverse.

    To see why it works, reorder the active Hessian so $q$ is last. Call the remaining Hessian $B$, its coupling column $b$, and the pivot $c$. Set $s=c-b^{\mathsf T}B^{-1}b$ and $u=B^{-1}b$:

    $$H_F=\begin{bmatrix}B&b\\b^{\mathsf T}&c\end{bmatrix}, \qquad G_F=\begin{bmatrix}B^{-1}+uu^{\mathsf T}/s&-u/s\\-u^{\mathsf T}/s&1/s\end{bmatrix}.$$

    The inverse's top-left block contains an extra $uu^{\mathsf T}/s$. The downdate subtracts exactly that term, since $(-u/s)(-u^{\mathsf T}/s)/(1/s)=uu^{\mathsf T}/s$. What remains is $B^{-1}$.

    In our example $q=1$ and $R=\{0,2\}$. The reduced Hessian and its inverse are:

    $$H_{R,R}= \begin{bmatrix}9.24&1.28\\1.28&2.28\end{bmatrix}, \qquad G_{\mathrm{next}}\approx \begin{bmatrix}0.11735&-0.06588\\-0.06588&0.47558\end{bmatrix}.$$

    The check script verifies that the downdate produces this same inverse. The outer product costs $O(|F|^2)$ rather than a fresh cubic inversion. Repeating it over all coordinates is still cubic per independent row; sharing a fixed order is GPTQ's next improvement.
