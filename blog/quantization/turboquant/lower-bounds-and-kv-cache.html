    3. TurboQuant: Shannon lower bounds, KV-cache outlier splitting, and vector search

    October 2026 · 7 min read

    Part 1 and Part 2 established upper bounds for online vector quantization: $\mathrm{TurboQuant}_{\mathrm{mse}}$ achieves Euclidean distortion at most $\frac{\sqrt{3}\pi}{2}\cdot 4^{-b}$, and two-stage $\mathrm{TurboQuant}_{\mathrm{prod}}$ achieves unbiased inner-product distortion at most $\frac{\sqrt{3}\pi^2}{d}\cdot 4^{-b}$.

    A natural question is whether a smarter joint vector quantizer — one that quantizes all $d$ coordinates simultaneously using a high-dimensional lattice or trained $k$-means codebook rather than $d$ scalar lookups — could decay much faster with $b$. Theorem 3 of TurboQuant proves that no randomized quantizer, even an intractable offline one, can beat $4^{-b}$ for Euclidean MSE or $\frac{1}{d \cdot 4^b}$ for inner-product MSE. This closing post walks through the Shannon lower bound and Yao minimax proof, then shows how TurboQuant is engineered in practice for $2.5$-bit and $3.5$-bit LLM KV caches and zero-indexing-time nearest-neighbor search.

    The Shannon lower bound on the unit sphere

    Consider a continuous random source $X \sim p_X$ supported on the unit hypersphere $\mathbb{S}^{d-1}$ with differential entropy $h(X) = -\int_{\mathbb{S}^{d-1}} p_X(x) \log_2 p_X(x)\,dx$. When $X$ is compressed to a total budget of $B = b d$ bits, rate-distortion theory defines $D(p_X, B)$ as the minimum achievable expected squared error $\mathbb{E}[\lVert X - \widehat{X} \rVert_2^2]$ over all encoders and decoders with mutual information $I(X; \widehat{X}) \le B$.

    By the classical Shannon Lower Bound (SLB) (Shannon, 1959), any source with differential entropy $h(X)$ satisfies:

    $$D(p_X, B) \ge \frac{d}{2\pi e}\,2^{\frac{2}{d}\bigl(h(X) - B\bigr)}.$$

    To make this lower bound as tight as possible on $\mathbb{S}^{d-1}$, choose $p_X = \mathrm{Unif}(\mathbb{S}^{d-1})$, which maximizes differential entropy over the sphere at $h(X) = \log_2 A_d$, where $A_d = \frac{2\pi^{d/2}}{\Gamma(d/2)}$ is the surface area of $\mathbb{S}^{d-1}$. Substituting $h(X) = \log_2 A_d$ and $B = b d$ gives:

    $$D\bigl(\mathrm{Unif}(\mathbb{S}^{d-1}),\, b d\bigr) \ge \frac{d}{2\pi e}\,A_d^{2/d}\,2^{-2b} = \frac{d}{2\pi e}\!\left(\frac{2\pi^{d/2}}{\Gamma(d/2)}\right)^{\!2/d}\frac{1}{4^b}.$$

    Applying Stirling's approximation $\Gamma(d/2) \sim \sqrt{2\pi}\bigl(\frac{d}{2e}\bigr)^{d/2}\sqrt{\frac{2}{d}}$ shows that $\bigl(\Gamma(d/2)\bigr)^{2/d} \sim \frac{d}{2e}$, so $A_d^{2/d} \sim \frac{2\pi e}{d}$. The geometric prefactor $\frac{d}{2\pi e}\,A_d^{2/d}$ converges to $1$ in high dimensions, yielding Lemma 3 of TurboQuant:

    $$D\bigl(\mathrm{Unif}(\mathbb{S}^{d-1}),\, b d\bigr) \ge \frac{1}{4^b}.$$

    From average-case SLB to worst-case randomized quantizers via Yao's principle

    An online quantizer $Q$ may use internal randomness (such as the random rotation $\Pi$ and random Gaussian projection $S$). Any randomized quantizer $Q$ is a probability distribution $\mathcal{D}_Q$ over deterministic quantizers $Q_{\mathrm{det}} : \mathbb{S}^{d-1} \to \{0,1\}^{bd}$. By Yao's minimax principle, the worst-case distortion of $Q$ over all unit inputs $x \in \mathbb{S}^{d-1}$ is at least the average-case distortion of the best deterministic quantizer under $X \sim \mathrm{Unif}(\mathbb{S}^{d-1})$:

    $$D_{\mathrm{mse}}(Q) = \sup_{\lVert x \rVert_2 = 1} \mathbb{E}_{Q \sim \mathcal{D}_Q}\bigl[\lVert x - Q^{-1}(Q(x)) \rVert_2^2\bigr] \ge \inf_{Q_{\mathrm{det}}} \mathbb{E}_{X \sim \mathrm{Unif}(\mathbb{S}^{d-1})}\bigl[\lVert X - Q_{\mathrm{det}}^{-1}(Q_{\mathrm{det}}(X)) \rVert_2^2\bigr] \ge \frac{1}{4^b}.$$

    To lower-bound the inner-product distortion $D_{\mathrm{prod}}(Q)$, evaluate the query along the $d$ scaled standard basis vectors $y_j = \lVert y \rVert_2\,e_j$ for $j = 1, \dots, d$. Summing the inner-product squared errors across all $d$ basis directions reconstructs the Euclidean squared error:

    $$\sum_{j=1}^d \mathbb{E}\bigl[\lvert \langle \lVert y \rVert_2 e_j,\, x - Q^{-1}(Q(x)) \rangle \rvert^2\bigr] = \lVert y \rVert_2^2\,\mathbb{E}\bigl[\lVert x - Q^{-1}(Q(x)) \rVert_2^2\bigr] \ge \frac{\lVert y \rVert_2^2}{4^b}.$$

    By the pigeonhole principle, since the sum of $d$ non-negative numbers is at least $\frac{\lVert y \rVert_2^2}{4^b}$, at least one basis direction $y_j$ must have expected inner-product distortion at least the average $\frac{\lVert y \rVert_2^2}{d \cdot 4^b}$. This completes Theorem 3 of TurboQuant:

    $$D_{\mathrm{mse}}(Q) \ge \frac{1}{4^b}, \qquad D_{\mathrm{prod}}(Q) \ge \frac{\lVert y \rVert_2^2}{d}\cdot\frac{1}{4^b}.$$

    Comparing Theorem 1 ($\le \frac{\sqrt{3}\pi}{2} \cdot 4^{-b} \approx 2.72 \cdot 4^{-b}$) against Theorem 3 ($\ge 4^{-b}$) explains why TurboQuant does not need high-dimensional vector codebooks: after a random rotation onto $\mathbb{S}^{d-1}$, independent 1D Lloyd-Max scalar quantization is already within $1.45\times$ (at $b=1$) to $2.43\times$ (at $b=4$) of the information-theoretic limit of any $d$-dimensional quantizer.

    Practical KV-cache compression: outlier channel splitting

    In transformer attention heads (typically $d = 128$), key tensors exhibit severe channel-wise outliers: a handful of fixed channels carry magnitudes $10\times$ larger than the rest across every token. While a single $128$-dimensional random rotation spreads those spikes across all $128$ rotated coordinates, doing so raises the overall norm $\lVert x \rVert_2$ that scales the quantization noise of the quiet channels.

    TurboQuant's KV-cache implementation combines random rotation with a lightweight outlier channel split that also enables fractional average bit-widths ($2.5$ or $3.5$ bits per channel):

    1. Identify the $d_{\mathrm{out}} = 32$ channels with the largest magnitudes and the remaining $d_{\mathrm{reg}} = 96$ regular channels inside each $d = 128$ head (or across grouped query heads).
    2. Quantize the $32$ outlier channels with a $(b+1)$-bit $\mathrm{TurboQuant}_{\mathrm{prod}}$ instance and the $96$ regular channels with a $b$-bit $\mathrm{TurboQuant}_{\mathrm{prod}}$ instance.

    The resulting payload bit-widths (verified in tools/check_turboquant.py) are:

    $$b_{\mathrm{index}}(2\text{--}3\text{ split}) = \frac{32 \times 3 + 96 \times 2}{128} = 2.25\text{ bits/coord}, \qquad b_{\mathrm{index}}(3\text{--}4\text{ split}) = \frac{32 \times 4 + 96 \times 3}{128} = 3.25\text{ bits/coord}.$$

    Adding two FP16 norms ($\gamma_0 = \lVert x \rVert_2$ and $\gamma = \lVert r \rVert_2$) per sub-vector adds roughly $0.25$ bits per coordinate, bringing the total effective footprint to $2.5$ bits and $3.5$ bits per channel.

    Zero-indexing-time vector search vs. Product Quantization and RabitQ

    High-dimensional approximate nearest-neighbor (ANN) search engines traditionally rely on Product Quantization (PQ), which slices $d$-dimensional embeddings into $m$ subspaces and runs Lloyd's $k$-means algorithm on a training set of database vectors to learn $m$ codebooks, or on RabitQ, which centers vectors around learned $k$-means centroids before randomized lattice quantization.

    Because $\mathrm{TurboQuant}_{\mathrm{prod}}$ uses a universal Beta/Gaussian codebook after a random rotation, its codebook is data-oblivious:

    • Near-zero indexing overhead: on $100{,}000$ OpenAI-3 embeddings ($d = 1536$ and $d = 3072$), PQ requires $239\text{--}494$ seconds and RabitQ requires $2{,}267\text{--}3{,}957$ seconds to build their quantization index on CPU, whereas TurboQuant finishes in $0.0007\text{--}0.0021$ seconds (only generating the shared rotation seed).
    • Higher top-$k$ recall at $1\text{--}4$ bits: because PQ struggles when subspace dimension $d/m$ grows and RabitQ's 1-bit unbiased estimator is outperformed by TurboQuant's $(b-1)$-bit MSE + $1$-bit QJL split, $\mathrm{TurboQuant}_{\mathrm{prod}}$ consistently achieves higher $1@k$ recall across GloVe ($d = 200$) and DBpedia OpenAI-3 ($d = 1536, 3072$) benchmarks.
    How do PolarQuant, QJL, and TurboQuant fit together as a family?

    All three replace data-dependent per-block scales with random geometry: PolarQuant removes scales by recursively converting rotated coordinates into independent polar angles that concentrate around $\pi/4$; QJL provides a zero-overhead 1-bit sign sketch whose asymmetric reconstruction is strictly unbiased for inner products; and TurboQuant unifies the two stages by applying 1D Beta/Gaussian Lloyd-Max quantization to the rotated coordinates first and running 1-bit QJL on the residual to reach within a constant factor of the Shannon lower bound.
