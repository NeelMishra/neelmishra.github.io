    2. TurboQuant: why rounding shrinks dot products, and how 1-bit QJL fixes it

    October 2026 · 6 min read

    In Part 1, $\mathrm{TurboQuant}_{\mathrm{mse}}$ rotated a unit vector $x$ and rounded each coordinate to the nearest value in a fixed lookup table. That works well when our only goal is to reconstruct $x$ with low squared error.

    However, attention layers and vector search engines do not stop at reconstructing $x$. They multiply the quantized key vector $\tilde{x}$ by a query vector $y$ to compute a dot product $\langle y, \tilde{x} \rangle = \sum_{j=1}^{d} y_j \tilde{x}_j$. At $1$ or $2$ bits per coordinate, standard rounding systematically makes every dot product too small.

    Why rounding to bucket averages shrinks dot products

    Suppose we round a number $U$ to the average value $\tilde{U}$ of its bucket. Because $\tilde{U}$ is the average of all $U$ values that land in that bucket, the leftover error $(U - \tilde{U})$ has average $0$ inside every bucket. Multiplying by $\tilde{U}$ shows that $\mathbb{E}[\tilde{U}(U - \tilde{U})] = 0$, or equivalently $\mathbb{E}[U\tilde{U}] = \mathbb{E}[\tilde{U}^2]$.

    Now expand the average squared rounding error $D_{\mathrm{mse}} = \mathbb{E}[\lVert x - \tilde{x}_{\mathrm{mse}} \rVert_2^2]$ for a unit vector ($\lVert x \rVert_2^2 = 1$). Because $\mathbb{E}[\langle x, \tilde{x}_{\mathrm{mse}} \rangle] = \mathbb{E}[\lVert \tilde{x}_{\mathrm{mse}} \rVert_2^2]$, the squared length of the rounded vector is smaller than $1$ by exactly $D_{\mathrm{mse}}$:

    $$D_{\mathrm{mse}} = \mathbb{E}\bigl[\lVert x \rVert_2^2 - 2\langle x, \tilde{x}_{\mathrm{mse}} \rangle + \lVert \tilde{x}_{\mathrm{mse}} \rVert_2^2\bigr] = 1 - \mathbb{E}\bigl[\lVert \tilde{x}_{\mathrm{mse}} \rVert_2^2\bigr] \quad\Longrightarrow\quad \mathbb{E}\bigl[\lVert \tilde{x}_{\mathrm{mse}} \rVert_2^2\bigr] = 1 - D_{\mathrm{mse}}.$$

    Since the random rotation treats every direction equally, the rounded vector $\tilde{x}_{\mathrm{mse}}$ points in the right direction on average, but its length is scaled down by $(1 - D_{\mathrm{mse}})$. As a result, its dot product with any query vector $y$ is also shrunk by $(1 - D_{\mathrm{mse}})$:

    $$\mathbb{E}_{\Pi}\bigl[\langle y, \tilde{x}_{\mathrm{mse}} \rangle\bigr] = (1 - D_{\mathrm{mse}})\,\langle y, x \rangle.$$

    At $b = 1$ bit ($D_{\mathrm{mse}} \approx 0.3634$), every dot product is multiplied by $1 - 0.3634 = 0.6366$ ($2/\pi$). In a Transformer attention layer, shrinking the logits before softmax makes the attention weights too flat and blurs the model's focus.

    How 1-bit QJL estimates dot products without bias

    Why not just divide $\tilde{x}_{\mathrm{mse}}$ by $(1 - D_{\mathrm{mse}})$? Dividing removes the average shrinkage, but at $b = 1$ bit it multiplies the random noise by $1 / 0.6366 \approx 1.57\times$ ($2.47\times$ in squared error). Instead, TurboQuant measures the leftover error vector directly using a 1-bit sign sketch called QJL (Quantized Johnson-Lindenstrauss).

    For any vector $r \in \mathbb{R}^d$, QJL draws a shared random $d \times d$ matrix $S$ whose entries are independent standard normal numbers $\mathcal{N}(0, 1)$. To compress $r$ to $1$ bit per coordinate, QJL saves only its length $\lVert r \rVert_2$ and the $\pm 1$ signs of $Sr$:

    $$z = \operatorname{sign}(Sr) \in \{-1, +1\}^d, \qquad \tilde{r}_{\mathrm{qjl}} = \frac{\sqrt{\pi / 2}}{d}\,\lVert r \rVert_2\,S^\top z.$$

    Where does the constant $\sqrt{\pi / 2} \approx 1.2533$ come from? Each entry of $Sr$ is a normal random variable with variance $\lVert r \rVert_2^2$. Taking its $\pm 1$ sign throws away its magnitude, which on average is $\sqrt{2 / \pi}\,\lVert r \rVert_2$, so multiplying by $\sqrt{\pi / 2}\,\lVert r \rVert_2$ restores the exact scale.

    Because the scale is restored exactly, the estimated dot product $\langle y, \tilde{r}_{\mathrm{qjl}} \rangle$ is unbiased on average, and its random variance is proportional to $\lVert r \rVert_2^2 / d$:

    $$\mathbb{E}_S\bigl[\langle y, \tilde{r}_{\mathrm{qjl}} \rangle\bigr] = \langle y, r \rangle, \qquad \operatorname{Var}_S\bigl(\langle y, \tilde{r}_{\mathrm{qjl}} \rangle\bigr) \le \frac{\pi}{2d}\,\lVert y \rVert_2^2\,\lVert r \rVert_2^2.$$

    Notice that $\lVert r \rVert_2^2$ sits right on top of the variance formula. If we feed a raw vector of length $1$ into 1-bit QJL, the variance is $\frac{\pi}{2d}\lVert y \rVert_2^2$. If we first shrink the vector down to a tiny residual $r$, the variance shrinks by that exact same factor.

    How $\mathrm{TurboQuant}_{\mathrm{prod}}$ combines rounding and QJL

    To quantize a vector $x$ with a budget of $b$ bits per coordinate, $\mathrm{TurboQuant}_{\mathrm{prod}}$ splits the budget into two stages:

    • Stage 1 ($b - 1$ bits): run $\mathrm{TurboQuant}_{\mathrm{mse}}$ with $b - 1$ bits per coordinate to get a coarse vector $\tilde{x}_{\mathrm{mse}}$, and compute the leftover residual $r = x - \tilde{x}_{\mathrm{mse}}$.
    • Stage 2 ($1$ bit): compress the residual $r$ with 1-bit QJL ($z = \operatorname{sign}(Sr)$ and the scalar norm $\gamma = \lVert r \rVert_2$), and add the reconstructed residual back to $\tilde{x}_{\mathrm{mse}}$.
    $$\tilde{x}_{\mathrm{prod}} = \tilde{x}_{\mathrm{mse}} + \frac{\sqrt{\pi / 2}}{d}\,\gamma\,S^\top z, \qquad \langle y, \tilde{x}_{\mathrm{prod}} \rangle = \langle y, \tilde{x}_{\mathrm{mse}} \rangle + \frac{\sqrt{\pi / 2}}{d}\,\gamma\,\langle Sy, z \rangle.$$

    Why does this two-stage split work so well? Stage 1 captures most of the vector so the residual has tiny squared length $\mathbb{E}[\lVert r \rVert_2^2] = D_{\mathrm{mse}}(b - 1)$. Stage 2 is unbiased on $r$, so the sum $\tilde{x}_{\mathrm{mse}} + \tilde{r}_{\mathrm{qjl}}$ has zero bias on $\langle y, x \rangle$ and its dot-product error is bounded by Stage 1's residual times Stage 2's $1/d$ variance:

    $$\mathbb{E}\bigl[\langle y, \tilde{x}_{\mathrm{prod}} \rangle\bigr] = \langle y, x \rangle, \qquad D_{\mathrm{prod}} = \mathbb{E}\bigl[\bigl(\langle y, x \rangle - \langle y, \tilde{x}_{\mathrm{prod}} \rangle\bigr)^2\bigr] \le \frac{\pi}{2d}\,\lVert y \rVert_2^2\,D_{\mathrm{mse}}(b - 1).$$

    At $b = 2$ bits ($1$ bit for Stage 1 + $1$ bit for Stage 2), Stage 1 leaves $D_{\mathrm{mse}}(1) \approx 0.3634$ of the vector's energy in $r$, so the total dot-product error is $\frac{\pi}{2} \times 0.3634 / d \approx 0.5708 / d$ — nearly $3\times$ smaller than running QJL alone.

    Check it on 4 numbers

    Let us trace a $2$-bit $\mathrm{TurboQuant}_{\mathrm{prod}}$ step on our $4$-number key vector $x$ from Part 1 and a query vector $y$:

    $$x = [0.9,\,-0.3,\,0.3,\,0.1]^\top, \qquad y = [0.7,\,-0.5,\,0.5,\,0.1]^\top, \qquad \langle y, x \rangle = 0.63 + 0.15 + 0.15 + 0.01 = 0.94.$$

    Stage 1 ($1$ bit of $\mathrm{TurboQuant}_{\mathrm{mse}}$): Using the $4 \times 4$ Walsh-Hadamard rotation $\Pi = \frac{1}{2}H_4$ from Part 1, the $1$-bit rounded vector is $\tilde{x}_{\mathrm{mse}} = [0.5585, -0.5585, 0, 0]^\top$. Its dot product with $y$ is shrunk by more than a third, leaving a residual $r = x - \tilde{x}_{\mathrm{mse}}$:

    $$\langle y, \tilde{x}_{\mathrm{mse}} \rangle = 0.7(0.5585) + (-0.5)(-0.5585) = 0.5585, \qquad r = x - \tilde{x}_{\mathrm{mse}} = [0.3415,\,0.2585,\,0.3000,\,0.1000]^\top.$$

    The residual has length $\gamma = \lVert r \rVert_2 = 0.5324$, and the true missing dot product is $\langle y, r \rangle = 0.94 - 0.5585 = 0.3815$.

    Stage 2 ($1$ bit of QJL on $r$): Using the test projection matrix $S$ in tools/check_turboquant.py, multiplying $S$ by $r$ and taking signs gives $z = \operatorname{sign}(Sr) = [+1, -1, +1, +1]^\top$. Adding the QJL correction brings the estimated dot product right back to the true value $0.94$:

    $$\langle y, \tilde{r}_{\mathrm{qjl}} \rangle = \frac{\sqrt{\pi / 2}}{4}\,(0.5324)\,\langle Sy, z \rangle = 0.4321 \quad\Longrightarrow\quad \langle y, \tilde{x}_{\mathrm{prod}} \rangle = 0.5585 + 0.4321 = 0.9906.$$

    A single $4 \times 4$ draw of $S$ has random noise around $0.94$, giving $0.9906$ (versus $0.5585$ without QJL). Averaging across $40{,}000$ random draws of $(\Pi, S)$ gives $0.5982$ for Stage 1 alone and $0.9408$ after the 1-bit QJL correction — matching the true dot product $0.9400$.
