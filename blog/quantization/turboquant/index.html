    TurboQuant: online vector quantization near the Shannon limit

    October 2026 · 5 min read

    Weight-only methods such as GPTQ or AWQ compress fixed model matrices once, offline, with a calibration set in hand. Key-value (KV) caches during long-context LLM decoding and high-dimensional embedding indexes face a harder regime: vectors arrive on the fly, and quantization must happen online without per-dataset codebook training or heavy optimization.

    Google Research's TurboQuant trilogy — combining PolarQuant, the Quantized Johnson-Lindenstrauss (QJL) transform, and the unified two-stage TurboQuant quantizer — solves this online problem by replacing data-dependent block scales with random geometry on the unit hypersphere $\mathbb{S}^{d-1}$:

    1. Normalize a $d$-dimensional vector $x \in \mathbb{R}^d$ to unit norm and apply a shared random orthogonal rotation $\Pi x$ (or recursive polar transformation in PolarQuant) so every coordinate follows a universal, known probability density regardless of input outliers.
    2. Snap each rotated coordinate independently to a precomputed 1D Lloyd-Max codebook ($\mathrm{TurboQuant}_{\mathrm{mse}}$), eliminating per-block scale and zero-point storage overhead.
    3. Because MSE-optimal codebooks systematically shrink inner products by $(1 - D_{\mathrm{mse}})$, spend the final bit of a $b$-bit budget on an asymmetric 1-bit QJL sign sketch of the residual $r = x - \tilde{x}_{\mathrm{mse}}$ ($\mathrm{TurboQuant}_{\mathrm{prod}}$) to restore an unbiased, low-variance inner-product estimator.

    Across every bit-width $b \ge 1$, the resulting expected squared error and inner-product distortion sit within a small constant factor ($\frac{\sqrt{3}\pi}{2} \approx 2.72$) of the universal Shannon and Yao minimax lower bounds.

    Two distortion targets: Euclidean reconstruction vs. inner products

    Given an input vector $x \in \mathbb{R}^d$ and a bit budget of $b$ bits per coordinate, an online randomized quantizer $Q : \mathbb{R}^d \to \{0,1\}^{bd}$ with reconstruction map $Q^{-1}$ is judged by two different criteria depending on the workload:

    $$D_{\mathrm{mse}}(Q) = \sup_{\lVert x \rVert_2 = 1} \mathbb{E}\bigl[\lVert x - Q^{-1}(Q(x)) \rVert_2^2\bigr], \qquad D_{\mathrm{prod}}(Q) = \sup_{\lVert x \rVert_2 = \lVert y \rVert_2 = 1} \mathbb{E}\bigl[\lvert \langle y, x \rangle - \langle y, Q^{-1}(Q(x)) \rangle \rvert^2\bigr].$$

    Minimizing $D_{\mathrm{mse}}$ alone is not enough for attention logits $\langle q, k \rangle$ or cosine-similarity search. As Part 2 proves, any Lloyd-Max quantizer satisfies $\mathbb{E}[\langle y, \tilde{x}_{\mathrm{mse}} \rangle] = (1 - D_{\mathrm{mse}})\langle y, x \rangle$. At $b=1$ bit, that shrinks every logit to $\frac{2}{\pi} \approx 63.7\%$ of its true value unless a residual QJL stage removes the bias.

    The distinctions to keep straight

    • Offline weight PTQ versus online vector quantization: GPTQ solves a calibration-set least-squares problem once for static weight matrices. TurboQuant quantizes dynamic vectors (KV cache entries or streaming embeddings) on the fly in $O(d \log d)$ or $O(d^2)$ time with a fixed, data-oblivious codebook.
    • Block scale metadata versus concentration after rotation: grouping $G=32$ coordinates with a 16-bit scale and 16-bit zero-point adds $32/32 = 1.0$ extra bit per coordinate. A random rotation equalizes coordinate variances to $1/d$ so a single vector norm (plus one residual norm in $\mathrm{TurboQuant}_{\mathrm{prod}}$) suffices across the whole head or sub-vector.
    • Cartesian Beta grids ($\mathrm{TurboQuant}_{\mathrm{mse}}$) versus recursive polar grids (PolarQuant): $\mathrm{TurboQuant}_{\mathrm{mse}}$ quantizes rotated Cartesian coordinates directly against a 1D Beta/Gaussian Lloyd-Max codebook; PolarQuant recursively pairs coordinates into angles whose densities concentrate tightly around $\pi/4$ at deeper levels.
    • MSE reconstruction ($\mathrm{TurboQuant}_{\mathrm{mse}}$) versus unbiased inner product ($\mathrm{TurboQuant}_{\mathrm{prod}}$): $\mathrm{TurboQuant}_{\mathrm{mse}}$ minimizes $\lVert x - \tilde{x} \rVert_2^2$ using all $b$ bits. $\mathrm{TurboQuant}_{\mathrm{prod}}$ uses $(b-1)$ bits for $\mathrm{TurboQuant}_{\mathrm{mse}}$ and 1 bit of QJL on the residual so $\mathbb{E}[\langle y, \tilde{x} \rangle] = \langle y, x \rangle$.

    A small result you can check

    In the worked 4D example, a unit key vector $x = [0.9, -0.3, 0.3, 0.1]$ (with $81\%$ of its energy in the first channel) and a unit query $y = [0.7, -0.5, 0.5, 0.1]$ have exact inner product $\langle y, x \rangle = 0.94$. A 1-bit Lloyd-Max quantizer after signed Hadamard rotation shrinks the inner product to $0.558519$ (error $-0.381481$), while adding a 1-bit QJL correction on the residual $r = x - \tilde{x}_{\mathrm{mse}}$ removes the bias in expectation ($0.9408$ across $40{,}000$ Monte Carlo draws, and $0.990565$ on the single deterministic demo matrix).

    The standalone verification script tools/check_turboquant.py checks the Lloyd-Max centroids, Panter-Dite and Shannon bounds, the 4D Cartesian and polar examples, and the effective bit-budget accounting.

    When should you use TurboQuant instead of GPTQ or SmoothQuant?

    Use GPTQ (or AWQ) for static model weights, SmoothQuant for joint W8A8 weight-and-activation matrix multiplies, and TurboQuant / PolarQuant / QJL when the memory or bandwidth bottleneck is the growing KV cache in long-context generation or high-dimensional vector search indexes.
