    Optimizers in Deep Learning: From SGD to Matrix Geometry

    September 2026 · 3 min read + interactive lab

    Ten detailed chapters with worked updates, original figures, and interactive experiments.

    Backpropagation computes a gradient. The optimizer decides what to do with it. This series follows that decision from a single learning rate to memory, coordinate-wise scales, and matrix geometry. Every chapter connects the equations to a small calculation and an experiment you can inspect.

    What changes between optimizers?

    FamilyQuestion it answersMethods
    One global scaleHow far should we step along the gradient?SGD
    Directional memoryWhich directions persist across steps?Momentum
    Coordinate-wise adaptationHow large has each coordinate’s gradient or update been?Adadelta, RMSprop, Adam
    Matrix structureHow should we treat coupled row, column, and singular directions?Muon, Shampoo, SOAP
    Sign of a filtered gradientWhich way should each coordinate move?Lion

    Notation that stays consistent

    $w_t$ is the parameter after step $t$; $g_t$ is evaluated at $w_{t-1}$. A matrix parameter is $W$, and its gradient is $G$. The learning rate is $\eta$. Squares, square roots, and divisions on vectors are element-wise; matrix products are explicitly identified. A zero-initialized buffer means its value before the first update is zero.

    How to use the experiments

    Try the suggested experiment before changing everything at once. The first-order labs use the same two-dimensional quadratic dataset, seeded sampling, and exact gradients. Matrix labs expose the small matrices used in every calculation. These are controlled teaching experiments, not neural-network benchmarks or a ranking of optimizers.

    Each chapter includes a static figure and worked values, so the argument also works without JavaScript. Interactive plots have text readouts and explicit step controls. No experiment starts playing automatically.

    Before you start

    You need partial derivatives, vector addition, and the idea of a training loss. The matrix chapters introduce the needed singular-value and eigenvalue geometry. For broader context, see the numerical optimization notes.
