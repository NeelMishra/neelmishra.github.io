    Weight Decay and Norm Constraints: Control the Right Quantity

    September 2026 · 4 min read + interactive lab

    See why L2 regularization and weight decay diverge in adaptive optimizers, how max-norm projection enforces a hard ball, and how spectral normalization bounds Lipschitz amplification.

    We can control parameter scale in three distinct ways: add an L2 penalty $\frac\lambda2\|w\|_2^2$ to the loss, apply direct multiplicative weight decay $(1-\eta\lambda)w$ in the optimizer, or project weights onto a norm constraint after each step. Only in plain SGD do the first two coincide.

    1. Plain SGD vs. preconditioned / adaptive updates

    Let $g=\nabla L(w)$ be the data gradient. Under plain SGD, adding $\frac\lambda2\|w\|_2^2$ to the loss adds $\lambda w$ to the gradient, which rearranges into direct multiplicative decay:

    $$w^+=w-\eta(g+\lambda w)=(1-\eta\lambda)w-\eta g.$$

    Now suppose an adaptive optimizer scales coordinates by a diagonal preconditioner $P_t$ (such as $(\sqrt{\widehat v_t}+\epsilon)^{-1}$ in Adam). Comparing coupled L2 against decoupled weight decay (AdamW) shows why they differ:

    $$\begin{aligned}\text{Coupled L2 through }P_t:\quad w^+&=w-\eta P_t(g+\lambda w)=(I-\eta\lambda P_t)w-\eta P_tg,\\\text{Decoupled decay (AdamW)}:\quad w^+&=(1-\eta\lambda)w-\eta P_tg.\end{aligned}$$

    In coupled L2, the shrinkage factor $\eta\lambda P_t$ is divided by the historical gradient magnitude—so coordinates with large gradients ($P_{ii}$ small) receive less weight decay. Take $w=(2,2)$, $g=(0,0)$, $P=\operatorname{diag}(1,0.1)$, $\eta=0.1$, and $\lambda=0.2$:

    • Coupled L2: $w^+=\bigl(2(1-0.02\times 1),\,2(1-0.02\times 0.1)\bigr)=(1.96,\,1.996)$.
    • Decoupled weight decay: $w^+=\bigl(2(1-0.02),\,2(1-0.02)\bigr)=(1.96,\,1.96)$.

    3. Max-norm projection: enforce a hard boundary

    Instead of shrinking weights at every step—even when they are already small—a max-norm constraint requires $\|w\|_2\le c$ (typically applied separately to each neuron’s incoming weight row). Projected gradient descent takes the unconstrained step $z=w-\eta g$ and projects onto the ball of radius $c$:

    $$w^+=\Pi_{\|w\|_2\le c}(z)=z\cdot\min\!\left(1,\,\frac{c}{\|z\|_2}\right)=\begin{cases}z,&\|z\|_2\le c,\\c\,\dfrac{z}{\|z\|_2},&\|z\|_2>c.\end{cases}$$

    For $z=(3,4)$ (so $\|z\|_2=5$) and radius $c=2$, projection rescales $z$ by $2/5$ to $w^+=(1.2,1.6)$. Any proposal already inside $\|z\|_2\le 2$ is left untouched.

    4. Spectral normalization: bound layer amplification

    Frobenius/L2 norm sums all squared singular values $\|W\|_F^2=\sum_i\sigma_i^2$, whereas the worst-case amplification of a linear layer is its spectral (operator) norm:

    $$\|W\|_2=\max_{\|x\|_2=1}\|Wx\|_2=\sigma_{\max}(W).$$

    Spectral normalization divides the weight matrix by its largest singular value (estimated via 1 step of power iteration $u\leftarrow Wu/\|Wu\|_2$, $v\leftarrow W^\top u/\|W^\top u\|_2$, $\sigma_{\max}\approx u^\top Wv$ per forward pass):

    $$\bar W=\frac{c\,W}{\sigma_{\max}(W)}\implies\|\bar Wx_1-\bar Wx_2\|_2\le c\|x_1-x_2\|_2.$$

    For example, $W=\operatorname{diag}(3,1)$ has $\sigma_{\max}(W)=3$; normalizing to $c=1$ produces $\bar W=\operatorname{diag}(1,1/3)$, guaranteeing the layer is $1$-Lipschitz while preserving the ratio of its singular values.

    OperationFormula / RuleWhat it controls
    Decoupled weight decay$w\leftarrow(1-\eta\lambda)w-\eta P_tg$Uniform fractional parameter shrinkage per step.
    Max-norm projection$w\leftarrow z\min(1,c/\|z\|_2)$Hard upper bound $c$ on a weight vector’s Euclidean norm.
    Spectral normalization$\bar W=W/\sigma_{\max}(W)$Worst-case directional amplification (Lipschitz constant $\le 1$).
    Gradient clipping$g\leftarrow g\min(1,\tau/\|g\|_2)$Single-step update size $\eta\|g\|_2$, not the parameter norm $\|w\|_2$.

    5. Questions worth answering

    Why isn’t `loss + 0.5 * wd * (w ** 2).sum()` with Adam equivalent to AdamW?

    Because adding $\lambda w$ to the loss gradient feeds $\lambda w$ into Adam’s $m_t$ and $v_t$ buffers and divides the penalty by $\sqrt{\widehat v_t}$, shrinking high-gradient weights less than low-gradient weights.

    Does gradient clipping bound the weight norm $\|w\|_2$?

    No. Gradient clipping bounds the step size $\|\Delta w\|_2\le\eta\tau$ on each update, but many small steps in the same direction can still grow $\|w\|_2$ arbitrarily large.
