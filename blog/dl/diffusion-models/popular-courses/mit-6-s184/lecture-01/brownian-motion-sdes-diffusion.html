    Brownian Motion, SDEs & Diffusion Models

    September 2026 · 23 min read · MIT 6.S184, Lecture 1

    From one path to a distribution over paths. An ODE gives one deterministic trajectory for each initial point. An SDE adds fresh randomness through time, so the same initial point can lead to many possible trajectories.

    A stochastic process is a random trajectory

    A stochastic process is a collection of random variables indexed by time:

    $$X=(X_t)_{0\le t\le1},\qquad X_t\in\mathbb R^d.$$

    One run produces one sample path $t\mapsto X_t$. Running the process again can produce a different path even from the same initial condition.

    ODE

    $X_0$ fixed + vector field fixed $\Rightarrow$ one trajectory.

    SDE

    $X_0$ fixed + drift/diffusion fixed $\Rightarrow$ a probability distribution over trajectories.

    Brownian motion

    Brownian motion $W=(W_t)_{t\ge0}$ is the continuous-time limit of a random walk. In $d$ dimensions it has three defining properties:

    1. Starts at zero: $W_0=0$.
    2. Independent Gaussian increments: for $0\le s<t$,
      $$W_t-W_s\sim\mathcal N(0,(t-s)I_d),$$
      and increments over disjoint time intervals are independent.
    3. Continuous sample paths: $t\mapsto W_t$ is continuous with probability one.

    Brownian paths are continuous but almost surely nowhere differentiable. They move without jumps, yet remain too rough to possess an ordinary instantaneous velocity.

    Drift plus diffusion

    A stochastic differential equation combines systematic motion and Brownian motion:

    $$\boxed{dX_t=u_t(X_t)\,dt+\sigma_t\,dW_t}.$$
    TermRole
    $u_t(X_t)\,dt$Drift: directed motion from the vector field.
    $\sigma_t\,dW_t$Diffusion: random motion scaled by $\sigma_t$.
    $\sigma_t=0$The SDE reduces to an ODE.
    larger $\sigma_t$Trajectories spread more strongly.

    Why Brownian noise scales as $\sqrt h$

    Over a time interval of length $h$, a Brownian increment satisfies

    $$\Delta W_k=W_{t_k+h}-W_{t_k}\sim\mathcal N(0,hI_d).$$

    If $\varepsilon_k\sim\mathcal N(0,I_d)$, then

    $$\Delta W_k\overset{d}{=}\sqrt h\,\varepsilon_k,$$

    because multiplying by $\sqrt h$ multiplies variance by $h$. Using $h\varepsilon_k$ would give variance $h^2$, which is the wrong process.

    Euler–Maruyama

    The SDE analogue of Euler's method is

    $$\boxed{X_{k+1}=X_k+h\,u_{t_k}(X_k)+\sigma_{t_k}\sqrt h\,\varepsilon_k,\qquad\varepsilon_k\sim\mathcal N(0,I_d)}.$$
    Euler–Maruyama SDE sampler
    1Choose $n$, set $h=1/n$, and initialize $X_0$.
    2for $k=0,\ldots,n-1$:
    3Draw fresh $\varepsilon_k\sim\mathcal N(0,I_d)$.
    4$X_{k+1}\leftarrow X_k+h\,u_{t_k}(X_k)+\sigma_{t_k}\sqrt h\,\varepsilon_k$
    5Return the endpoint $X_n$ or the complete random path.

    Ornstein–Uhlenbeck: contraction with noise

    The Ornstein–Uhlenbeck process adds Brownian noise to the linear contraction field:

    $$dX_t=-\theta X_t\,dt+\sigma\,dW_t.$$

    The drift pulls toward zero while diffusion spreads paths apart. Starting from fixed $X_0=x_0$,

    $$\mathbb E[X_t]=e^{-\theta t}x_0,$$
    $$\operatorname{Var}(X_t)=\frac{\sigma^2}{2\theta}\left(1-e^{-2\theta t}\right).$$

    The mean follows the deterministic ODE. The variance grows from zero and approaches $\sigma^2/(2\theta)$: drift and diffusion reach a balance.

    Does an SDE have a well-defined solution?

    As with ODEs, regularity matters. If the drift and diffusion coefficients are Lipschitz with suitable growth bounds, the SDE has a unique strong solution. Informally: once the initial condition and Brownian path are fixed, the resulting $X_t$ path is well-defined.

    Sampling from a diffusion model

    A diffusion model uses a learned drift $u^\theta_t$ and a chosen diffusion schedule $\sigma_t$:

    $$X_0\sim p_{\text{init}},\qquad dX_t=u^\theta_t(X_t)\,dt+\sigma_t\,dW_t,\qquad X_1\sim p_{\text{data}}.$$
    Diffusion-model sampler
    1Draw $X_0\sim p_{\text{init}}$.
    2Choose $h=1/n$.
    3for $k=0,\ldots,n-1$:
    4Draw $\varepsilon_k\sim\mathcal N(0,I_d)$.
    5$X_{k+1}\leftarrow X_k+h\,u^\theta_{t_k}(X_k)+\sigma_{t_k}\sqrt h\,\varepsilon_k$
    6Return $X_n$.
