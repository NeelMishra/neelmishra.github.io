    Generative Modeling as Distribution Transport

    September 2026 · 18 min read · MIT 6.S184: Generative AI with Stochastic Differential Equations, Lecture 1

    The single idea behind the lecture. A generative model starts with a distribution that is easy to sample and moves those samples until their final distribution looks like the data. Flow models move them deterministically; diffusion models allow random motion along the way.

    What kind of object is generated?

    A generated object is represented by a vector $x\in\mathbb R^d$. The dimension may be enormous:

    Data typeOne representationDimension
    ImageAll red, green, and blue pixel values$H\times W\times3$
    VideoAll pixel values across frames$T\times H\times W\times3$
    MoleculeAtom types and 3D coordinatesDepends on representation
    AudioA waveform or time-frequency arrayNumber of samples or spectral bins

    The mathematics does not require $x$ to be an image. It only needs a space in which samples can be represented and moved.

    The data distribution

    Imagine an unknown probability distribution $p_{\text{data}}$ over valid objects. It assigns substantial probability to neighborhoods filled with realistic dog images and negligible probability to arbitrary pixel arrangements such as random noise.

    $$x\sim p_{\text{data}}.$$

    Generation means drawing a new sample from that distribution. The difficulty is that $p_{\text{data}}$ is not given as a formula. Only a finite dataset is available:

    $$\mathcal D=\{x^{(1)},\ldots,x^{(N)}\},\qquad x^{(i)}\sim p_{\text{data}}.$$

    The training set provides examples, not the density itself and not a direct sampler for unseen objects.

    Likelihood is a useful intuition, not a complete definition of quality. A sample in a high-density region should resemble the training domain, but human quality also depends on semantics, prompt alignment, and the geometry of high-dimensional probability. The distributional view formalizes generation; it does not replace evaluation.

    Conditional generation

    Unconditional generation asks for any plausible data sample. Conditional generation fixes side information $y$—for example a text prompt, class label, segmentation map, or another image—and samples

    $$x\sim p_{\text{data}}(x\mid y).$$

    For the prompt “a picture of a dog,” a cat may be a realistic image but a poor conditional sample. The condition changes which part of the data distribution is relevant.

    Start from a distribution that is easy to sample

    A model cannot begin by sampling $p_{\text{data}}$—that is the problem being solved. It begins with a simple reference distribution such as a standard Gaussian:

    $$X_0\sim p_{\text{init}}=\mathcal N(0,I_d).$$

    Sampling independent Gaussian noise is cheap in any dimension. The model's job is to transform this unstructured source into a structured endpoint:

    $$X_0\sim p_{\text{init}}\quad\xrightarrow{\text{generative process}}\quad X_1\sim p_{\text{data}}.$$

    A path of distributions

    Rather than jumping from noise to data, introduce a continuous time variable $t\in[0,1]$. At every time, the random variable $X_t$ has a distribution $p_t$:

    $$X_t\sim p_t,\qquad p_0=p_{\text{init}},\qquad p_1=p_{\text{data}}.$$

    The family $(p_t)_{0\le t\le1}$ is a probability path. Individual particles move through space; the cloud formed by many particles reveals how the distribution changes.

    Two mechanisms for moving samples

    Flow model

    A deterministic velocity field tells each point how to move. Once $X_0$ is sampled, its entire trajectory is fixed.

    $$dX_t=u_t(X_t)\,dt.$$

    Diffusion model

    A drift field gives the systematic motion while Brownian noise continually adds random motion.

    $$dX_t=u_t(X_t)\,dt+\sigma_t\,dW_t.$$

    Both aim for the same endpoint distribution. The distinction is about the paths: one trajectory per initial point for a flow, many possible random trajectories for a diffusion.

    Sampling and training are separate questions

    Lecture 1 asks: if a useful vector field is already available, how does it generate samples? Later lectures answer the training question: how can that field be learned from data?
