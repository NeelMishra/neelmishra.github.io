    Flow Matching, Score Matching & Diffusion Models

    September 2026 · 30 min read · MIT 6.S184, Lecture 3

    One probability path, several equivalent training views. Flow matching learns the velocity that transports a path of densities. Score matching learns the spatial gradient of log density. For nondegenerate Gaussian schedules at interior times, the two targets are algebraically convertible, so denoising diffusion models can train a score network and use it to build the vector field needed by an ODE or SDE sampler.

    What this lecture adds

    Lecture 2 built conditional paths, marginal fields, and the SDE extension; the preceding notes also previewed conditional flow matching. This lecture puts those pieces beside the training objectives used in modern systems:

    Flow matching

    Regress a vector field $u_t^\theta(x)$ toward the velocity of a chosen probability path. With a straight-line path, the target is simply $z-\varepsilon$.

    Score matching

    Regress a score network $s_t^\theta(x)$ toward $\nabla_x\log p_t(x)$. For Gaussian conditional paths, the target is available from the injected noise.

    The important engineering conclusion is not that one objective is universally better. It is that the same Gaussian path gives both a tractable target and a conversion between the two parameterizations.

    Set the time direction and notation first

    Throughout these notes, $t=0$ is noise and $t=1$ is data. We sample $Z\sim p_{\mathrm{data}}$ independently of $\varepsilon\sim\mathcal N(0,I_d)$, then form $X_t=\alpha_tZ+\beta_t\varepsilon$. The schedules satisfy $(\alpha_0,\beta_0)=(0,1)$ and $(\alpha_1,\beta_1)=(1,0)$, with $\beta_t>0$ in the interior. A dot means a time derivative; $\nabla_x$ means a spatial gradient.

    QuantityMeaningAvailable during training?
    $p_t(x\mid z)$Noise cloud associated with one endpoint $z$Yes: chosen analytically
    $p_t(x)$Mixture after hiding the endpointEasy to sample; usually hard to evaluate

    Lecture 2's marginalization proof tells us which field transports $p_t$. Here we turn that formula into training. The endpoint $Z$ is a hidden regression label, not a text prompt or a conditioning input for guided generation.

    Flow matching as supervised regression

    If the marginal path $p_t$ and its target velocity $u_t^{\mathrm{target}}$ were available, the ideal objective would be

    $$\mathcal L_{\mathrm{FM}}(\theta)= \mathbb E_{t\sim\mathrm{Unif}[0,1],\,X_t\sim p_t} \left[\left\|u_t^\theta(X_t)-u_t^{\mathrm{target}}(X_t)\right\|^2\right].$$

    Sampling the marginal path is easy once we choose a conditional construction. The inaccessible part is evaluating the marginal velocity at that sample: its formula contains a posterior average over data. The conditional construction supplies a sample and a computable conditional label:

    $$Z\sim p_{\mathrm{data}},\qquad \varepsilon\sim\mathcal N(0,I_d),\qquad X_t=\alpha_tZ+\beta_t\varepsilon,$$
    $$u_t^{\mathrm{target}}(X_t\mid Z)=\dot\alpha_tZ+\dot\beta_t\varepsilon.$$

    So the trainable conditional flow-matching loss is

    $$\boxed{\mathcal L_{\mathrm{CFM}}(\theta)= \mathbb E_{t,Z,\varepsilon}\left[ \left\|u_t^\theta(\alpha_tZ+\beta_t\varepsilon) -(\dot\alpha_tZ+\dot\beta_t\varepsilon)\right\|^2\right].}$$

    The network sees only $(t,X_t)$. $Z$ and $\varepsilon$ are used to construct the training label, not passed into the model.

    Why conditional targets are valid

    At a fixed $x$, several hidden pairs $(Z,\varepsilon)$ may have produced the same noisy point. Their conditional velocities can disagree. Squared-error regression resolves that ambiguity by taking their conditional mean:

    $$\mathbb E\left[u_t^{\mathrm{target}}(X_t\mid Z)\mid X_t=x\right] =u_t^{\mathrm{target}}(x).$$

    Writing the random conditional target as its mean plus a zero-mean residual makes the reason explicit:

    $$U=m(X_t)+\eta,\qquad \mathbb E[\eta\mid X_t]=0,$$
    $$\mathbb E\|u_t^\theta(X_t)-U\|^2 =\mathbb E\|u_t^\theta(X_t)-m(X_t)\|^2+\mathbb E\|\eta\|^2.$$

    The second term is independent of $\theta$, so conditional flow matching and ideal marginal flow matching have the same minimizer and the same parameter gradients. The conditional labels may be noisy, but they are noisy around exactly the field we want.

    The constant has a sign, and time belongs in the conditioning

    The preceding argument fixes $t$. If time is random, write $m(t,X_t)=\mathbb E[U\mid t,X_t]$ and $\mathbb E[\eta\mid t,X_t]=0$. The orthogonality calculation is

    $$\mathbb E[(u_t^\theta(X_t)-m(t,X_t))^\top\eta] =\mathbb E[(u_t^\theta(X_t)-m(t,X_t))^\top\mathbb E[\eta\mid t,X_t]]=0.$$
    $$\boxed{\mathcal L_{\mathrm{CFM}}=\mathcal L_{\mathrm{FM}}+C_{\mathrm{flow}},\qquad C_{\mathrm{flow}}=\mathbb E\|U-m(t,X_t)\|^2\ge0.}$$

    Thus the conditional loss is at least the marginal loss. A positive training loss can remain even at the exact marginal field. Equality of gradients is a statement about the expected objectives, assuming finite second moments and differentiation under the expectation; individual stochastic gradient estimates can differ. With a restricted neural network, both objectives still have the same minimizers within that model class, but the exact target may not be representable.

    The straight-line path

    The most convenient choice is $\alpha_t=t$ and $\beta_t=1-t$:

    $$X_t=(1-t)\varepsilon+tZ,\qquad \dot X_t=Z-\varepsilon.$$

    The target no longer depends explicitly on time. A complete training example is:

    Flow matching training procedure
    1sample a data point $Z$ and noise $\varepsilon\sim\mathcal N(0,I_d)$
    2sample $t\sim\mathrm{Unif}[0,1]$
    3construct $X_t=(1-t)\varepsilon+tZ$
    4compute $\mathcal L=\|u_t^\theta(X_t)-(Z-\varepsilon)\|^2$
    5update $\theta$ by gradient descent

    At $t=0$ the input is noise and the target points toward the data sample. At $t=1$ the point reaches $Z$. At inference, integrate the learned ODE from noise to data; no diffusion noise is required.

    Straight conditional paths need not produce straight generated paths. Each fixed pair $(\varepsilon,z)$ traces a line. The learned field averages many possible pair velocities at $(t,x)$, so its ODE trajectories can curve. The lecture calls this a conditional optimal-transport (CondOT) path: straight motion to a fixed endpoint does not establish a globally optimal coupling between the source and data distributions.

    Score matching

    The score of a differentiable density is

    $$s_t(x)\doteq\nabla_x\log p_t(x).$$

    It points in the direction of increasing log density. A direct score-matching objective would be

    $$\mathcal L_{\mathrm{SM}}(\theta)= \mathbb E_{t,X_t\sim p_t}\left[ \left\|s_t^\theta(X_t)-\nabla_x\log p_t(X_t)\right\|^2\right].$$

    That marginal score is usually inaccessible because $p_t$ is a mixture over data. The Gaussian conditional score is easy:

    $$p_t(x\mid z)=\mathcal N(\alpha_tz,\beta_t^2I_d) \quad\Longrightarrow\quad \boxed{s_t(x\mid z)=-\frac{x-\alpha_tz}{\beta_t^2}}.$$

    Denoising score matching

    Sample $X_t=\alpha_tZ+\beta_t\varepsilon$. Since $X_t-\alpha_tZ=\beta_t\varepsilon$, the conditional score becomes

    $$s_t(X_t\mid Z)=-\frac{\varepsilon}{\beta_t}.$$

    Regress the network against this target:

    $$\boxed{\mathcal L_{\mathrm{DSM}}(\theta)= \mathbb E_{t,Z,\varepsilon}\left[ \left\|s_t^\theta(\alpha_tZ+\beta_t\varepsilon) +\frac{\varepsilon}{\beta_t}\right\|^2\right].}$$

    Just as with flow matching, conditional regression learns the marginal score because the squared-loss minimizer is the conditional mean. The conditional score is a noisy but unbiased label for the score at the observed $X_t$.

    Small noise is numerically delicate. As $\beta_t\to0$, the target $-\varepsilon/\beta_t$ becomes large. This is why implementations choose a carefully designed noise schedule, weight the loss by noise level, or parameterize the network to predict a rescaled quantity.

    Why denoising learns the score of the mixture

    Let $q_t(x\mid z)=p_t(x\mid z)$. Differentiate the mixture density, then insert Bayes' rule:

    $$\begin{aligned} s_t(x)&=\frac{\int \nabla_x q_t(x\mid z)p_{\mathrm{data}}(z)\,dz}{p_t(x)}\\ &=\int \nabla_x\log q_t(x\mid z)\, \underbrace{\frac{q_t(x\mid z)p_{\mathrm{data}}(z)}{p_t(x)}}_{p_t(z\mid x)}\,dz\\ &=\mathbb E[-\varepsilon/\beta_t\mid t,X_t=x]. \end{aligned}$$

    This is a posterior average, not an unweighted average over endpoints and not the score of a single Gaussian. Applying the same orthogonal-residual proof gives $\mathcal L_{\mathrm{DSM}}=\mathcal L_{\mathrm{SM}}+C_{\mathrm{score}}$ with $C_{\mathrm{score}}\ge0$, whenever these expectations are finite.

    Noise prediction makes the denoising objective usable

    Instead of predicting a quantity whose target explodes at small $\beta_t$, parameterize the score as $s_t^\theta(x)=-\varepsilon_t^\theta(x)/\beta_t$. Substitution gives

    $$\|s_t^\theta(X_t)+\varepsilon/\beta_t\|^2 =\frac{1}{\beta_t^2}\|\varepsilon_t^\theta(X_t)-\varepsilon\|^2.$$

    Multiplying the DSM loss at each time by $\beta_t^2$ therefore produces ordinary noise-prediction MSE. The network learns $\mathbb E[\varepsilon\mid t,X_t]$, not the exact noise used in every ambiguous training pair. A positive time-only weight preserves the unrestricted pointwise optimum; it changes which times dominate optimization and can change the best fit of a finite model.

    Excluding the endpoint alone does not ensure a finite loss. For $\beta_t=1-t$, the conditional score target has second moment $d/(1-t)^2$. Its time integral diverges under uniform sampling all the way to 1. Use a specified time cutoff or suitable weighting when invoking the finite-loss proof. Weighted noise prediction avoids this exploding label, but converting it back to a score still requires care near zero noise.
    One noise-prediction training step

    Draw a minibatch of independent $(t,z,\varepsilon)$; compute $x=\alpha_tz+\beta_t\varepsilon$; predict $\widehat\varepsilon=\varepsilon_t^\theta(x)$; average $\|\widehat\varepsilon-\varepsilon\|^2$ over the batch; differentiate that scalar loss and update $\theta$. No ODE or SDE is solved during this training step.

    One Gaussian path, two equivalent targets

    For the Gaussian path, write the conditional sample as $x=\alpha_tz+\beta_t\varepsilon$. The conditional velocity is $u_t=\dot\alpha_tz+\dot\beta_t\varepsilon$, while the conditional score is $s_t=-\varepsilon/\beta_t$. Eliminating $z$ and $\varepsilon$ gives

    $$\boxed{u_t(x)= \frac{\dot\alpha_t}{\alpha_t}x+ \left(\frac{\dot\alpha_t}{\alpha_t}\beta_t^2-\beta_t\dot\beta_t\right)s_t(x).}$$

    The same linear relation holds for the marginal fields because both are posterior averages of their conditional counterparts. For the straight-line path, $\alpha_t=t$, $\beta_t=1-t$, so

    $$u_t(x)=\frac{x}{t}+\frac{1-t}{t}\,s_t(x).$$

    This is the practical bridge: train either a vector-field network with flow matching or a score network with denoising score matching, then convert the prediction when the sampler needs the other parameterization. They are not two unrelated models.

    Derive the conversion instead of memorizing it

    At a fixed interior time, $\varepsilon=-\beta_t s_t(x\mid z)$ and $z=(x+\beta_t^2s_t(x\mid z))/\alpha_t$. Substitute both into $\dot\alpha_tz+\dot\beta_t\varepsilon$. The coefficients depend only on time, so posterior averaging leaves them unchanged and gives the marginal identity above.

    Define $A_t=\dot\alpha_t/\alpha_t$ and $B_t=A_t\beta_t^2-\beta_t\dot\beta_t$. Then $u_t=A_tx+B_ts_t$, and the inverse is

    $$s_t(x)=\frac{u_t(x)-A_tx}{B_t},\qquad \alpha_t\ne0,\ B_t\ne0.$$

    For the straight-line schedule, $B_t=(1-t)/t$, so $s_t(x)=(tu_t(x)-x)/(1-t)$ for $0<t<1$. The factor $1/t$ in the forward conversion is essential.

    $$u_t^\theta(x)=\frac{x-\varepsilon_t^\theta(x)}{t}\quad\text{(straight-line path, noise prediction).}$$

    This formula is singular at $t=0$. Although the exact marginal field may have a finite limit, cancellation of large terms can amplify prediction errors. A directly trained velocity network does not need this division to start its ODE at noise.

    Convertible outputs do not mean identical unweighted losses

    For predictions tied by $u_t^\theta=A_tx+B_ts_t^\theta$, the conditional residual obeys

    $$\|u_t^\theta(X_t)-u_t(X_t\mid Z)\|^2 =B_t^2\|s_t^\theta(X_t)-s_t(X_t\mid Z)\|^2.$$

    Consequently, flow matching corresponds to a particular time-weighted DSM loss, not generally unweighted DSM. For the straight-line path it corresponds to noise-prediction error weighted by $1/t^2$. Parameterization, time sampling, and loss weighting therefore matter even though exact outputs are algebraically related.

    Denoising diffusion models

    In this lecture's terminology, a denoising diffusion model is a diffusion model built from a Gaussian probability path. The score network is trained with DSM, and the learned score supplies the correction needed by a stochastic sampler:

    $$dX_t=\left[ u_t^{\mathrm{target}}(X_t)+\frac{\sigma_t^2}{2}s_t(X_t) \right]dt+\sigma_t\,dW_t.$$

    The ODE limit sets $\sigma_t=0$ and uses the flow field alone. A nonzero diffusion coefficient adds randomness, while the score term counteracts the density spreading so the one-time marginals remain $p_t$. For a nondegenerate Gaussian schedule, the conversion supplies both terms from one network. Exact marginal preservation assumes the true fields, the correct initial law, and a well-defined continuous-time process; learned fields and finite solver steps introduce error.

    Lecture 3 in one sentence

    Flow matching and diffusion models choose different quantities to regress, but on a Gaussian probability path their targets are algebraically linked; conditional sampling makes both objectives trainable, and the learned field or score can be used to transport noise into data.

    From a trained network to samples

    Training draws arbitrary points on the chosen path. Sampling follows a trajectory sequentially. For a velocity network, start with $x_0\sim\mathcal N(0,I_d)$ and use the Euler update at $t_k=k/N$, with $h=1/N$:

    $$x_{k+1}=x_k+h\,u_{t_k}^\theta(x_k),\qquad k=0,\ldots,N-1.$$

    Return $x_N$. It is an approximate generated sample, not a known training endpoint. Smaller steps reduce numerical error under suitable regularity, but cannot remove errors in the learned field.

    For the stochastic version, Euler–Maruyama uses a fresh independent $\xi_k\sim\mathcal N(0,I_d)$ at each step:

    $$x_{k+1}=x_k+h\left[u_{t_k}^\theta(x_k)+\frac{\sigma_{t_k}^2}{2}s_{t_k}^\theta(x_k)\right] +\sigma_{t_k}\sqrt h\,\xi_k.$$

    The Brownian increment scales as $\sqrt h$, not $h$. The score correction has a plus sign because time runs from noise to data here. This is the SDE extension of a chosen path; it should not be mixed with reverse-time formulas written using the opposite time convention.

    When computing a score from velocity, $s_t^\theta=(tu_t^\theta-x)/(1-t)$ is defined at $t=0$ but not $t=1$. Left-endpoint updates avoid evaluating the latter, though near-terminal stiffness remains. Starting a score-to-velocity sampler at $t=\delta>0$ instead of zero avoids its $1/t$ division, but requires an initial sample from $p_\delta$ for exactness. Using standard noise there is an approximation, not an exact endpoint fix.

    A complete one-dimensional example

    Take $t=1/4$, $z=2$, and $\varepsilon=-1$. Under the straight-line path:

    StepCalculationResult
    Network input$x=(3/4)(-1)+(1/4)2$$-1/4$
    Flow label$z-\varepsilon$$3$
    Score label$-\varepsilon/(1-t)$$4/3$
    Recovered velocity$x/t+[(1-t)/t]s=-1+3(4/3)$$3$
    Recovered noise$-(1-t)s$$-1$

    These are conditional labels for one pair. If a second endpoint can explain the same $(t,x)$, the model is trained toward a posterior average of their labels. The explorer above checks conditional algebra; it does not demonstrate that a network knows the hidden endpoint.

    What extends beyond the Gaussian example?

    The lecture closes by separating the general construction from its convenient Gaussian instance. Flow matching needs a sampleable conditional path and an available velocity label. The source need not be standard noise: one can build bridges between other distributions if suitable conditional paths and an endpoint coupling are supplied. The Gaussian score–velocity conversion derived here relies on the particular $\alpha_tZ+\beta_t\varepsilon$ construction and does not automatically extend to every bridge.

    Short exercises with solutions

    Why can a perfect marginal model have nonzero conditional loss?

    Given $(t,x)$, hidden endpoints can supply different labels. Their conditional variance contributes $C\ge0$ even when the network equals the posterior mean. It contributes no parameter gradient.

    At $t=1/2$, $x=0.4$, a velocity model predicts $u=1.2$. Recover score and noise.

    $s=(tu-x)/(1-t)=(0.6-0.4)/0.5=0.4$. Then $\widehat\varepsilon=-(1-t)s=-0.2$. Converting back gives $(x-\widehat\varepsilon)/t=1.2$.

    For $\alpha_t=\sin(\pi t/2)$ and $\beta_t=\cos(\pi t/2)$, what is the flow label?

    Differentiate the schedules: $U=(\pi/2)[\cos(\pi t/2)Z-\sin(\pi t/2)\varepsilon]$. Reusing $Z-\varepsilon$ would train the wrong field.

    Does setting the SDE score correction to zero preserve the path?

    Only if the diffusion contribution also vanishes or a special cancellation already holds. In general, keeping $\sigma_t>0$ without the correction leaves an extra $(\sigma_t^2/2)\Delta p_t$ in Fokker–Planck.
