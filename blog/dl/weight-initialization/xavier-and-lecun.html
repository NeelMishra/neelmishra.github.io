    Xavier and LeCun Initialization: Count the Inputs and Outputs

    September 2026 · 4 min read + interactive lab

    Derive fan-in, fan-out, and Xavier variances from forward and backward sums, convert variance into uniform bounds, and count connections in dense and convolutional layers.

    Fan-based initialization sets a layer’s weight variance $s_W^2=\operatorname{Var}(W_{l,ij})$ by counting how many terms each matrix multiplication sums: LeCun preserves forward scale ($q_l=q_{l-1}$), fan-out preserves backward gradient scale ($\tilde q_{l-1}=\tilde q_l$), and Xavier (Glorot) balances the two.

    1. Forward sums ($n_{\rm in}$) vs. backward sums ($n_{\rm out}$)

    Consider layer $l$ with $z_l=W_lh_{l-1}$ and $h_l=\phi(z_l)$ in the linear or small-signal $\tanh$ regime ($\phi(z)\approx z$, $\phi'(z)\approx 1$), where $W_l\in\mathbb R^{n_{\rm out}\times n_{\rm in}}$ has independent zero-mean weights $\mathbb E[W_{l,ij}]=0$ of variance $s_W^2=\operatorname{Var}(W_{l,ij})=\mathbb E[W_{l,ij}^2]$, independent of incoming activations $a_j=h_{l-1,j}$ with second moment $q_{l-1}=\mathbb E[h_{l-1,j}^2]$:

    • Forward pass (LeCun / fan-in): Each output neuron $i\in\{1,\dots,n_{\rm out}\}$ takes the dot product of row $i$ of $W_l$ with $h_{l-1}$, summing over its fan-in of $n_{\rm in}$ incoming connections: $z_{l,i}=\sum_{j=1}^{n_{\rm in}}W_{l,ij}h_{l-1,j}$. Because the weights have zero mean and are independent, all cross terms ($j\ne k$) vanish in expectation, and each of the $n_{\rm in}$ squared terms contributes $\mathbb E[(W_{l,ij}h_{l-1,j})^2]=\mathbb E[W_{l,ij}^2]\,\mathbb E[h_{l-1,j}^2]=s_W^2\,q_{l-1}$:
      $$q_l=\mathbb E[h_{l,i}^2]\approx\mathbb E[z_{l,i}^2]=\sum_{j=1}^{n_{\rm in}}\mathbb E[W_{l,ij}^2]\,\mathbb E[h_{l-1,j}^2]=\sum_{j=1}^{n_{\rm in}}s_W^2\,q_{l-1}=(n_{\rm in}\,s_W^2)\,q_{l-1}.$$
      Requiring the forward second moment to stay constant ($q_l=q_{l-1}$) sets the forward multiplier $c=n_{\rm in}\,s_W^2=1$, giving LeCun (fan-in) initialization:
      $$n_{\rm in}\,s_W^2=1\implies s_{W,\text{fan-in}}^2=\frac{1}{n_{\rm in}},\qquad s_W=\operatorname{std}(W_{l,ij})=\frac{1}{\sqrt{n_{\rm in}}}.$$
    • Backward pass (fan-out): During backpropagation, the chain rule routes upstream gradients $g_l=\partial\ell/\partial h_l$ backward via the transpose $g_{l-1}=W_l^\top\delta_l\approx W_l^\top g_l$. Whereas the forward pass sums across the $n_{\rm in}$ columns of row $i$, the backward pass sums down the $n_{\rm out}$ rows of column $j$—the input unit’s fan-out (how many downstream outputs unit $j$ feeds into):
      $$g_{l-1,j}=\sum_{i=1}^{n_{\rm out}}W_{l,ij}\,g_{l,i}\implies\tilde q_{l-1}=\mathbb E[g_{l-1,j}^2]=\sum_{i=1}^{n_{\rm out}}\mathbb E[W_{l,ij}^2]\,\mathbb E[g_{l,i}^2]=(n_{\rm out}\,s_W^2)\,\tilde q_l.$$
      Requiring backward gradient second moments to keep their scale ($\tilde q_{l-1}=\tilde q_l$) sets the backward multiplier $c_{\rm back}=n_{\rm out}\,s_W^2=1$, giving fan-out initialization:
      $$n_{\rm out}\,s_W^2=1\implies s_{W,\text{fan-out}}^2=\frac{1}{n_{\rm out}},\qquad s_W=\operatorname{std}(W_{l,ij})=\frac{1}{\sqrt{n_{\rm out}}}.$$

    When $n_{\rm in}=n_{\rm out}$, both rules match. When $n_{\rm in}\ne n_{\rm out}$, fan-in scaling ($s_W^2=1/n_{\rm in}$) keeps the forward multiplier $c=1$ but scales backward gradient moments by $c_{\rm back}=n_{\rm out}/n_{\rm in}$, whereas fan-out scaling ($s_W^2=1/n_{\rm out}$) keeps $c_{\rm back}=1$ but scales forward moments by $c=n_{\rm in}/n_{\rm out}$.

    2. Xavier initialization balances both multipliers

    To compromise when $n_{\rm in}\ne n_{\rm out}$, Glorot and Bengio require the average of the forward multiplier $c=n_{\rm in}\,s_W^2$ and backward multiplier $c_{\rm back}=n_{\rm out}\,s_W^2$ to equal $1$:

    $$\frac{n_{\rm in}\,s_W^2+n_{\rm out}\,s_W^2}{2}=1\implies s_{W,\text{Xavier}}^2=\frac{2}{n_{\rm in}+n_{\rm out}},\qquad s_W=\operatorname{std}(W_{l,ij})=\sqrt{\frac{2}{n_{\rm in}+n_{\rm out}}}.$$

    Note that $s_{W,\text{Xavier}}^2$ is the reciprocal of the average fan count (the harmonic mean of $1/n_{\rm in}$ and $1/n_{\rm out}$), not their arithmetic mean.

    3. Converting variance to uniform bounds: how $a=\sqrt{3}\,s_W$ translates to weight init and $\sqrt{6}$

    Our signal-propagation equations in Sections 1 and 2 only depend on two numbers: $\mathbb E[W_{l,ij}]=0$ and $\operatorname{Var}(W_{l,ij})=s_W^2$. They do not require the weights to be Gaussian—we can draw $W_{l,ij}$ from either a Normal distribution or a Uniform distribution, provided both have mean $0$ and variance $s_W^2$:

    • Normal weight initialization ($W_{l,ij}\sim\mathcal N(0,s_W^2)$): A Gaussian is parameterized directly by its standard deviation $s_W=\sqrt{s_W^2}$, so we pass $s_W$ straight to the sampler (e.g., $s_{W,\text{Xavier}}=\sqrt{\frac{2}{n_{\rm in}+n_{\rm out}}}$).
    • Uniform weight initialization ($W_{l,ij}\sim\operatorname{Uniform}(-a,a)$): A uniform sampler is parameterized by its interval endpoints $[-a,+a]$, where every weight in $[-a,a]$ has constant density $p(w)=\frac{1}{a-(-a)}=\frac{1}{2a}$. What bound $a$ must we choose so that $\operatorname{Uniform}(-a,a)$ has the target variance $s_W^2$? Integrating $w^2p(w)$ over $[-a,a]$ gives:
      $$\operatorname{Var}(W_{l,ij})=\mathbb E[W_{l,ij}^2]=\int_{-a}^a w^2\,\frac{1}{2a}\,dw=\frac{1}{2a}\left[\frac{w^3}{3}\right]_{-a}^a=\frac{1}{2a}\left(\frac{a^3-(-a)^3}{3}\right)=\frac{1}{2a}\cdot\frac{2a^3}{3}=\frac{a^2}{3}.$$
      Setting this uniform variance $\frac{a^2}{3}$ equal to the target variance $s_W^2$ solves for the uniform endpoint $a$:
      $$\frac{a^2}{3}=s_W^2\implies a^2=3\,s_W^2\implies a=\sqrt{3}\,s_W\approx 1.732\,s_W.$$
      What $a=\sqrt{3}\,s_W$ means in practice: If you naively initialized weights from $\operatorname{Uniform}(-s_W,+s_W)$, their variance would only be $s_W^2/3$—three times too small. Because a uniform box on $[-a,a]$ has no tails beyond $\pm a$, its endpoints must stretch out to $\pm\sqrt{3}\,s_W$ to achieve variance $s_W^2$.
    • How $a=\sqrt{3}\,s_W$ produces $\sqrt{6}$ in Xavier (and He) Uniform: In Xavier initialization, the target standard deviation $s_{W,\text{Xavier}}=\sqrt{\frac{2}{n_{\rm in}+n_{\rm out}}}$ already has a $\sqrt{2}$ in the numerator (from averaging $n_{\rm in}$ and $n_{\rm out}$). Plugging $s_{W,\text{Xavier}}$ into $a=\sqrt{3}\,s_W$ multiplies $\sqrt{3}$ by $\sqrt{2}$ to give $\sqrt{3\times 2}=\sqrt{6}$:
      $$\begin{aligned}\text{LeCun Uniform }\left(s_W=\sqrt{\tfrac{1}{n_{\rm in}}}\right):&\quad a=\sqrt{3}\,s_W=\sqrt{3}\cdot\sqrt{\frac{1}{n_{\rm in}}}=\sqrt{\frac{3\times 1}{n_{\rm in}}}=\sqrt{\frac{3}{n_{\rm in}}}\implies W_{l,ij}\sim\operatorname{Uniform}\!\left(-\sqrt{\frac{3}{n_{\rm in}}},\;+\sqrt{\frac{3}{n_{\rm in}}}\right),\\[6pt]\text{Xavier Uniform }\left(s_W=\sqrt{\tfrac{2}{n_{\rm in}+n_{\rm out}}}\right):&\quad a=\sqrt{3}\,s_W=\underbrace{\sqrt{3}}_{\text{uniform factor}}\cdot\underbrace{\sqrt{\frac{2}{n_{\rm in}+n_{\rm out}}}}_{s_{W,\text{Xavier}}}=\sqrt{\frac{3\times 2}{n_{\rm in}+n_{\rm out}}}=\sqrt{\frac{6}{n_{\rm in}+n_{\rm out}}}\implies W_{l,ij}\sim\operatorname{Uniform}\!\left(-\sqrt{\frac{6}{n_{\rm in}+n_{\rm out}}},\;+\sqrt{\frac{6}{n_{\rm in}+n_{\rm out}}}\right).\end{aligned}$$

    5. Fan counts for dense and convolutional layers

    • Dense layer (weight shape $[n_{\rm out}, n_{\rm in}]$): $n_{\rm in}$ is the second dimension and $n_{\rm out}$ is the first.
    • Conv2d layer (weight shape $[C_{\rm out}, C_{\rm in}/G, k_h, k_w]$ with $G$ groups): Each output pixel sums over a $k_h\times k_w$ spatial patch across $C_{\rm in}/G$ input channels, so
      $$n_{\rm in}=\frac{C_{\rm in}}{G}k_hk_w,\qquad n_{\rm out}=\frac{C_{\rm out}}{G}k_hk_w.$$

    6. Questions worth answering

    Is Xavier variance the arithmetic average of $1/n_{\rm in}$ and $1/n_{\rm out}$?

    No. Xavier averages the fan counts in the denominator—$s_W^2=2/(n_{\rm in}+n_{\rm out})$—which is the harmonic mean of $1/n_{\rm in}$ and $1/n_{\rm out}$ and guarantees $\tfrac12(n_{\rm in}\,s_W^2+n_{\rm out}\,s_W^2)=1$.

    Where does the $\sqrt{6}$ in Xavier uniform come from?

    Matching $\operatorname{Var}(\operatorname{Uniform}(-a,a))=a^2/3$ to $s_{W,\text{Xavier}}^2=2/(n_{\rm in}+n_{\rm out})$ gives $a=\sqrt{3}\,s_{W,\text{Xavier}}=\sqrt{6/(n_{\rm in}+n_{\rm out})}$.

    When is Xavier’s linear approximation inaccurate?

    Xavier assumes zero-centered activations with unit slope near zero ($\phi(z)\approx z$, such as linear or small-signal $\tanh$). For ReLU, which zeros out negative inputs, it loses half the second moment $q_l$ per layer—handled by He initialization.
