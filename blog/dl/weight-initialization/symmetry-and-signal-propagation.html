    Initialization: Break Symmetry and Keep Signals Alive

    September 2026 · 4 min read + interactive lab

    See why identical hidden units stay identical, derive the per-layer signal multiplier, and track how weight scale compounds across depth.

    Weight initialization has two jobs before the first gradient step: ensure hidden units learn distinct features, and keep forward activations and backward gradients from vanishing or exploding as they pass through many layers.

    1. Why identical hidden units stay identical

    If hidden neurons in a layer start with identical parameters, they compute the same forward activation and receive the same backward gradient on every step. Compare three ways of initializing a layer’s weights $W$ and biases $b$:

    • Equal nonzero weights: Every hidden unit computes the exact same activation $\phi(w^\top x+b)$ and receives the exact same gradient during backpropagation. Every deterministic update changes all units identically, so the layer collapses to a single duplicated feature forever.
    • All-zero weights ($W=0$): Every hidden unit outputs the same constant activation $\phi(b)$, and incoming-weight gradients vanish on the first step wherever downstream weights are zero, keeping hidden units locked in symmetry.
    • Zero biases ($b=0$) with random weights: Sampling distinct random weight rows $w_i\ne w_j$ immediately gives each hidden unit a different preactivation $w_i^\top x$ and a different gradient on the very first pass, so initializing biases to zero is completely safe.

    2. How one layer changes signal scale

    Breaking symmetry only requires random weights, but picking an arbitrary scale like $W_{ij}\sim\mathcal N(0,0.01^2)$ fails in deep networks. Consider a single preactivation $z=\sum_{j=1}^{n_{\rm in}}W_ja_j$, where weights $W_j$ are independent with mean $\mathbb E[W_j]=0$ and variance $\operatorname{Var}(W_j)=\mathbb E[W_j^2]=s_W^2$, and are independent of the inputs $a_j$, which have second moment $q=\mathbb E[a_j^2]$.

    First, compute the expected value (mean) $\mathbb E[z]$. By linearity of expectation and the independence of $W_j$ and $a_j$, the mean of $z$ is always zero—even if the inputs $a_j$ have a nonzero mean:

    $$\mathbb E[z]=\mathbb E\!\left[\sum_{j=1}^{n_{\rm in}}W_ja_j\right]=\sum_{j=1}^{n_{\rm in}}\mathbb E[W_ja_j]=\sum_{j=1}^{n_{\rm in}}\underbrace{\mathbb E[W_j]}_{0}\mathbb E[a_j]=0.$$

    Because $\mathbb E[z]=0$, the output variance equals the output second moment: $\operatorname{Var}(z)=\mathbb E[z^2]-(\mathbb E[z])^2=\mathbb E[z^2]$. Now we can compute $\mathbb E[z^2]$ in three steps:

    • 1. Expand the square of the sum: Squaring a sum means multiplying the sum by a copy of itself. Using a second dummy index $k$ for the second factor so the two summation indices do not collide, every term $W_ja_j$ in the first sum multiplies every term $W_ka_k$ in the second sum, producing an $n_{\rm in}\times n_{\rm in}$ grid of $n_{\rm in}^2$ pairwise products:
      $$\begin{aligned}z^2&=\left(\sum_{j=1}^{n_{\rm in}}W_ja_j\right)^2=\left(\sum_{j=1}^{n_{\rm in}}W_ja_j\right)\left(\sum_{k=1}^{n_{\rm in}}W_ka_k\right)=\sum_{j=1}^{n_{\rm in}}\sum_{k=1}^{n_{\rm in}}W_jW_ka_ja_k\\[4pt]&=\underbrace{\sum_{j=1}^{n_{\rm in}}W_j^2a_j^2}_{n_{\rm in}\text{ diagonal terms }(j=k)}+\underbrace{\sum_{j\ne k}W_jW_ka_ja_k}_{n_{\rm in}(n_{\rm in}-1)\text{ cross terms }(j\ne k)}.\end{aligned}$$
      Just as $(x_1+x_2)^2=x_1^2+x_2^2+2x_1x_2$, we partition the $n_{\rm in}^2$ pairs into two groups before taking expectations: the $n_{\rm in}$ diagonal terms where both factors pick the same index ($j=k$, squaring a single weight into $W_j^2a_j^2$), and the $n_{\rm in}(n_{\rm in}-1)$ off-diagonal cross terms where they pick different indices ($j\ne k$, pairing two distinct weights $W_jW_ka_ja_k$). For $n_{\rm in}=2$, for instance, $(W_1a_1+W_2a_2)^2=(W_1^2a_1^2+W_2^2a_2^2)+(W_1W_2a_1a_2+W_2W_1a_2a_1)$.
    • 2. Take the expectation and drop the cross terms ($j\ne k$): Applying $\mathbb E[\cdot]$ to both sums, because $W_j$ and $W_k$ ($j\ne k$) are independent of each other and of the inputs with $\mathbb E[W_j]=0$, every off-diagonal term vanishes: $\mathbb E[W_jW_ka_ja_k]=\mathbb E[W_j]\mathbb E[W_k]\mathbb E[a_ja_k]=0\cdot 0\cdot\mathbb E[a_ja_k]=0$.
    • 3. Sum the $n_{\rm in}$ diagonal expectations: Unlike the cross terms, a squared weight $W_j^2\ge 0$ cannot cancel out—each diagonal term has expectation $\mathbb E[W_j^2a_j^2]=\mathbb E[W_j^2]\mathbb E[a_j^2]=s_W^2q$, so adding all $n_{\rm in}$ of them gives $\mathbb E[z^2]$ and $\operatorname{Var}(z)$:
      $$\begin{aligned}\mathbb E[z^2]&=\mathbb E\!\left[\sum_{j=1}^{n_{\rm in}}W_j^2a_j^2+\sum_{j\ne k}W_jW_ka_ja_k\right]=\sum_{j=1}^{n_{\rm in}}\mathbb E[W_j^2]\,\mathbb E[a_j^2]+\sum_{j\ne k}\underbrace{\mathbb E[W_j]}_{0}\underbrace{\mathbb E[W_k]}_{0}\mathbb E[a_ja_k]\\[4pt]&=\sum_{j=1}^{n_{\rm in}}s_W^2\,q=n_{\rm in}\,s_W^2\,q,\qquad\operatorname{Var}(z)=\mathbb E[z^2]-\bigl(\mathbb E[z]\bigr)^2=n_{\rm in}\,s_W^2\,q.\end{aligned}$$

    Why the recurrence tracks the second moment $\mathbb E[a_j^2]$ instead of variance $\operatorname{Var}(a_j)$: By definition, variance measures expected squared deviation around the mean $\mu_a=\mathbb E[a_j]$, whereas the second moment measures expected squared magnitude around zero. Expanding $\operatorname{Var}(a_j)=\mathbb E[(a_j-\mu_a)^2]$ connects the two:

    $$\begin{aligned}\operatorname{Var}(a_j)&=\mathbb E\bigl[(a_j-\mu_a)^2\bigr]=\mathbb E\bigl[a_j^2-2\mu_aa_j+\mu_a^2\bigr]=\mathbb E[a_j^2]-2\mu_a\mathbb E[a_j]+\mu_a^2=\mathbb E[a_j^2]-\bigl(\mathbb E[a_j]\bigr)^2\\[4pt]\implies\quad\underbrace{\mathbb E[a_j^2]}_{\text{second moment}}&=\underbrace{\operatorname{Var}(a_j)}_{\text{spread around }\mu_a}+\underbrace{\bigl(\mathbb E[a_j]\bigr)^2}_{\text{squared mean offset}}.\end{aligned}$$

    The two quantities are equal only when the inputs are zero-centered ($\mathbb E[a_j]=0$). Why does the layer recurrence multiply the full $\mathbb E[a_j^2]$ rather than $\operatorname{Var}(a_j)$? Because each weight $W_j$ is random with $\mathbb E[W_j]=0$, the output preactivation automatically has zero mean ($\mathbb E[z]=0$, so $\operatorname{Var}(z)=\mathbb E[z^2]$), and multiplying even a constant input offset $\mu_a\ne 0$ by a random weight $W_j\sim\mathcal N(0,s_W^2)$ turns that offset into random output spread $W_j\mu_a$ with variance $s_W^2\mu_a^2$:

    $$\operatorname{Var}(z)=\mathbb E[z^2]=n_{\rm in}\,s_W^2\,\mathbb E[a_j^2]=n_{\rm in}\,s_W^2\Bigl(\operatorname{Var}(a_j)+\bigl(\mathbb E[a_j]\bigr)^2\Bigr).$$

    For zero-mean activations (such as linear layers or symmetric $\tanh$), $\mathbb E[a_j]=0$ and $\mathbb E[a_j^2]=\operatorname{Var}(a_j)$. After a non-negative activation like ReLU ($a_j=\max(0,z_{l-1})\ge 0$), however, $\mathbb E[a_j]>0$, so the squared mean $(\mathbb E[a_j])^2$ contributes directly to the next layer’s preactivation variance—a distinction we will use in Chapter 3.

    3. Compounding across depth: forward and backward

    Now stack $L$ linear layers in a chain: $h_l=W_lh_{l-1}$ for $l=1,2,\dots,L$, starting from input $h_0=x$ with coordinate second moment $q_0=\mathbb E[h_{0,j}^2]$. Suppose every layer has width $n$ ($n_{\rm in}=n$) and each weight matrix $W_l\in\mathbb R^{n\times n}$ has independent zero-mean entries of variance $s_W^2$, independent of the incoming activations $h_{l-1}$.

    Let $q_l=\mathbb E[h_{l,i}^2]$ denote the second moment of a coordinate after layer $l$. Applying our single-layer result from Section 2 to layer $l$—where $h_{l,i}=\sum_{j=1}^n W_{l,ij}h_{l-1,j}$ sums $n$ inputs of second moment $q_{l-1}$—shows that each layer multiplies the signal’s second moment by the per-layer factor $c=ns_W^2$. Unrolling that recurrence from layer $1$ through layer $L$ multiplies by $c$ at every step:

    $$\begin{aligned}q_1&=\mathbb E[h_{1,i}^2]=(n\,s_W^2)\,q_0=c\,q_0,\\q_2&=\mathbb E[h_{2,i}^2]=c\,q_1=c(c\,q_0)=c^2q_0,\\&\;\;\vdots\\q_L&=c\,q_{L-1}=\underbrace{c\cdot c\cdots c}_{L\text{ layers}}\,q_0=c^Lq_0=(n\,s_W^2)^Lq_0.\end{aligned}$$

    Because depth $L$ sits in the exponent, the signal scale forms a geometric progression with ratio $c=ns_W^2$, splitting initialization into three regimes:

    • Vanishing forward signal ($c<1$, or $s_W<1/\sqrt{n}$): $c^L\to 0$ exponentially with depth $L$, shrinking activations toward zero before they reach the output.
    • Exploding forward signal ($c>1$, or $s_W>1/\sqrt{n}$): $c^L\to\infty$ exponentially with depth $L$, causing activations to blow up across deep stacks.
    • Scale-preserving boundary ($c=1$, or $s_W=1/\sqrt{n}$): $1^L=1$ for every $L$, keeping $q_L=q_0$ constant no matter how deep the network is.

    Adding nonlinearities between layers ($z_l=W_lh_{l-1},\; h_l=\phi(z_l)$): In practice, deep networks place a nonlinear activation $\phi$ between every linear layer (otherwise $W_L\cdots W_1x$ would collapse into a single linear matrix). Each layer now alternates two steps: the linear sum multiplies the incoming activation second moment $q_{l-1}=\mathbb E[h_{l-1,j}^2]$ by $n_{\rm in}s_W^2$ to produce $\mathbb E[z_{l,i}^2]=n_{\rm in}s_W^2\,q_{l-1}$, and the nonlinearity $\phi$ rescales that second moment by an activation factor $\kappa_\phi=\mathbb E[\phi(z_l)^2]/\mathbb E[z_l^2]$:

    $$q_l=\mathbb E[\phi(z_{l,i})^2]=\kappa_\phi\,\mathbb E[z_{l,i}^2]=\bigl(n_{\rm in}\,s_W^2\,\kappa_\phi\bigr)\,q_{l-1}\implies q_L=\bigl(n_{\rm in}\,s_W^2\,\kappa_\phi\bigr)^L q_0.$$
    • Small-signal $\tanh$ ($\phi(z)\approx z$ near $0$): When preactivations stay near the origin, $\kappa_\phi\approx 1$ and the linear rule $n_{\rm in}s_W^2=1$ holds (Chapter 2). If $s_W$ is too large, $\tanh(z_l)$ saturates near $\pm 1$: forward activations stop growing, but the slope $\phi'(z_l)=1-\tanh^2(z_l)\to 0$ kills the backward gradient.
    • ReLU ($\phi(z)=\max(0,z)$): Zeroing out the negative half of a symmetric preactivation distribution cuts the second moment in half ($\kappa_\phi=\tfrac12$), so keeping the per-layer multiplier $n_{\rm in}s_W^2\kappa_\phi=1$ requires doubling the weight variance to $s_W^2=2/n_{\rm in}$ (Chapter 3).

    The exact same exponential compounding happens in reverse during backpropagation. With $h_l=\phi(z_l)$ and $z_l=W_lh_{l-1}$, let $g_l=\partial\ell/\partial h_l$. By the chain rule, layer $l$ maps upstream gradients $g_l$ backward to $g_{l-1}$ via $W_l^\top$ and the local activation derivative $\phi'(z_l)$:

    $$g_{l-1}=W_l^\top\bigl(g_l\odot\phi'(z_l)\bigr),\qquad g_{l-1,i}=\sum_{k=1}^{n_{\rm out}}W_{l,ki}\,g_{l,k}\,\phi'(z_{l,k}).$$

    Because $W_l^\top$ sums over $n_{\rm out}$ output units and multiplies each term by $\phi'(z_{l,k})$, each backward step multiplies the gradient second moment by $c_{\rm back}=n_{\rm out}\,s_W^2\,\mathbb E[\phi'(z_l)^2]$, compounding as $c_{\rm back}^L$ from the output layer back to $g_0$. Forward propagation counts inputs $n_{\rm in}$ and activation energy $\kappa_\phi$; backward propagation counts outputs $n_{\rm out}$ and squared slope $\mathbb E[\phi'(z_l)^2]$.

    4. Lab: measure a sampled network before training

    The lab below propagates $48$ Gaussian input vectors forward and $48$ Gaussian output-gradient probes backward through an untrained width-$32$ network initialized with $W_{ij}\sim\mathcal N(0, g^2/32)$, so the linear per-layer multiplier is $c=32(g^2/32)=g^2$.

    Follow values forward and derivatives backward

    Each layer has width 32 and zero bias. Weight entries are Gaussian with standard deviation $g/\sqrt{32}$. The experiment propagates 48 independent Gaussian input vectors, then backpropagates independent Gaussian output directions through the same sampled network. No optimizer steps are taken.

    • Linear exponential scaling: Select Linear and compare $g=0.8$, $g=1.0$, and $g=1.25$. On the $\log_{10}$ vertical axis, $q_l = g^{2l}q_0$ traces a straight line with slope $2\log_{10}g$.
    • Tanh saturation: Select Tanh with $g=2.0$. Forward activations stay bounded inside $(-1,1)$, yet backward gradients still vanish because $\tanh'(z)=1-\tanh^2(z)\approx 0$ in the saturated tails.
    • ReLU halving: Select ReLU with $g=1.0$. Because ReLU zeros out negative values, both curves drop by roughly $\log_{10}(0.5)\approx -0.3$ per layer—motivating He’s $g=\sqrt{2}$ scaling in Chapter 3.

    5. Questions worth answering

    Why can biases start at zero while hidden weights must be random?

    Distinct random weight rows $w_i$ already give each hidden unit a different preactivation $w_i^\top x$ and a different gradient on step 1, so $b_i=0$ does not trap units in symmetry.

    If forward activations are bounded (as in $\tanh$), can backward gradients still vanish or explode?

    Yes. When $|\tanh(z)|\approx 1$, the activation is O(1) but its derivative $1-\tanh^2(z)\approx 0$, causing backward products $\prod_l W_l^\top \operatorname{diag}(\phi'(z_l))$ to vanish.

    Why does our derivation track the second moment $\mathbb E[a^2]$ instead of centered variance $\operatorname{Var}(a)$?

    Because $\mathbb E[(W_j a_j)^2]=\mathbb E[W_j^2]\mathbb E[a_j^2]=s_W^2\mathbb E[a_j^2]$. After a non-negative activation like ReLU, $\mathbb E[a_j]>0$, so $\mathbb E[a_j^2]=\operatorname{Var}(a_j)+(\mathbb E[a_j])^2$ is strictly larger than $\operatorname{Var}(a_j)$.
