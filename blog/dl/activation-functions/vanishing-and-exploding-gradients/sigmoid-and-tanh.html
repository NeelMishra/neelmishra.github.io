    1. Sigmoid and Tanh: Tail Saturation, the 1/4 Derivative Ceiling, and Mean Shift

    October 2026 · 7 min read

    Sigmoid and Tanh squash the entire real line into a bounded interval: $\sigma(z) \in (0, 1)$ and $\tanh(z) \in (-1, 1)$. While bounded outputs are useful for probabilities and retention gates, stacking them as hidden layers in a deep network is the classic textbook source of the vanishing gradient problem.

    This post derives the exact mathematical causes of both vanishing and exploding gradients in Sigmoid and Tanh, along with the numerical overflow failure mode covered in Stable Sigmoid.

    1. Causes of vanishing gradients in Sigmoid and Tanh

    Cause 1a: Sigmoid's global $1/4$ derivative ceiling

    Recall the definition and derivative of the logistic sigmoid:

    $$\sigma(z) = \frac{1}{1 + e^{-z}}, \qquad \sigma'(z) = \sigma(z)\bigl(1 - \sigma(z)\bigr).$$

    Complete the square in $p = \sigma(z) \in (0, 1)$ to find the largest slope Sigmoid can ever achieve:

    $$\sigma'(z) = p(1 - p) = \frac{1}{4} - \left(p - \frac{1}{2}\right)^{2} \le \frac{1}{4}.$$

    Even at the single best point $z = 0$ (where $p = 0.5$), the derivative is at most $\sigma'(0) = 0.25$. In an $L$-layer chain with unit-scale weights ($|w^{(\ell)}| \approx 1$), each layer multiplies the backward gradient by at most $1/4$:

    $$\left|\frac{\partial \mathcal{L}}{\partial h^{(0)}}\right| \le \left(\frac{1}{4}\right)^{L}\left|\frac{\partial \mathcal{L}}{\partial h^{(L)}}\right|.$$

    By $L = 10$ layers, $(1/4)^{10} \approx 9.54 \times 10^{-7}$: less than one-millionth of the gradient reaches the first layer even if every single neuron stays perfectly centered at $z = 0$.

    Cause 1b: Exponential tail saturation in both Sigmoid and Tanh

    Tanh rescales and shifts Sigmoid via $\tanh(z) = 2\sigma(2z) - 1$, giving the derivative

    $$\tanh'(z) = 1 - \tanh^{2}(z) \in (0, 1].$$

    At $z = 0$, $\tanh'(0) = 1.0$, which removes Sigmoid's $1/4$ penalty at the origin. However, because both functions flatten out toward horizontal asymptotes as $|z|$ grows, their derivatives decay exponentially in both tails:

    $$\sigma'(z) \approx e^{-|z|}, \qquad \tanh'(z) = \frac{4}{(e^{z} + e^{-z})^{2}} \approx 4e^{-2|z|} \quad (|z| \gg 1).$$

    At $|z| = 4$, $\sigma'(4) \approx 0.0177$ and $\tanh'(4) \approx 0.00134$. Once a pre-activation lands in $|z| > 4$, its local gradient is nearly zero, cutting off gradient flow to all earlier layers.

    Cause 1c: Positive mean shift in Sigmoid pushes the next layer into saturation

    Because $\sigma(z) > 0$ for every $z$, a layer of $d$ Sigmoid neurons has strictly positive outputs $h_j^{(\ell-1)} \in (0, 1)$ with mean $\mu_h \approx 0.5$. Consider the next layer's pre-activation $z_i^{(\ell)} = \sum_{j=1}^{d} w_{ij} h_j^{(\ell-1)}$. Even if the weights $w_{ij}$ have mean $\mu_w$ slightly different from zero, the expected pre-activation scales with layer width $d$:

    $$\mathbb{E}\bigl[z_i^{(\ell)}\bigr] = d \cdot \mu_w \cdot \mu_h.$$

    In a wide layer ($d = 512$), a tiny non-zero weight drift drives $z_i^{(\ell)}$ far away from $0$ into the saturated tail where $\sigma'(z_i^{(\ell)}) \approx 0$. Furthermore, because $h_j^{(\ell-1)} > 0$ for all $j$, the weight gradients for neuron $i$,

    $$\frac{\partial \mathcal{L}}{\partial w_{ij}} = \frac{\partial \mathcal{L}}{\partial z_i^{(\ell)}} \cdot h_j^{(\ell-1)},$$

    all share the exact same sign (the sign of $\partial \mathcal{L}/\partial z_i^{(\ell)}$), forcing gradient descent to zig-zag along positive-orthant diagonals instead of moving directly toward the optimum.

    2. Causes of exploding gradients in Sigmoid and Tanh

    If Sigmoid and Tanh have bounded derivatives ($\sigma'(z) \le 0.25$ and $\tanh'(z) \le 1$), how can they ever suffer from exploding gradients?

    1. Over-scaling weights to fight vanishing gradients: Suppose you initialize weights with large standard deviation $\sigma_w$ (for example, $w > 4$ in Sigmoid so that $|w\,\sigma'(0)| > 1$, or $w > 1$ in Tanh so that $|w\,\tanh'(0)| > 1$). Along any path of neurons whose pre-activations happen to stay near $z \approx 0$, each layer multiplies the backward gradient by $r = |w\,f'(0)| > 1$:
      $$\left|\frac{\partial \mathcal{L}}{\partial h^{(0)}}\right| \sim \bigl(|w\,f'(0)|\bigr)^{L} \to \infty.$$
      Meanwhile, most other neurons see large pre-activations $|z| = |w h| \gg 1$ and saturate to zero slope. The network fractures into dead saturated neurons and exploding active paths (or exploding recurrent state updates in RNNs).
    2. Floating-point overflow in naive exponential and log evaluations: As shown in Stable Sigmoid, computing $\sigma(z) = 1/(1 + e^{-z})$ for a large negative logit ($z = -100$ in FP32 or $z = -15$ in FP16) overflows $e^{-z} \to \infty$. Worse, computing binary cross-entropy via $-y\log(\sigma(z))$ when $\sigma(z)$ underflows to $0.0$ evaluates $\log(0) = -\infty$ and derivative $-y/\sigma(z) = -\infty$, instantly corrupting every weight gradient with NaN.

    3. Cause, effect, and numerical comparison

    Summary of vanishing and exploding gradient mechanisms in Sigmoid and Tanh.
    PhenomenonMathematical causeObserved effect during training
    Origin attenuation (Sigmoid)$\max_z \sigma'(z) = 0.25$ at $z = 0$.Early layers learn $4^{L}\times$ slower than the output layer even before saturation occurs; loss plateaus near random chance in deep plain MLPs.
    Tail saturation (Both)$\sigma'(z) \sim e^{-|z|}$ and $\tanh'(z) \sim 4e^{-2|z|}$ for $|z| \gg 1$.Neurons pushed past $|z| > 4$ freeze; large initial weights or large learning-rate steps lock units in their asymptotes.
    Positive mean shift (Sigmoid)$\sigma(z) \in (0, 1) \implies h_j > 0$.Shifts next-layer pre-activations into the positive saturated tail and forces all weights $\nabla_{w_i}\mathcal{L} = \delta_i h$ of a neuron to update with the same sign.
    Active-path explosionLarge weights ($|w|\sigma'(0) > 1$ or $|w|\tanh'(0) > 1$) near $z \approx 0$.Sharp cliff losses in RNNs and deep nets where gradients spike exponentially whenever trajectory passes through $z \approx 0$.

    Five-layer numerical check ($L = 5$). Trace a 5-layer scalar chain with unit weights ($w^{(\ell)} = 1$) when every layer sits at the origin ($z = 0$) versus in the tail ($z = 3$):

    • Sigmoid at $z = 0$: $\sigma'(0) = 0.25 \implies \prod_{\ell=1}^{5}\sigma'(0) = 0.25^{5} = \mathbf{9.77 \times 10^{-4}}$ ($1{,}024\times$ shrinkage).
    • Sigmoid at $z = 3$: $\sigma'(3) \approx 0.0452 \implies (0.0452)^{5} \approx \mathbf{1.88 \times 10^{-7}}$.
    • Tanh at $z = 0$: $\tanh'(0) = 1.00 \implies 1.00^{5} = \mathbf{1.00}$ (no shrinkage at the origin).
    • Tanh at $z = 3$: $\tanh'(3) \approx 0.00987 \implies (0.00987)^{5} \approx \mathbf{9.35 \times 10^{-11}}$.
    • Sigmoid with compensation weight $w = 6$ at $z = 0$: $|w\,\sigma'(0)| = 6 \times 0.25 = 1.50 \implies 1.50^{5} = \mathbf{7.59}$ (and by $L = 20$ layers, $1.50^{20} \approx \mathbf{3{,}325.3}$).
