    GMV, Revenue Per Visitor (RPV), Average Order Value (AOV) & Net Take Rate

    October 2026 · 10 min read

    In e-commerce search and recommendation systems, every candidate ranking score $\hat{s}(u, i)$ must balance relevance ($P(\text{buy}\mid u,i)$) against item price $p_i$ and platform margin. Learn how Gross Merchandise Value ($\text{GMV}$) and Revenue Per Visitor ($\text{RPV}$) decompose into conversion rate times Average Order Value ($\text{AOV}$), why $\text{AOV}$ alone is a dangerous trap in A/B testing, how to compute exact Delta Method variance for clustered orders, and why $p_{99.5}$ winsorization is mandatory when whale buyers are present.

    1. Gross Merchandise Value (GMV) and Revenue Per Visitor (RPV) Decomposition

    Suppose $N$ users $u \in \{1, \dots, N\}$ are randomized into an experiment arm. During the evaluation window, user $u$ places $O_u \in \{0, 1, 2, \dots\}$ completed orders, where order $k \in \{1, \dots, O_u\}$ has gross dollar checkout value $v_{u,k} > 0$ (prior to merchant payouts). The user-level Gross Merchandise Value is $G_u = \sum_{k=1}^{O_u} v_{u,k}$ (with $G_u = 0$ if $O_u = 0$).

    Because total $\text{GMV} = \sum_{u=1}^N G_u$ scales with traffic allocation, we normalize by the number of randomized visitors $N$ (an immutable pre-treatment randomization denominator) to obtain Revenue Per Visitor ($\text{RPV}$), which factors cleanly into three multiplicative levers:

    $$\operatorname{RPV} = \frac{1}{N}\sum_{u=1}^N G_u = \underbrace{\left(\frac{1}{N}\sum_{u=1}^N \mathbf{1}[O_u > 0]\right)}_{\text{Buyer Conversion Rate }(\operatorname{CVR}_{\text{user}})} \times \underbrace{\left(\frac{\sum_{u=1}^N O_u}{\sum_{u=1}^N \mathbf{1}[O_u > 0]}\right)}_{\text{Orders per Buyer}} \times \underbrace{\left(\frac{\sum_{u=1}^N G_u}{\sum_{u=1}^N O_u}\right)}_{\text{Average Order Value }(\operatorname{AOV})}.$$

    Equivalently, defining Orders Per Visitor ($\text{OPV}$) as $\bar{O} = \frac{1}{N}\sum_{u=1}^N O_u$, we have the fundamental identity:

    $$\operatorname{RPV} = \bar{G} = \operatorname{OPV} \times \operatorname{AOV}.$$
    • Why $\operatorname{RPV}$ has a clean randomization denominator: Because $N$ is the count of randomized users, $\operatorname{RPV} = \bar{G}$ is a simple sample mean of i.i.d. user-level variables $G_u$, so standard $t$-tests and CUPED apply directly to $G_u$.
    • Why $G_u$ is zero-inflated and heavy-tailed: In a typical e-commerce experiment, $92\%\text{–}97\%$ of visitors have $G_u = 0$, while $G_u \mid (G_u > 0)$ follows a right-skewed log-normal or Pareto distribution. By the Law of Total Variance, denoting buyer conversion $\pi = \Pr(G_u > 0)$, positive spend mean $\mu_+ = \mathbb{E}[G_u \mid G_u > 0]$, and positive spend variance $\sigma_+^2 = \operatorname{Var}(G_u \mid G_u > 0)$:
      $$\operatorname{Var}(G_u) = \pi\,\sigma_+^2 + \pi(1 - \pi)\,\mu_+^2.$$
      Notice that when $\sigma_+ \gg \mu_+$ (due to bulk or enterprise "whale" buyers spending $\$20{,}000$ when the median order is $\$45$), the first term $\pi\sigma_+^2$ dominates sample variance and destroys statistical power unless capped.

    2. The Average Order Value (AOV) Selection-Bias Trap & Delta Method Variance

    Average Order Value ($\text{AOV}$) measures the mean dollar size per completed order:

    $$\operatorname{AOV} = \frac{\sum_{u=1}^N G_u}{\sum_{u=1}^N O_u} = \frac{\bar{G}}{\bar{O}}.$$

    Why $\text{AOV}$ alone can be gamed: Notice that the denominator $\sum_{u=1}^N O_u$ is a post-treatment endogenous random variable (only users who decide to buy enter the denominator). Suppose a treatment model floods the top of search results with $\$500$ luxury items when a user searches for "headphones," burying $\$25$ budget headphones. Budget shoppers abandon the site without purchasing, while wealthy shoppers still buy the $\$500$ headphones.

    • What happens to $\text{AOV}$? Because low-dollar orders vanished from the denominator, $\text{AOV}$ skyrockets by $+18\%$!
    • What happens to $\text{OPV}$ and $\text{RPV}$? Order volume $\text{OPV}$ drops by $-25\%$, so net $\text{RPV} = \text{OPV} \times \text{AOV}$ drops by $(0.75)(1.18) - 1 = -11.5\%$!
    • Design Rule: Never use $\text{AOV}$ as a standalone North Star metric. Always evaluate $\text{RPV}$ (or Winsorized $\text{RPV}$) as the primary OEC, and use the $(\text{OPV}, \text{AOV})$ pair strictly as diagnostic decomposes.

    Furthermore, because a single repeat customer can place multiple orders ($O_u \ge 2$) with correlated basket sizes, treating orders as independent observations underestimates $\operatorname{Var}(\widehat{\operatorname{AOV}})$. Applying the Delta Method to the ratio of user-level sums $\widehat{\operatorname{AOV}} = \bar{G}/\bar{O}$ yields the exact linearized per-user score $Z_u$:

    $$\operatorname{Var}(\widehat{\operatorname{AOV}}) \approx \frac{1}{N\,\bar{O}^2}\left(\operatorname{Var}(G_u) - 2\widehat{\operatorname{AOV}}\operatorname{Cov}(G_u, O_u) + \widehat{\operatorname{AOV}}^2\operatorname{Var}(O_u)\right) = \frac{\operatorname{Var}(Z_u)}{N},$$

    where the linearized user residual is $Z_u = \frac{1}{\bar{O}}\bigl(G_u - \widehat{\operatorname{AOV}}\cdot O_u\bigr)$.

    3. Whale-Buyer Winsorization ($p_{99.5}$ Capping) vs. Log-Transformations

    To prevent a handful of B2B reseller accounts from swinging an A/B test by millions of dollars, production experimentation platforms apply Winsorization (capping) to user-level spend $G_u$ at a fixed threshold $\tau$ (typically the $99.5\text{th}$ or $99.9\text{th}$ percentile of positive user spend computed on pre-experiment data or pooled across arms):

    $$G_u^{\text{win}}(\tau) = \min(G_u, \tau).$$
    • Bias–Variance Tradeoff: Capping $G_u$ at $\tau = p_{99.5}$ typically reduces $\operatorname{Var}(G_u)$ by $40\%\text{–}75\%$ (cutting required sample size or runtime by more than half) while introducing negligible bias because legitimate UI/ranking treatments rarely aim to alter the behavior of the top $0.5\%$ bulk institutional buyers.
    • Why Winsorization is preferred over $\log(1 + G_u)$ for business decisions: While $\mathbb{E}[\log(1 + G_u)]$ heavily compresses variance, its expectation shifts with the geometric mean rather than total dollar revenue—meaning a treatment that increases $\mathbb{E}[\log(1+G_u)]$ can actually lose total dollars if it trades one $\$200$ order for two $\$15$ orders ($\ln(16)+\ln(16) = 5.54 > \ln(201) = 5.30$). Winsorized $\text{RPV}$ preserves additive dollar interpretability.

    4. Net Take Rate, First-Party (1P) vs. Third-Party (3P) Blending, and Contribution Margin

    A $\$100$ sale of a low-margin electronics item (where the platform keeps a $4\%$ commission) is worth far less to the business than a $\$40$ sale of a high-margin apparel item or sponsored listing ($20\%$ take rate). For each item $i$ with price $p_i$, let $r_i \in (0, 1]$ denote the platform Take Rate (commission rate on 3P marketplace goods, or gross margin $(p_i - \text{COGS}_i)/p_i$ on 1P retail inventory) and let $c_{\text{fulfill}, i}$ denote variable payment processing and fulfillment subsidies. User $u$'s Net Contribution Margin is:

    $$M_u = \sum_{k=1}^{O_u}\sum_{i \in \text{basket}_{u,k}} \bigl(r_i\,p_i - c_{\text{fulfill}, i}\bigr),\qquad \text{Effective Take Rate} = \frac{\sum_{u=1}^N M_u}{\sum_{u=1}^N G_u}.$$

    In multi-objective ranking, transitioning the scoring function from gross expected GMV $\hat{P}(\text{buy}\mid u,i)\cdot p_i$ to a convex combination of GMV and Margin:

    $$\operatorname{Score}(u, i) = \hat{P}(\text{buy} \mid u, i) \cdot \bigl((1 - \alpha)\,p_i + \alpha\,(r_i p_i - c_{\text{fulfill}, i})\bigr), \qquad \alpha \in [0, 1]$$

    allows the platform to trace the Pareto frontier between market-share growth ($\text{GMV}$) and unit profitability ($\text{Net Margin}$).

    Worked numerical example: AOV Increase Masking an RPV Collapse

    Suppose $N_C = N_T = 100{,}000$ users are randomized into Control ($C$) and Treatment ($T$), where Treatment boosts higher-priced items in ranking:

    • Control ($C$): $5{,}000$ orders placed ($\text{OPV}_C = 0.050$), total $\text{GMV}_C = \$250{,}000$. Thus $\text{AOV}_C = \frac{\$250{,}000}{5{,}000} = \mathbf{\$50.00}$, and $\text{RPV}_C = \frac{\$250{,}000}{100{,}000} = \mathbf{\$2.50}$.
    • Treatment ($T$): $4{,}000$ orders placed ($\text{OPV}_T = 0.040$, a $-20\%$ drop in orders!), total $\text{GMV}_T = \$232{,}000$. Thus $\text{AOV}_T = \frac{\$232{,}000}{4{,}000} = \mathbf{\$58.00}$ ($+16.0\%$ lift in $\text{AOV}$!), but $\text{RPV}_T = \frac{\$232{,}000}{100{,}000} = \mathbf{\$2.32}$ ($\mathbf{-7.2\%}$ loss in revenue per visitor!).

    5. Python Implementation: RPV, Delta-Method AOV, Winsorization & CUPED

    import numpy as np
from scipy import stats

def evaluate_ecommerce_ab(
    gmv_c: np.ndarray, orders_c: np.ndarray, pre_gmv_c: np.ndarray,
    gmv_t: np.ndarray, orders_t: np.ndarray, pre_gmv_t: np.ndarray,
    winsor_quantile: float = 0.995
):
    """
    Computes raw RPV, Winsorized + CUPED-adjusted RPV, and Delta-Method AOV
    for user-clustered e-commerce A/B experiment logs.
    """
    # 1. Winsorize user GMV at pooled pre-experiment positive spend quantile
    pooled_pos_pre = np.concatenate([pre_gmv_c[pre_gmv_c > 0], pre_gmv_t[pre_gmv_t > 0]])
    cap = np.quantile(pooled_pos_pre, winsor_quantile)
    w_gmv_c = np.minimum(gmv_c, cap)
    w_gmv_t = np.minimum(gmv_t, cap)
    w_pre_c = np.minimum(pre_gmv_c, cap)
    w_pre_t = np.minimum(pre_gmv_t, cap)

    # 2. CUPED variance reduction on Winsorized RPV
    pooled_w_pre = np.concatenate([w_pre_c, w_pre_t])
    pooled_w_gmv = np.concatenate([w_gmv_c, w_gmv_t])
    theta = np.cov(pooled_w_gmv, pooled_w_pre, ddof=1)[0, 1] / np.var(pooled_w_pre, ddof=1)
    mean_pre = np.mean(pooled_w_pre)

    cuped_c = w_gmv_c - theta * (w_pre_c - mean_pre)
    cuped_t = w_gmv_t - theta * (w_pre_t - mean_pre)

    rpv_diff = np.mean(cuped_t) - np.mean(cuped_c)
    rpv_se = np.sqrt(np.var(cuped_t, ddof=1) / len(cuped_t) + np.var(cuped_c, ddof=1) / len(cuped_c))
    rpv_pval = 2 * (1 - stats.norm.cdf(abs(rpv_diff / rpv_se)))

    # 3. Delta Method for AOV = sum(G_u) / sum(O_u)
    aov_c = np.sum(gmv_c) / np.sum(orders_c)
    aov_t = np.sum(gmv_t) / np.sum(orders_t)
    z_c = (gmv_c - aov_c * orders_c) / np.mean(orders_c)
    z_t = (gmv_t - aov_t * orders_t) / np.mean(orders_t)
    aov_se = np.sqrt(np.var(z_t, ddof=1) / len(z_t) + np.var(z_c, ddof=1) / len(z_c))
    aov_pval = 2 * (1 - stats.norm.cdf(abs((aov_t - aov_c) / aov_se)))

    return {
        "Winsor_Cap_$": round(float(cap), 2),
        "Raw_RPV_C": round(float(np.mean(gmv_c)), 4),
        "Raw_RPV_T": round(float(np.mean(gmv_t)), 4),
        "CUPED_Winsor_RPV_Lift_%": round(float(100 * rpv_diff / np.mean(w_gmv_c)), 2),
        "CUPED_Winsor_RPV_p": round(float(rpv_pval), 4),
        "AOV_C": round(float(aov_c), 2),
        "AOV_T": round(float(aov_t), 2),
        "DeltaMethod_AOV_p": round(float(aov_pval), 4),
    }

if __name__ == "__main__":
    rng = np.random.default_rng(42)
    N = 50_000
    pre_c = rng.binomial(1, 0.06, N) * rng.lognormal(3.8, 0.9, N)
    pre_t = rng.binomial(1, 0.06, N) * rng.lognormal(3.8, 0.9, N)
    ord_c = rng.poisson(0.06 + 0.0005 * (pre_c > 0), N)
    ord_t = rng.poisson(0.064 + 0.0005 * (pre_t > 0), N)  # +6.7% order lift
    gmv_c = ord_c * rng.lognormal(3.8, 0.8, N) + 0.35 * pre_c
    gmv_t = ord_t * rng.lognormal(3.8, 0.8, N) + 0.35 * pre_t
    print(evaluate_ecommerce_ab(gmv_c, ord_c, pre_c, gmv_t, ord_t, pre_t))

    6. Self-Check Questions

    Why should the Winsorization threshold $\tau$ be computed on pre-experiment spend (or pooled spend across both arms) rather than separately at the $99.5\text{th}$ percentile of Control and the $99.5\text{th}$ percentile of Treatment?

    If a treatment genuinely shifts the spend distribution upward (or increases the fraction of buyers), computing separate percentile caps $\tau_C \ne \tau_T$ clips the two arms at different dollar ceilings and introduces systematic asymmetric truncation bias. Using a single fixed dollar threshold $\tau$ learned from pre-experiment data applies the exact same bounded transformation $g(x) = \min(x, \tau)$ to both arms.

    When a ranking team changes its objective from $\hat{P}(\text{buy}\mid u,i)$ (Conversion optimization) to $\hat{P}(\text{buy}\mid u,i)\cdot p_i$ (GMV optimization), what price-bias pathology typically appears in online A/B tests?

    Multiplying linearly by price $p_i$ causes a $\$500$ item with a tiny $0.2\%$ purchase probability ($\text{expected GMV} = \$1.00$) to outrank a $\$20$ everyday essential with a $4.5\%$ purchase probability ($\text{expected GMV} = \$0.90$), even if the $0.2\%$ tail probability is poorly calibrated noise. Production rankers therefore use concave price weighting $p_i^\gamma$ with $\gamma \in [0.3, 0.7]$ or enforce a conversion-rate guardrail so order volume $\text{OPV}$ cannot degrade.
