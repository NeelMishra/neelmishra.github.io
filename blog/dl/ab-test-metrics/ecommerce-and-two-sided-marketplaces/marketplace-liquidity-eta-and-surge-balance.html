    Two-Sided Marketplace Liquidity, ETA Pinball Loss & Surge-Pricing Guardrails

    October 2026 · 11 min read

    In spatially constrained two-sided marketplaces (Uber, Lyft, DoorDash, Instacart, Airbnb), supply is rivalrous: assigning a driver or courier to Rider $A$ makes that driver unavailable for Rider $B$. Master the four pillars of real-time marketplace experimentation: Search-to-Fill Liquidity, the Supply Utilization vs. Wait-Time frontier, asymmetric ETA evaluation with Pinball Loss $\rho_\tau$, and Surge-Pricing conversion elasticity under Switchback testing.

    1. Marketplace Liquidity: Search-to-Fill Rate & Match Funnel

    Marketplace Liquidity measures the probability that a buyer's intent is successfully matched and fulfilled within an acceptable time window $T_{\max}$. In ride-sharing and delivery, a demand session progresses through three stages: (1) Eyeball / Price-Quote Intent ($E$) $\to$ (2) Trip / Order Request ($R$) $\to$ (3) Driver Dispatch Match ($M$) $\to$ (4) Completed Fulfillment ($F$, no driver/rider cancellation):

    $$\operatorname{Liquidity\ (Search-to-Fill\ Rate)} = \frac{N_{\text{completed fulfillments } F}}{N_{\text{unique demand intents } E}} = \underbrace{\Pr(R \mid E)}_{\text{Quote-to-Request}} \times \underbrace{\Pr(M \mid R)}_{\text{Dispatch Match Rate}} \times \underbrace{\bigl(1 - \Pr(\text{Cancel} \mid M)\bigr)}_{\text{Post-Match Completion}}.$$
    • Supply-Side Liquidity (Listing / Driver Utilization): On the supply side, liquidity is measured by the fraction of active listings that receive a booking within $D$ days (Airbnb/Etsy) or Time Utilization Rate ($\text{TUR}$) for drivers/couriers—the fraction of online supply hours spent actively fulfilling a trip:
      $$\operatorname{TUR} = \frac{T_{\text{en-route to pickup}} + T_{\text{on-trip}}}{T_{\text{idle waiting}} + T_{\text{en-route to pickup}} + T_{\text{on-trip}}}.$$

    2. The Supply Utilization vs. Demand Wait-Time Frontier (Kingman's Queueing Law)

    Why can't a dispatch algorithm drive driver utilization $\rho = \operatorname{TUR} \to 100\%$? In spatial queueing theory (and Kingman's $G/G/m$ approximation), expected buyer pickup time / ETA scales nonlinearly as utilization $\rho$ approaches $1$ because fewer idle drivers remain nearby—forcing the dispatcher to match riders with distant drivers (“wild goose chases”):

    $$\mathbb{E}[W_{\text{pickup}}] \propto \frac{\rho}{1 - \rho} + d_{\text{spatial}}(\text{idle density}).$$

    When $\rho$ exceeds a critical threshold $\rho^* \approx 0.75\text{–}0.82$ in a hex cell (e.g., H3 resolution 7), average pickup distance jumps, $T_{\text{en-route}}$ swells, and effective completed trips per hour actually decrease! Therefore, dispatch and batch-matching A/B tests track Net Completed Trips Per Supply Hour alongside Batched Wait-Time Savings and Driver En-Route Deadhead Fraction:

    $$\operatorname{Deadhead\ Ratio} = \frac{T_{\text{en-route to pickup}}}{T_{\text{en-route to pickup}} + T_{\text{on-trip}}}.$$

    3. Asymmetric ETA Accuracy: MAE, Pinball Loss ($\rho_\tau$) & Extreme Late Rate

    Estimated Time of Arrival ($\widehat{\text{ETA}}$) is both an offline ML prediction and a customer-facing product promise shown on the checkout screen. Let $y_i$ be the actual delivery/arrival duration (in minutes) and $\hat{y}_i$ be the predicted ETA shown to the user, so the residual error is $e_i = y_i - \hat{y}_i$ ($e_i > 0$ means the order arrived late; $e_i < 0$ means the order arrived early).

    Symmetric metrics like $\operatorname{MAE} = \frac{1}{M}\sum_{i=1}^M |y_i - \hat{y}_i|$ or $\operatorname{RMSE}$ treat arriving $12$ minutes early identically to arriving $12$ minutes late. In reality, user trust and cancellation/refund rates are sharply asymmetric: a food delivery arriving $15$ minutes late causes cold food, support tickets, and churn, whereas arriving $5$ minutes early is mildly positive—unless you pad $\hat{y}_i$ so aggressively at checkout that users abandon the order before buying!

    • Quantile / Pinball Loss ($\rho_\tau$): To balance late-arrival dissatisfaction against pre-checkout ETA inflation, production ETA systems evaluate (and train) quantile heads at $\tau \in [0.65, 0.80]$ using the asymmetric Pinball Loss:
      $$\rho_\tau(y_i, \hat{y}_i) = \begin{cases} \tau\,(y_i - \hat{y}_i) & \text{if } y_i \ge \hat{y}_i \text{ (Late arrival: penalized by }\tau\text{)}, \\[4pt] (1 - \tau)\,(\hat{y}_i - y_i) & \text{if } y_i < \hat{y}_i \text{ (Early arrival / padded quote: penalized by }1-\tau\text{)}. \end{cases}$$
      For $\tau = 0.75$, every minute of lateness costs $3\times$ as much as a minute of earliness ($\frac{\tau}{1-\tau} = \frac{0.75}{0.25} = 3$), and the Bayes-optimal predictor $\hat{y}^*(x) = F_{Y|X}^{-1}(\tau)$ guarantees that exactly $75\%$ of deliveries arrive on or before the promised time.
    • Online Guardrails for ETA Models:
      1. On-Time Delivery Rate ($\text{OTDR}$): $\frac{1}{M}\sum_{i=1}^M \mathbf{1}[y_i - \hat{y}_i \le \delta_{\text{grace}}]$ (typically $\delta_{\text{grace}} = 5\text{ min}$).
      2. Extreme Late Delivery Rate ($\text{ELDR}_{15}$ / Defect Rate): $\frac{1}{M}\sum_{i=1}^M \mathbf{1}[y_i - \hat{y}_i > 15\text{ min}]$—the single strongest predictor of post-delivery user churn and support refunds.
      3. Quoted ETA Conversion Impact: Every $+1\text{ minute}$ increase in mean displayed checkout $\widehat{\text{ETA}}$ reduces checkout conversion $\Pr(R \mid E)$ by $\approx 0.5\%\text{–}1.5\%$. Thus an ETA model cannot "cheat" $\text{ELDR}_{15}$ downward simply by adding $+10\text{ min}$ of bias without tripping the conversion guardrail.

    4. Surge / Dynamic Pricing Elasticity & Switchback Experimentation

    When local demand exceeds supply ($D_t > S_t$ in hex cell $h$), dynamic surge multipliers $m_{h,t} \ge 1.0$ restore marketplace liquidity by rationing low-urgency demand and attracting nearby supply. We measure the Price Elasticity of Quote-to-Request Conversion:

    $$\varepsilon_p = \frac{\partial \ln \Pr(R \mid E, m)}{\partial \ln m} = \frac{\Delta \Pr(R \mid E) / \Pr(R \mid E)}{\Delta m / m} < 0.$$

    Why User-Level Randomization Fails for Dispatch, ETA & Surge Models: If Treatment users in a user-level A/B test receive lower prices or faster priority dispatch, they consume the shared pool of idle drivers in hex cell $h$, leaving Control users in the same hex cell with longer ETAs and fewer drivers. This violates SUTVA (Stable Unit Treatment Value Assumption) and biases the estimated treatment lift by up to $2\times\text{–}5\times$.

    Production marketplaces therefore evaluate supply-allocating models via Spatiotemporal Switchback Experiments: partitioning cities into spatial region clusters $r \in \{1, \dots, R\}$ and time blocks $b \in \{1, \dots, B\}$ (e.g., 30-minute or 1-hour windows), randomizing $(r, b)$ units to Treatment or Control, and computing cluster-robust (Liang–Zeger) standard errors across region-time blocks.

    Worked numerical example: Symmetric MAE vs. Pinball Loss ($\tau = 0.75$) on ETA Models

    Suppose actual delivery times on $4$ orders are $y = [30, 30, 30, 50]\text{ min}$:

    • Model A (Predicting conditional median $30\text{ min}$): $\hat{y}_A = [30, 30, 30, 30]$. Residual errors are $e_A = [0, 0, 0, +20\text{ min late}]$.
      • $\operatorname{MAE}_A = \frac{0+0+0+20}{4} = \mathbf{5.00\text{ min}}$, $\text{ELDR}_{15, A} = \mathbf{25\%}$ ($1/4$ orders arrived $20\text{ min}$ late!).
      • $\text{Pinball}_{0.75}(A) = \frac{1}{4}\bigl(0 + 0 + 0 + 0.75(20)\bigr) = \mathbf{3.75}$.
    • Model B (Predicting $0.75$-quantile $35\text{ min}$): $\hat{y}_B = [35, 35, 35, 35]$. Residual errors are $e_B = [-5, -5, -5, +15\text{ min}]$.
      • $\operatorname{MAE}_B = \frac{5+5+5+15}{4} = \mathbf{7.50\text{ min}}$ (worse on symmetric MAE!), but $\text{ELDR}_{15, B} = \mathbf{0\%}$ (no order is $>15\text{ min}$ late).
      • $\text{Pinball}_{0.75}(B) = \frac{1}{4}\bigl(3 \times 0.25(5) + 0.75(15)\bigr) = \frac{3.75 + 11.25}{4} = \mathbf{3.75}$ (and for any $\hat{y} \in (30, 35)$, say $\hat{y}=34$, $\text{ELDR}_{15}$ drops while conversion impact stays minimal).

    5. Python Implementation: ETA Pinball Loss, Liquidity & Switchback Cluster Analysis

    import numpy as np
from scipy import stats

def evaluate_eta_and_switchback(
    y_true: np.ndarray, y_pred: np.ndarray, tau: float = 0.75,
    block_region_ids: np.ndarray = None, block_treat: np.ndarray = None,
    block_fulfilled: np.ndarray = None, block_intents: np.ndarray = None
):
    """
    Computes asymmetric ETA Pinball Loss, On-Time Delivery Rate (<=5m late),
    Extreme Late Delivery Rate (>15m late), and cluster-robust Switchback Liquidity lift.
    """
    residual = y_true - y_pred  # >0 means late arrival
    mae = float(np.mean(np.abs(residual)))
    pinball = float(np.mean(np.where(residual >= 0, tau * residual, (tau - 1.0) * residual)))
    otdr_5m = float(np.mean(residual <= 5.0))
    eldr_15m = float(np.mean(residual > 15.0))
    mean_bias = float(np.mean(residual))

    out = {
        "MAE_min": round(mae, 3),
        f"Pinball_{tau}": round(pinball, 3),
        "Mean_Residual_Late_min": round(mean_bias, 3),
        "OnTime_<=5m_%": round(100 * otdr_5m, 2),
        "Extreme_Late_>15m_%": round(100 * eldr_15m, 2),
    }

    if block_region_ids is not None:
        # Delta method + cluster aggregation across spatial regions for Switchback test
        c_mask = (block_treat == 0)
        t_mask = (block_treat == 1)
        liq_c = np.sum(block_fulfilled[c_mask]) / np.sum(block_intents[c_mask])
        liq_t = np.sum(block_fulfilled[t_mask]) / np.sum(block_intents[t_mask])

        mean_intent_c = np.mean(block_intents[c_mask])
        mean_intent_t = np.mean(block_intents[t_mask])
        z_c = (block_fulfilled[c_mask] - liq_c * block_intents[c_mask]) / mean_intent_c
        z_t = (block_fulfilled[t_mask] - liq_t * block_intents[t_mask]) / mean_intent_t

        se = np.sqrt(np.var(z_t, ddof=1) / len(z_t) + np.var(z_c, ddof=1) / len(z_c))
        pval = 2 * (1 - stats.norm.cdf(abs((liq_t - liq_c) / se)))
        out["Liquidity_C_%"] = round(float(100 * liq_c), 2)
        out["Liquidity_T_%"] = round(float(100 * liq_t), 2)
        out["Switchback_p"] = round(float(pval), 4)
    return out

if __name__ == "__main__":
    rng = np.random.default_rng(7)
    actual = rng.gamma(shape=9.0, scale=3.5, size=10_000)  # mean 31.5 min, right-skewed
    pred_median = np.full(10_000, np.median(actual))
    pred_q75 = np.full(10_000, np.quantile(actual, 0.75))
    print("Median ETA Quote :", evaluate_eta_and_switchback(actual, pred_median))
    print("Q75    ETA Quote :", evaluate_eta_and_switchback(actual, pred_q75))

    6. Self-Check Questions

    Why does a Switchback experiment require a "burn-in" (washout) period at the boundary when switching a city from Treatment to Control at 2:00 PM?

    Because marketplace state (the spatial distribution and availability of idle drivers) has temporal carryover! If the Treatment algorithm from 1:00–2:00 PM depleted idle drivers in downtown, the Control algorithm taking over at 2:00 PM inherits an artificially starved supply state for the first 10–15 minutes (the average trip duration). Discarding the first $\Delta t_{\text{burn-in}} \approx 15\text{ min}$ of each switchback window or lengthening windows to 1–2 hours mitigates temporal carryover bias.

    If a new ETA model lowers Extreme Late Delivery Rate ($\text{ELDR}_{15}$) by $-2.5\%$, what two metrics must you immediately check before celebrating?

    First, check Mean Quoted ETA ($\mathbb{E}[\hat{y}]$) to verify the model improved discriminative accuracy rather than simply adding a $+4\text{ minute}$ constant buffer to all quotes. Second, check Quote-to-Order Conversion Rate $\Pr(R \mid E)$ (and Gross Bookings per session) to confirm that any shift in displayed ETA did not scare away price/time-sensitive buyers at checkout.
