    Sample Ratio Mismatch (SRM), Novelty/Primacy & Network Interference

    October 2026 · ML System Design · Experiment Validity & SUTVA Violations

    What happens when a $50/50$ A/B test on $1{,}000{,}000$ users logs $502{,}150$ users in Control and $497{,}850$ users in Treatment? Even though $50.215\%$ vs. $49.785\%$ looks tiny to the human eye, its $\chi^2$ $p$-value is $1.7 \times 10^{-5}$—proving that 4,300 users are systematically missing from Treatment, which invalidates every metric in the test! In this note we cover the three pillars of experiment validity: Sample Ratio Mismatch ($\text{SRM}$), Novelty & Primacy Effects, and Network Interference (SUTVA violations).

    1. Sample Ratio Mismatch (SRM): The Ultimate Trustworthiness Gate

    Suppose an experiment has $K$ arms with target traffic allocation probabilities $p_1, \dots, p_K$ ($\sum_{k=1}^K p_k = 1$) and observed randomized user counts $O_1, \dots, O_K$ ($N = \sum_{k=1}^K O_k$). Under valid independent hash assignment, $(O_1, \dots, O_K) \sim \text{Multinomial}(N; p_1, \dots, p_K)$ with expected counts $E_k = N p_k$. Pearson's goodness-of-fit statistic follows a chi-square distribution with $K-1$ degrees of freedom:

    $$\chi^2_{\text{SRM}} = \sum_{k=1}^{K} \frac{\left(O_k - E_k\right)^2}{E_k} \;\xrightarrow{d}\; \chi^2_{K-1}$$

    For a two-arm $50/50$ split ($p_C = p_T = 0.5$, $E = N/2$), this simplifies to:

    $$\chi^2_{\text{SRM}} = \frac{(O_C - O_T)^2}{N} = z^2, \qquad z = \frac{O_T - N/2}{\sqrt{N/4}}$$

    Production platforms flag an SRM Alert (blocking all metric readouts) whenever $p_{\text{SRM}} < 0.001$. Why does even a $0.4\%$ missing-user gap wreck an A/B test? Because the missing $0.4\%$ of users are never missing at random—they are typically users whose client app crashed on the slow treatment model, or users on older devices who timed out before firing the exposure event. Removing the worst-performing $0.4\%$ of users from Treatment creates massive survivorship bias that makes a broken model look like a $+3\%$ winner!

    Common Root Causes of SRM in ML Systems

    • Triggered Exposure Asymmetry: Logging experiment exposure after model inference completes rather than right before calling the model (so treatment timeouts/crashes fail to log exposure).
    • Bot & Fraud Filtering Post-Randomization: A treatment ranking change alters how many clicks a crawler generates, causing downstream heuristic bot filters to remove more users from one arm.
    • Client Redirects & Cache Eviction: Treatment redirects or larger payload sizes cause users on high-latency cellular networks to abandon before the telemetry beacon fires.

    2. Novelty Effect vs. Primacy (Learning) Effect

    Even when randomization is clean, the treatment effect $\hat{\Delta}(t)$ is often non-stationary over time $t$ since exposure:

    • Novelty Effect (Spike then Decay): A visually prominent new UI widget or novel recommendation carousel attracts curiosity clicks on Day 1–3, but once users realize its utility is low, $\hat{\Delta}(t)$ decays exponentially toward $0$: $\Delta(t) = \Delta_\infty + A e^{-t/\tau}$.
    • Primacy / Habituation Effect (Dip then Climb): Experienced users have muscle memory for the old ranking/layout. A superior new ranking initially slows them down on Day 1–3 before they adapt and $\hat{\Delta}(t)$ climbs to a positive steady state $\Delta_\infty > 0$.

    How to Isolate True Novelty from Day-of-Week Seasonality: Never just plot overall daily lift $\hat{\Delta}_t$ by calendar date—because Day 1 users and Day 10 users are mixed together! Instead, cohort by First-Exposure Day and compare users who are on Day 1 of exposure on Wednesday against users who are on Day 10 of exposure on that exact same Wednesday.

    3. Network Interference & SUTVA Violations

    Standard user-level A/B testing assumes the Stable Unit Treatment Value Assumption (SUTVA): user $u$'s outcome $Y_u$ depends only on user $u$'s treatment assignment $Z_u \in \{0,1\}$, and not on the treatment assignment $Z_{u'}$ of any other user $u' \ne u$. SUTVA fails catastrophically in three common ML System Design domains:

    Interference Type Mechanism of Spillover Bias Gold-Standard Experimental Design
    1. Two-Sided Shared Supply
    (Uber, DoorDash, Airbnb, Limited-Inventory E-Commerce)    		 Treatment buyers book scarce drivers/listings faster, stealing supply from Control buyers in the same city and artificially depressing Control conversion ($\hat{\Delta}_{\text{naïve}} \gg \Delta_{\text{true}}$). Switchback Testing (randomize city $\times$ 30-min time blocks) or Geo-Market Cluster A/B (Synthetic Control / Diff-in-Diff).
    2. Social Graph Spillover
    (Messaging, Social Sharing, Viral Notifications)    		 A Treatment user sends more messages to a Control friend, boosting the Control friend's activity and attenuating the measured lift toward zero. Graph Cluster Randomization (partition social graph via balanced min-cut / Louvain into $K$ clusters) + Ego-Cluster Analysis.
    3. Shared Advertiser Budgets
    (Ad Auctions & Budget Pacing)    		 Treatment ranking spends an advertiser's daily budget $B_a$ faster, starving Control auctions of that advertiser's bids. Budget-Split Experimentation: split both users and each advertiser's daily budget ($50\% B_a$ to Control, $50\% B_a$ to Treatment).

    4. Mathematical Formulation of Switchback Experiments

    In a Switchback Experiment across $M$ geographic regions and $T$ discrete time windows (e.g., $\Delta t = 30\text{ or }60\text{ minutes}$, chosen longer than the marketplace memory / trip duration to limit temporal carryover), the randomization unit is the $(m, t)$ region-time block with outcome $Y_{m,t}$ and requests $X_{m,t}$. Because adjacent blocks within region $m$ are correlated over time, we compute cluster-robust (Liang–Zeger / Newey–West) standard errors clustered at the region-day or region level, often paired with pre-experiment CUPED on historical $(m, t)$ demand.

    5. Production Python Implementation: SRM Check & Cluster-Robust Switchback Test

    import numpy as np
from scipy import stats

def check_srm(observed_counts: list[int], expected_probs: list[float], alpha: float = 0.001) -> dict:
    """Runs Pearson Chi-Square goodness-of-fit test for Sample Ratio Mismatch."""
    obs = np.asarray(observed_counts, dtype=float)
    probs = np.asarray(expected_probs, dtype=float)
    probs = probs / probs.sum()
    exp = obs.sum() * probs
    chi2_stat, p_val = stats.chisquare(f_obs=obs, f_exp=exp)
    return {
        "observed": observed_counts,
        "expected": exp.round(1).tolist(),
        "chi2_stat": round(float(chi2_stat), 4),
        "p_value": float(p_val),
        "srm_detected": bool(p_val < alpha),
    }

if __name__ == "__main__":
    # 502,150 Control vs 497,850 Treatment on a 50/50 split
    print(check_srm([502_150, 497_850], [0.5, 0.5]))

    6. Self-Check & Interview Exercises

    Exercise 1 — Diagnosing Shared-Budget Interference in Ad Auctions

    You test a new CTR model that increases ad clicks by $+8\%$ in a user-randomized $50/50$ A/B test, where both arms draw from a single shared advertiser daily budget pool. When you roll the model out to $100\%$ of users, the actual lift is only $+1.5\%$. Why did the $50/50$ test overestimate the lift by more than $5\times$?

    Check solution

    In the shared-budget $50/50$ test, Treatment auctions bid higher or won more clicks, draining budget-constrained advertisers' daily caps early—which starved the Control arm of eligible ads! Thus Treatment gained $+4\%$ by cannibalizing $-4\%$ from Control, creating an apparent $+8\%$ gap. Splitting each advertiser's daily budget proportionally ($50\%$ dedicated to Control users, $50\%$ dedicated to Treatment users) eliminates budget cannibalization.
