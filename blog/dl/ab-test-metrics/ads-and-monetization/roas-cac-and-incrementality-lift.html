    Return on Ad Spend (ROAS), CPA/CAC & Ghost-Ad Incrementality Lift (iROAS)

    October 2026 · ML System Design · Causal Incrementality & Ghost Ads

    From the advertiser's perspective, clicks and impressions are merely means to an end: every dollar spent on ads must return more than a dollar in incremental gross margin. Yet standard Attributed ROAS suffers from a fatal flaw: an ML conversion model can achieve a $10\times$ attributed ROAS simply by showing ads to loyal customers who already had items in their shopping cart and were going to buy anyway! Here we distinguish Attributed ROAS / CPA from causal Incremental ROAS ($\text{iROAS}$) and derive the Ghost Ads methodology (Johnson, Lewis & Nubbemeyer 2017) that reduces incrementality test variance by over $90\%$.

    1. Attributed CPA, CAC & ROAS vs. Causal Incrementality

    Let $S_a$ be an advertiser's total ad spend, $N_{\text{attr}}$ be the number of attributed conversions, and $V_{\text{attr}} = \sum_{i=1}^{N_{\text{attr}}} v_i$ be total attributed conversion value (revenue):

    $$\text{Attributed CPA} = \frac{S_a}{N_{\text{attr}}}, \qquad \text{Attributed ROAS} = \frac{V_{\text{attr}}}{S_a}$$

    Now let $Y_u(1)$ be user $u$'s conversion value when eligible to see advertiser $a$'s ads (Treatment), and $Y_u(0)$ be user $u$'s counterfactual conversion value when held out from advertiser $a$'s ads (Control). True causal Conversion Lift and Incremental ROAS ($\text{iROAS}$) are:

    $$\Delta V_{\text{incremental}} = N_T \left(\bar{Y}_T - \bar{Y}_C\right), \qquad \boxed{\text{iROAS} = \frac{N_T \left(\bar{Y}_T - \bar{Y}_C\right)}{S_{a,T}}}$$ $$\text{Incremental CPA (iCPA)} = \frac{S_{a,T}}{N_T \left(\bar{C}_T - \bar{C}_C\right)}, \qquad \text{Incrementality Factor (IF)} = \frac{\text{iROAS}}{\text{Attributed ROAS}}$$
    Why Retargeting Campaigns Often Have High ROAS but Low iROAS: Consider a brand keyword search ("Nike.com login") or a cart-abandonment retargeting campaign. Because users in this segment have a $40\%$ baseline organic purchase probability ($Y_u(0) = 0.40$), showing them an ad and claiming 1-day view-through or click attribution yields a massive Attributed ROAS (e.g., $12.0\times$), even if the ad only increased conversion probability from $40.0\%$ to $41.0\%$ ($\text{Incrementality Factor} = 1\% / 41\% = 2.4\%$, giving a true $\text{iROAS}$ of only $0.29\times$!).

    2. Why Naive Intent-to-Treat (ITT) Holdouts Have Huge Variance—and How Ghost Ads Fix It

    Suppose we run a randomized user holdout (Intent-to-Treat, $\text{ITT}$) for Advertiser $a$, where only $p_{\text{exp}} = 2\%$ of users in the platform's target audience actually win an auction and see Advertiser $a$'s ad during the 14-day test. The remaining $98\%$ of users in Treatment never see Advertiser $a$'s ad!

    In a naïve ITT comparison ($\bar{Y}_T^{\text{all}} - \bar{Y}_C^{\text{all}}$), the $98\%$ unexposed users have zero treatment effect ($\mathbb{E}[Y_T - Y_C \mid \text{unexposed}] = 0$)—they contribute **pure noise ($98\%$ of the variance)** that drowns out the signal from the $2\%$ exposed users! Specifically, the required sample size scales as $O(1 / p_{\text{exp}}^2)$: when $p_{\text{exp}} = 2\%$, you need $2{,}500\times$ more total users than if you only compared exposed users!

    Why can't we just filter the Treatment arm to the $2\%$ exposed users and compare them to all Control users? Because the $2\%$ of users who won Advertiser $a$'s auction are a highly non-random high-intent subset (selection bias).

    The Ghost Ads Solution (Counterfactual Auction Logging)

    In Ghost Ads (Google / Meta Conversion Lift):

    1. When a Treatment user enters an ad auction and Advertiser $a$ wins, we serve Advertiser $a$'s ad and log a Treated Impression ($D_u = 1$).
    2. When a Control user enters an ad auction, we run the exact same auction including Advertiser $a$'s bid. If Advertiser $a$ would have won the auction, we log a "Ghost Ad" tag ($D_u = 1$) for that Control user, suppress Advertiser $a$'s ad, and serve the runner-up #2 ad instead!

    Now both Treatment users with $D_u = 1$ (saw Ad $a$) and Control users with $D_u = 1$ (would have seen Ad $a$ under the identical auction rules) are 100% exchangeable! We can discard the $98\%$ unexposed users ($D_u = 0$) in both arms and compute the Average Treatment Effect on the Treated ($\text{ATT}$) exclusively on the $D_u = 1$ subset:

    $$\widehat{\text{ATT}}_{\text{Ghost}} = \bar{Y}_{T, D=1} - \bar{Y}_{C, D=1}, \qquad \text{Var}\!\left(\widehat{\text{ATT}}_{\text{Ghost}}\right) \approx p_{\text{exp}} \cdot \text{Var}\!\left(\widehat{\text{ATT}}_{\text{ITT}}\right)$$

    When $p_{\text{exp}} = 2\%$, Ghost Ads cuts the variance of the incrementality estimator by a factor of $1 / 0.02 = \mathbf{50\times}$ (and without wasting ad spend on Paid Public Service Announcements / PSAs)!

    3. Production Python Implementation: Naive ITT vs. Ghost-Ad Incrementality

    import numpy as np
from scipy import stats

def compare_itt_vs_ghost_ads(
    y_t: np.ndarray, exposed_t: np.ndarray, spend_t: float,
    y_c: np.ndarray, ghost_exposed_c: np.ndarray,
) -> dict:
    """
    Compares Naive Intent-to-Treat (ITT) lift against Ghost-Ad conditioned lift
    when only a small fraction p_exp of auction users win the target ad.
    """
    n_t = len(y_t)
    p_exp_t = exposed_t.mean()

    # 1. Naive ITT Estimator (diluted by unexposed users)
    itt_diff_per_user = y_t.mean() - y_c.mean()
    itt_se_per_user = np.sqrt(y_t.var(ddof=1) / len(y_t) + y_c.var(ddof=1) / len(y_c))
    itt_total_inc_rev = n_t * itt_diff_per_user
    itt_iroas = itt_total_inc_rev / spend_t
    itt_z = itt_diff_per_user / itt_se_per_user

    # 2. Ghost-Ad Estimator (conditioned on D_u = 1 in both arms)
    yt_exp = y_t[exposed_t == 1]
    yc_ghost = y_c[ghost_exposed_c == 1]
    att_ghost = yt_exp.mean() - yc_ghost.mean()
    att_se = np.sqrt(yt_exp.var(ddof=1) / len(yt_exp) + yc_ghost.var(ddof=1) / len(yc_ghost))
    ghost_total_inc_rev = len(yt_exp) * att_ghost
    ghost_iroas = ghost_total_inc_rev / spend_t
    ghost_z = att_ghost / att_se

    return {
        "exposure_rate_pct": round(float(p_exp_t * 100), 2),
        "itt_iroas": round(float(itt_iroas), 3),
        "itt_z_stat": round(float(itt_z), 3),
        "ghost_ad_iroas": round(float(ghost_iroas), 3),
        "ghost_ad_z_stat": round(float(ghost_z), 3),
        "se_reduction_factor": round(float((itt_se_per_user / p_exp_t) / att_se), 2),
    }

if __name__ == "__main__":
    rng = np.random.default_rng(99)
    N = 200_000
    # Only 3% of users win the auction for Advertiser A
    exp_t = rng.binomial(1, 0.03, size=N)
    exp_c = rng.binomial(1, 0.03, size=N)

    # Unexposed users spend $5 on average; exposed users have higher baseline ($20) + $3 true lift
    rev_t = np.where(exp_t == 1, rng.normal(23.0, 30.0, N), rng.normal(5.0, 15.0, N))
    rev_c = np.where(exp_c == 1, rng.normal(20.0, 30.0, N), rng.normal(5.0, 15.0, N))
    print(compare_itt_vs_ghost_ads(rev_t, exp_t, spend_t=10_000.0, y_c=rev_c, ghost_exposed_c=exp_c))

    4. Self-Check & Interview Exercises

    Exercise 1 — Why Are Ghost Ads Superior to PSA (Public Service Announcement) Holdouts?

    Before Ghost Ads were invented, ad platforms measured incrementality by serving paid "Red Cross / Smokey Bear PSA" ads to the Control group whenever the advertiser won the auction. What are the two major advantages of Ghost Ads over PSA tests?

    Check solution

    (1) Zero Opportunity Cost: In a PSA test, either the advertiser or the platform has to pay for the 10–20% of impressions wasted on PSAs; Ghost Ads serves the runner-up paying ad (#2 in the auction) to Control users, preserving nearly 100% of platform revenue. (2) Realistic Counterfactual Baseline: In real life, if Advertiser $a$ shuts off their campaign, users don't see a Red Cross PSA—they see the next competitive commercial ad (#2 in the auction), which is exactly what Ghost Ads serves!
