    Regression & Forecasting Metrics: MAE, RMSE, Huber, $R^2$, MAPE/MASE, Pinball Loss, and CRPS

    October 2026 · 9 min read

    Prove why MSE elicits the conditional mean $\mathbb E[Y\mid X]$, MAE elicits the conditional median, and Pinball loss $\rho_\tau$ elicits the exact $\tau$-quantile; diagnose the asymmetry of MAPE and fix it with RMSLE and Hyndman’s MASE; and evaluate full predictive distributions with CRPS.

    When predicting continuous quantities—stock returns, demand forecasts, bounding-box coordinates, or molecular energies—your choice of evaluation metric secretly dictates which statistical functional of the conditional distribution $p(y\mid x)$ a Bayes-optimal model should output. If $p(y\mid x)$ is skewed (as in retail demand or income), the model that minimizes RMSE and the model that minimizes MAE output completely different numbers!

    1. MSE, RMSE, MAE, MedAE, and Huber: Which Functional Do They Elicit?

    For residuals $e_i = y_i - \hat y_i$ ($i = 1,\dots,N$), the four foundational point-error metrics are Mean Squared Error (MSE), Root Mean Squared Error (RMSE), Mean Absolute Error (MAE), and Median Absolute Error (MedAE):

    $$\operatorname{MSE} = \frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^N (y_i - \hat y_i)^2,\qquad \operatorname{RMSE} = \sqrt{\operatorname{MSE}},\qquad \operatorname{MAE} = \frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^N |y_i - \hat y_i|,\qquad \operatorname{MedAE} = \operatorname{median}_{1\le i\le N}|y_i - \hat y_i|.$$

    By the Cauchy–Schwarz inequality on $(|e_1|,\dots,|e_N|)$ and $(1,\dots,1)$, $\operatorname{MAE}$ and $\operatorname{RMSE}$ always obey the sandwich bound:

    $$\operatorname{MAE} \;\le\; \operatorname{RMSE} \;\le\; \sqrt{N}\,\operatorname{MAE}.$$

    The ratio $\operatorname{RMSE}/\operatorname{MAE} \ge 1$ directly measures outlier dispersion: it equals $1$ if and only if every residual has the exact same magnitude $|e_i| = c$, and approaches $\sqrt{N}$ when a single catastrophic outlier dominates the sum of squares.

    Proof of Functional Elicitation (Mean vs. Median): What constant prediction $\hat y$ minimizes expected squared error vs. expected absolute error under $Y \sim F_Y$?

    • MSE / RMSE elicits the Conditional Mean:
      $$\frac{\partial}{\partial \hat y}\mathbb E\big[(Y - \hat y)^2\big] = -2\,\mathbb E[Y - \hat y] = 0 \iff \hat y^* = \mathbb E[Y\mid X].$$
    • MAE elicits the Conditional Median: Split $\mathbb E[|Y - \hat y|] = \int_{-\infty}^{\hat y} (\hat y - y)f(y)\,dy + \int_{\hat y}^\infty (y - \hat y)f(y)\,dy$ and differentiate with respect to $\hat y$ using the Leibniz integral rule:
      $$\frac{\partial}{\partial \hat y}\mathbb E\big[|Y - \hat y|\big] = \int_{-\infty}^{\hat y} f(y)\,dy - \int_{\hat y}^\infty f(y)\,dy = F_Y(\hat y) - \big(1 - F_Y(\hat y)\big) = 2F_Y(\hat y) - 1 = 0 \iff F_Y(\hat y^*) = 0.5.$$

    To combine MSE’s smooth curvature near $e=0$ with MAE’s linear robustness to large outliers ($|e| > \delta$), Huber Loss (Smooth $L_1$) pieces together a parabola and a straight line with matching slope $\pm\delta$ at $|e| = \delta$:

    $$\ell_\delta(e) = \begin{cases} \frac{1}{2}e^2, & |e| \le \delta, \\[4pt] \delta\left(|e| - \frac{1}{2}\delta\right), & |e| > \delta. \end{cases}$$

    2. Coefficient of Determination ($R^2$) and Adjusted $R^2$

    Because $\operatorname{RMSE}$ and $\operatorname{MAE}$ carry the physical units of $y$ (dollars, megawatts, meters), we cannot tell from $\operatorname{RMSE} = 12.4$ alone whether a model is stellar or worse than guessing the sample mean $\bar y = \frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^N y_i$. The Coefficient of Determination ($R^2$) normalizes the residual sum of squares $\text{SS}_{\text{res}}$ by the total variance sum of squares $\text{SS}_{\text{tot}}$:

    $$R^2 = 1 - \frac{\text{SS}_{\text{res}}}{\text{SS}_{\text{tot}}} = 1 - \frac{\sum_{i=1}^N (y_i - \hat y_i)^2}{\sum_{i=1}^N (y_i - \bar y)^2} = 1 - \frac{\operatorname{MSE}(\hat y)}{\operatorname{Var}(y)} \in (-\infty, 1].$$
    • Why $R^2$ can be strictly negative on test sets: Despite its name, $R^2$ is not the square of a number on out-of-sample data! Anytime a model’s test $\operatorname{MSE}$ exceeds the variance of predicting the constant mean $\bar y$, $\frac{\text{SS}_{\text{res}}}{\text{SS}_{\text{tot}}} > 1 \implies R^2 < 0$.
    • Adjusted $R^2$: On training data, adding useless random features $p$ can never increase $\text{SS}_{\text{res}}$ under ordinary least squares, artificially inflating $R^2$. Adjusted $R^2$ divides numerator and denominator by their unbiased degrees of freedom ($N - p - 1$ and $N - 1$):
      $$R_{\text{adj}}^2 = 1 - \frac{\text{SS}_{\text{res}} / (N - p - 1)}{\text{SS}_{\text{tot}} / (N - 1)} = 1 - (1 - R^2)\frac{N - 1}{N - p - 1}.$$

    3. Relative & Time-Series Metrics: MAPE, sMAPE, WAPE, RMSLE, and MASE

    In demand forecasting and multi-item retail prediction where SKU A sells $10$ units/day and SKU B sells $100{,}000$ units/day, how do we aggregate percentage accuracy across items?

    • MAPE (Mean Absolute Percentage Error) — and its two famous traps:
      $$\operatorname{MAPE} = \frac{100\%}{N}\sum_{i=1}^N \frac{|y_i - \hat y_i|}{|y_i|}.$$
      Trap 1 (Zero division): Any intermittent demand step with $y_i = 0$ makes $\operatorname{MAPE} = \infty$. Trap 2 (Systematic under-forecast bias): For the exact same absolute error $|e| = 50$, under-forecasting $\hat y = 50$ when $y = 100$ gives $\frac{50}{100} = 50\%$ error, whereas over-forecasting $\hat y = 100$ when $y = 50$ gives $\frac{50}{50} = 100\%$ error! Optimizing MAPE therefore biases models to systematically under-predict ($0 \le \hat y \le y$).
    • sMAPE, WAPE, and RMSLE:
      $$\operatorname{sMAPE} = \frac{100\%}{N}\sum_{i=1}^N \frac{2|y_i - \hat y_i|}{|y_i| + |\hat y_i|},\qquad \operatorname{WAPE} = \frac{\sum_{i=1}^N |y_i - \hat y_i|}{\sum_{i=1}^N |y_i|},\qquad \operatorname{RMSLE} = \sqrt{\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^N \big(\ln(1 + \hat y_i) - \ln(1 + y_i)\big)^2}.$$
      Because $\ln\frac{1+\hat y}{1+y} = -\ln\frac{1+y}{1+\hat y}$, RMSLE treats multiplicative ratios ($\hat y = 2y$ vs. $\hat y = \frac{1}{2}y$) with exact symmetry!
    • MASE (Mean Absolute Scaled Error — Hyndman & Koehler, 2006): The gold standard for time-series competitions (M4/M5), MASE divides out-of-sample MAE by the in-sample MAE of the seasonal naive forecast $\hat y_t = y_{t-m}$ (period $m$, or $m=1$ for non-seasonal random walk):
      $$\operatorname{MASE} = \frac{\frac{1}{H}\sum_{t=T+1}^{T+H} |y_t - \hat y_t|}{\frac{1}{T - m}\sum_{t=m+1}^T |y_t - y_{t-m}|}.$$
      MASE is scale-free, symmetric, and never divides by zero (unless the entire historical series is flat); $\operatorname{MASE} < 1$ means the model beats the seasonal naive baseline.

    4. Probabilistic & Quantile Metrics: Pinball Loss and CRPS

    When decision-makers need uncertainty bounds (e.g., $P_{10}, P_{50}, P_{90}$ server capacity or inventory safety stock), we evaluate quantile and distributional forecasts:

    1. Pinball Loss (Quantile Loss / Check Function $\rho_\tau$): For target quantile $\tau \in (0, 1)$ and residual $e = y - \hat q_\tau$:
      $$\rho_\tau(y - \hat q_\tau) = \max\!\big(\tau(y - \hat q_\tau),\, (\tau - 1)(y - \hat q_\tau)\big) = \begin{cases} \tau(y - \hat q_\tau), & y \ge \hat q_\tau, \\ (1 - \tau)(\hat q_\tau - y), & y < \hat q_\tau. \end{cases}$$
      Setting $\frac{\partial}{\partial \hat q_\tau}\mathbb E[\rho_\tau(Y - \hat q_\tau)] = -\tau(1 - F_Y(\hat q_\tau)) + (1 - \tau)F_Y(\hat q_\tau) = F_Y(\hat q_\tau) - \tau = 0$ proves that Pinball loss is the unique strictly proper loss that elicits the $\tau$-quantile $\hat q_\tau^* = F_Y^{-1}(\tau)$! (At $\tau = 0.5$, $2\rho_{0.5}(e) = |e| = \operatorname{MAE}$.)
    2. Continuous Ranked Probability Score (CRPS — Gneiting & Raftery, 2007): To evaluate a full predictive cumulative distribution $F(z)$ against a scalar observation $y$, CRPS integrates the Brier score over all thresholds $z \in \mathbb R$ (or equivalently integrates Pinball loss over all quantiles $\tau \in [0, 1]$):
      $$\operatorname{CRPS}(F, y) = \int_{-\infty}^{+\infty} \big(F(z) - \mathbf 1[z \ge y]\big)^2\,dz = 2\int_0^1 \rho_\tau\!\big(y - F^{-1}(\tau)\big)\,d\tau = \mathbb E_F|X - y| - \frac{1}{2}\mathbb E_F|X - X'|,$$
      where $X, X' \stackrel{\text{i.i.d.}}{\sim} F$. When $F = \mathcal N(\mu, \sigma^2)$ is Gaussian, CRPS has the exact closed form $\operatorname{CRPS}\big(\mathcal N(\mu,\sigma^2), y\big) = \sigma\!\left[z(2\Phi(z) - 1) + 2\phi(z) - \frac{1}{\sqrt{\pi}}\right]$ where $z = \frac{y-\mu}{\sigma}$, and as $\sigma \to 0$ (point forecast), $\operatorname{CRPS}$ reduces identically to $\operatorname{MAE} = |y - \mu|$!

    5. Compute Regression, Quantile Pinball, MASE, and Gaussian CRPS in PyTorch

    import torch
import math

def pinball_loss(y_true: torch.Tensor, y_quant: torch.Tensor, tau: float) -> float:
    e = y_true - y_quant
    return torch.maximum(tau * e, (tau - 1.0) * e).mean().item()

def mase_score(y_train: torch.Tensor, y_test: torch.Tensor, y_pred: torch.Tensor, seasonality: int = 1) -> float:
    naive_mae = (y_train[seasonality:] - y_train[:-seasonality]).abs().mean()
    test_mae  = (y_test - y_pred).abs().mean()
    return (test_mae / (naive_mae + 1e-12)).item()

def crps_gaussian(y_true: torch.Tensor, mu: torch.Tensor, sigma: torch.Tensor) -> float:
    """Exact closed-form CRPS for predictive distribution N(mu, sigma^2)."""
    z = (y_true - mu) / (sigma + 1e-12)
    Phi = 0.5 * (1.0 + torch.erf(z / math.sqrt(2.0)))
    phi = torch.exp(-0.5 * z**2) / math.sqrt(2.0 * math.pi)
    crps = sigma * (z * (2.0 * Phi - 1.0) + 2.0 * phi - 1.0 / math.sqrt(math.pi))
    return crps.mean().item()

y_tr = torch.tensor([10., 12., 15., 11., 14., 18.])
y_te = torch.tensor([20., 22., 19.])
y_pr = torch.tensor([19., 21., 20.])
print("MASE (m=1):", round(mase_score(y_tr, y_te, y_pr), 4))
print("Pinball tau=0.9:", round(pinball_loss(y_te, y_pr, tau=0.9), 4))
print("Gaussian CRPS:", round(crps_gaussian(y_te, y_pr, torch.full_like(y_pr, 1.5)), 4))

    6. Questions worth answering

    If future retail demand for a product follows a right-skewed log-normal distribution with $\text{Median} = 100$ and $\text{Mean} = 140$, what happens if you train your model with MSE loss but the business evaluates your forecasts with MAE?

    Your MSE-trained model will output the conditional mean $\hat y \approx 140$, whereas MAE is uniquely minimized by the conditional median $\hat y^* = 100$. You will systematically lose the evaluation benchmark against a model trained directly with L1/MAE (or Pinball $\tau=0.5$) loss! Always align the training loss elicitation with the target evaluation metric.

    Why does $\operatorname{CRPS}(F, y) = \mathbb E_F|X - y| - \frac{1}{2}\mathbb E_F|X - X'|$ reward both accuracy and sharpness?

    The first term $\mathbb E_F|X - y|$ penalizes the expected distance from drawn samples $X \sim F$ to the truth $y$ (calibration/accuracy), while the second term $-\frac{1}{2}\mathbb E_F|X - X'|$ rewards the distribution for concentrating its probability mass tightly around $y$ without becoming overconfident.
