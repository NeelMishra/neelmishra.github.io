Search, Retrieval & Recommendation Ranking Metrics: Precision@K, MRR, MAP@K, nDCG@K, ERR, RBP, and Beyond-Accuracy

A search engine or recommender system does not output a single binary label—it outputs an ordered slate of $K$ items where user attention decays rapidly with rank position. Here we derive set-based top-$K$ metrics, position-sensitive binary metrics (MRR, MAP@$K$), graded logarithmic discounting (CG, DCG, IDCG, nDCG@$K$), user browsing cascade models (RBP, ERR), and beyond-accuracy ecosystem metrics (Intra-List Diversity, Self-Information Novelty, Serendipity, Catalog Coverage, and Gini Exposure).

1. Top-$K$ Set Metrics: Precision@$K$, Recall@$K$, $F_1$@$K$, and Hit Rate@$K$

Suppose a retrieval or recommendation model scores a catalog of items $\mathcal{I}$ for query (or user) $q \in \mathcal{Q}$ and returns the top-$K$ ranked list:

$$L_q(K) = \bigl(d_1, d_2, \dots, d_K\bigr)$$

In the binary relevance setting, let $\mathcal{R}_q \subseteq \mathcal{I}$ denote the ground-truth set of relevant items for query $q$, and let $\text{rel}_k = \mathbb{I}[d_k \in \mathcal{R}_q] \in \{0, 1\}$ indicate whether the item at rank $k$ is relevant.

1.1 Precision@$K$ and Recall@$K$

Treating $L_q(K)$ as an unordered set of size $K$:

$$\text{Precision@}K(q) = \frac{|L_q(K) \cap \mathcal{R}_q|}{K} = \frac{1}{K}\sum_{k=1}^{K} \text{rel}_k$$
$$\text{Recall@}K(q) = \frac{|L_q(K) \cap \mathcal{R}_q|}{|\mathcal{R}_q|} = \frac{1}{|\mathcal{R}_q|}\sum_{k=1}^{K} \text{rel}_k$$

Notice a critical ceiling anomaly: if a user only has $|\mathcal{R}_q| = 2$ relevant items in the entire catalog and we evaluate at $K = 10$, then even a perfect ranker that places both items at ranks $1$ and $2$ can achieve at most $\text{Precision@}10 = 2/10 = 0.20$. Conversely, if $|\mathcal{R}_q| = 50$ and $K = 5$, $\text{Recall@}5$ is upper-bounded by $5/50 = 0.10$. For this reason, some benchmarks report R-Precision, which evaluates Precision@$K$ at the query-specific cutoff $K = |\mathcal{R}_q|$ (where $\text{Precision@}|\mathcal{R}_q| = \text{Recall@}|\mathcal{R}_q|$).

1.2 $F_1$@$K$ and Hit Rate@$K$ (HR@$K$)

The harmonic mean at cutoff $K$ is:

$$F_1\text{@}K(q) = \frac{2\,\text{Precision@}K(q)\,\text{Recall@}K(q)}{\text{Precision@}K(q) + \text{Recall@}K(q)} = \frac{2\sum_{k=1}^{K}\text{rel}_k}{K + |\mathcal{R}_q|}$$

Hit Rate@$K$ (also called Success@$K$ or Top-$K$ Accuracy when $|\mathcal{R}_q|=1$) asks a binary question: did at least one relevant item appear anywhere in the top-$K$ slate?

$$\text{HR@}K(q) = \mathbb{I}\!\left[\sum_{k=1}^{K} \text{rel}_k \ge 1\right], \qquad \text{HR@}K = \frac{1}{|\mathcal{Q}|}\sum_{q \in \mathcal{Q}} \text{HR@}K(q)$$
Why Set Metrics Are Insufficient for Ranking: Precision@$10$, Recall@$10$, and HR@$10$ assign the exact same score whether the single relevant item appears at rank $k=1$ (top of the screen) or rank $k=10$ (below the fold). To evaluate ordering quality inside the top-$K$ slate, we need position-sensitive metrics.

2. Position-Aware Binary Ranking: MRR and MAP@$K$

2.1 Mean Reciprocal Rank (MRR)

When a user is looking for a single target answer (navigational search, question answering, link prediction in knowledge graphs, or next-item recommendation), only the rank of the first relevant item matters:

$$k_q^* = \min\{\,k \ge 1 : \text{rel}_k = 1\,\}$$

The Reciprocal Rank (RR) is $1/k_q^*$ (or $0$ if no relevant item appears in the top-$K$ cutoff for MRR@$K$), and Mean Reciprocal Rank (MRR) averages RR across all queries $\mathcal{Q}$:

$$\text{MRR@}K = \frac{1}{|\mathcal{Q}|}\sum_{q \in \mathcal{Q}} \frac{\mathbb{I}[k_q^* \le K]}{k_q^*}$$

Dropping the first relevant result from rank $1$ to rank $2$ cuts RR in half ($1.0 \to 0.5$); dropping from rank $1$ to rank $5$ cuts RR by $80\%$. However, MRR completely ignores every relevant item after $k_q^*$.

2.2 Average Precision (AP@$K$) and Mean Average Precision (MAP@$K$)

When a query has multiple relevant items ($|\mathcal{R}_q| > 1$), we want every relevant item to be ranked as high as possible. Average Precision at $K$ ($\text{AP@}K$) averages the Precision@$k$ values evaluated specifically at the ranks $k \in \{1, \dots, K\}$ where a relevant item occurs:

$$\text{AP@}K(q) = \frac{1}{\min(K, |\mathcal{R}_q|)} \sum_{k=1}^{K} \text{Precision@}k(q) \cdot \text{rel}_k$$

Wait—why is the denominator $\min(K, |\mathcal{R}_q|)$ (or in classic TREC retrieval, $|\mathcal{R}_q|$) instead of the number of hits found inside the top $K$?

  • If we divided only by $\sum_{k=1}^{K}\text{rel}_k$ (the number of hits retrieved), then retrieving only 1 relevant item at rank 1 and missing the other 9 relevant items would yield $\text{AP} = 1.0/1 = 1.0$!
  • Dividing by $|\mathcal{R}_q|$ (or $\min(K, |\mathcal{R}_q|)$ when $K < |\mathcal{R}_q|$ in Kaggle/recommender conventions) ensures that $\text{AP@}K$ jointly measures both precision at each hit and total recall—it is the discrete Riemann-sum approximation to the area under the Precision–Recall curve: $\text{AP} \approx \int_0^1 P(R)\,dR$.

Averaging $\text{AP@}K(q)$ across all queries yields Mean Average Precision ($\text{MAP@}K$):

$$\text{MAP@}K = \frac{1}{|\mathcal{Q}|}\sum_{q \in \mathcal{Q}} \text{AP@}K(q)$$

3. Graded Relevance & Logarithmic Discounting: CG, DCG, IDCG, and nDCG@$K$

In real-world web search, e-commerce, and media streaming, relevance is rarely binary. An item might be perfectly relevant ($r_k = 3$, e.g., exact product match or user watched 100%), highly relevant ($r_k = 2$), marginally relevant ($r_k = 1$), or irrelevant ($r_k = 0$). Järvelin and Kekäläinen (2002) introduced the Cumulative Gain family to handle non-binary graded relevance $r_k \ge 0$.

3.1 Cumulative Gain (CG@$K$) and Discounted Cumulative Gain (DCG@$K$)

Cumulative Gain ($\text{CG@}K$) simply sums the relevance grades in the top $K$ positions, ignoring rank order:

$$\text{CG@}K = \sum_{k=1}^{K} r_k$$

Discounted Cumulative Gain ($\text{DCG@}K$) maps each relevance grade $r_k$ through a gain function $G(r_k)$ and divides by a monotonically increasing logarithmic penalty $D(k) = \log_2(k+1)$:

$$\text{DCG@}K = \sum_{k=1}^{K} \frac{2^{r_k} - 1}{\log_2(k + 1)} \qquad \text{(Exponential / Burges Learning-to-Rank formulation)}$$
$$\text{DCG}_{\text{linear}}\text{@}K = \sum_{k=1}^{K} \frac{r_k}{\log_2(k + 1)} \qquad \text{(Linear formulation)}$$

Why use $\log_2(k+1)$ in the denominator? At rank $k=1$, $\log_2(1+1) = \log_2(2) = 1$, so the top slot suffers no discount ($D(1) = 1$). At $k=2$, the weight is $1/\log_2(3) \approx 0.6309$; at $k=3$, $1/\log_2(4) = 0.5000$. Furthermore, exponential gain $G(r_k) = 2^{r_k}-1$ maps $\{0, 1, 2, 3, 4\} \mapsto \{0, 1, 3, 7, 15\}$, heavily prioritizing "perfect" grade-3/4 hits over mediocre grade-1 hits.

3.2 Ideal DCG (IDCG@$K$) and Normalized DCG (nDCG@$K$)

Raw $\text{DCG@}K$ is not comparable across queries: a broad query with 20 grade-3 documents will naturally have a much larger $\text{DCG@}10$ than a niche query with only one grade-2 document. Let $r_{(1)} \ge r_{(2)} \ge \dots \ge r_{(|\mathcal{I}|)}$ denote all ground-truth relevance grades for query $q$ sorted in descending order (across the entire catalog, not just the model's top-$K$ retrieved list!). The Ideal DCG ($\text{IDCG@}K$) is the maximum possible $\text{DCG@}K$ achievable by an oracle ranker:

$$\text{IDCG@}K = \sum_{k=1}^{K} \frac{2^{r_{(k)}} - 1}{\log_2(k + 1)}$$

Dividing actual $\text{DCG@}K$ by $\text{IDCG@}K$ yields Normalized Discounted Cumulative Gain ($\text{nDCG@}K$):

$$\text{nDCG@}K = \frac{\text{DCG@}K}{\text{IDCG@}K} \in [0, 1]$$

(By convention, if a query has no relevant items at all so $\text{IDCG@}K = 0$, $\text{nDCG@}K$ is either defined as $0$ or excluded from evaluation.)

4. User Browsing & Cascade Models: RBP and Expected Reciprocal Rank (ERR)

A subtle limitation of $\text{nDCG@}K$ and $\text{MAP@}K$ is that the position discount $1/\log_2(k+1)$ is static and independent of the items above rank $k$. In reality, if rank $k=1$ already contains a perfect grade-3 answer that completely satisfies the user's information need, the user stops scanning—so rank $k=2$ receives far less attention than if rank $k=1$ had been irrelevant!

4.1 Rank-Biased Precision (RBP)

Moffat and Zobel (2008) modeled a user with a geometric persistence parameter $p \in (0, 1)$: after inspecting rank $k$, the user continues to rank $k+1$ with probability $p$ and stops with probability $1 - p$. Since $\sum_{k=1}^{\infty} (1-p)p^{k-1} = 1$, Rank-Biased Precision (RBP) is the expected utility per inspected item:

$$\text{RBP}_p = (1 - p)\sum_{k=1}^{K} r_k\,p^{k-1}$$

Unlike $\text{nDCG@}K$ and $\text{AP@}K$, RBP does not require knowing the total number of unretrieved relevant documents $|\mathcal{R}_q|$ in the corpus, and the residual $(1-p)\sum_{k=K+1}^{\infty} p^{k-1} = p^K$ provides a tight upper bound on truncation uncertainty.

4.2 Expected Reciprocal Rank (ERR)

Chapelle et al. (2009) introduced Expected Reciprocal Rank (ERR) based on the cascade user model. Map each relevance grade $r_k \in \{0, 1, \dots, r_{\max}\}$ to a probability $R_k$ that item $d_k$ satisfies the user:

$$R_k = \mathcal{R}(r_k) = \frac{2^{r_k} - 1}{2^{r_{\max}}}$$

Under the cascade model, the user scans from $k=1$ downward and stops at the first rank $k$ that satisfies them. The probability that the user reaches rank $k$ and is satisfied at rank $k$ is $R_k \prod_{i=1}^{k-1} (1 - R_i)$. Weighting the stopping rank $k$ by its reciprocal $1/k$ gives the expected reciprocal stopping rank:

$$\text{ERR@}K = \sum_{k=1}^{K} \frac{1}{k} R_k \prod_{i=1}^{k-1} (1 - R_i)$$

Notice how $\text{ERR}$ generalizes $\text{MRR}$ to graded relevance: if relevance is binary with $R_k \in \{0, 1\}$, then $\prod_{i=1}^{k-1}(1-R_i)$ vanishes for all ranks after the first relevant hit $k_q^*$, reducing $\text{ERR}$ to $1/k_q^* = \text{RR}$.

5. Complete Worked Numerical Example Across All Ranking Metrics

Suppose a search engine returns a top-$5$ list $L_q(5) = (d_1, d_2, d_3, d_4, d_5)$ for query $q$ where the maximum relevance grade is $r_{\max} = 3$. The ground-truth relevance grades of the top-$5$ items are:

$$\mathbf{r}_{1:5} = [3,\; 0,\; 2,\; 1,\; 0]$$

Additionally, there is one unretrieved relevant item in the corpus at rank $k=8$ with relevance grade $r_8 = 2$, and all other catalog items have grade $0$. Thus, there are $|\mathcal{R}_q| = 4$ relevant items in total with multiset of grades $\{3, 2, 2, 1\}$.

  1. Binary Set Metrics at $K=5$ (treating $r_k \ge 1$ as $\text{rel}_k = 1$, so $\text{rel}_{1:5} = [1, 0, 1, 1, 0]$):
    $$\text{Precision@}5 = \frac{1 + 0 + 1 + 1 + 0}{5} = \frac{3}{5} = 0.6000$$ $$\text{Recall@}5 = \frac{3}{|\mathcal{R}_q|} = \frac{3}{4} = 0.7500, \qquad F_1\text{@}5 = \frac{2(0.60)(0.75)}{0.60 + 0.75} = \frac{2(3)}{5 + 4} = 0.6667, \qquad \text{HR@}5 = 1$$
  2. Reciprocal Rank ($\text{RR}$) and $\text{AP@}5$: The first relevant item is at $k_q^* = 1$, so $\text{RR} = 1/1 = 1.0000$. The relevant hits inside $1 \dots 5$ occur at $k \in \{1, 3, 4\}$ with precisions $\text{P@}1 = 1/1 = 1.0000$, $\text{P@}3 = 2/3 \approx 0.6667$, and $\text{P@}4 = 3/4 = 0.7500$. With $|\mathcal{R}_q| = 4 = \min(5, 4)$:
    $$\text{AP@}5 = \frac{1}{4}\left(1 + \frac{2}{3} + \frac{3}{4}\right) = \frac{1}{4}\left(\frac{29}{12}\right) = \frac{29}{48} \approx 0.6042$$
  3. $\text{DCG@}5$, $\text{IDCG@}5$, and $\text{nDCG@}5$: Using exponential gain $G(r_k) = 2^{r_k} - 1$, the actual gains are $[7, 0, 3, 1, 0]$:
    $$\text{DCG@}5 = \frac{7}{\log_2 2} + \frac{0}{\log_2 3} + \frac{3}{\log_2 4} + \frac{1}{\log_2 5} + \frac{0}{\log_2 6} = 7.0000 + 0 + 1.5000 + 0.4307 + 0 = 8.9307$$
    The ideal top-$5$ ordering pulls the missed grade-$2$ item from rank $8$ into the top-$5$, giving $\mathbf{r}_{\text{ideal}} = [3, 2, 2, 1, 0]$ with gains $[7, 3, 3, 1, 0]$:
    $$\text{IDCG@}5 = \frac{7}{1} + \frac{3}{\log_2 3} + \frac{3}{2} + \frac{1}{\log_2 5} + 0 = 7.0000 + 1.8928 + 1.5000 + 0.4307 = 10.8235$$ $$\text{nDCG@}5 = \frac{8.9307}{10.8235} \approx 0.8251$$
  4. Expected Reciprocal Rank ($\text{ERR@}5$): With $r_{\max} = 3$, $2^{r_{\max}} = 8$, so the satisfaction probabilities $R_k = (2^{r_k}-1)/8$ for $\mathbf{r}_{1:5} = [3, 0, 2, 1, 0]$ are:
    $$R_1 = \frac{7}{8} = 0.875, \quad R_2 = 0, \quad R_3 = \frac{3}{8} = 0.375, \quad R_4 = \frac{1}{8} = 0.125, \quad R_5 = 0$$
    Walking the cascade:
    • $k=1$: $\frac{1}{1} R_1 = 0.8750$, remaining unsatisfied mass $= 1 - 0.875 = 0.125$.
    • $k=2$: $R_2 = 0 \implies 0$, remaining mass $= 0.125$.
    • $k=3$: $\frac{1}{3} R_3 (0.125) = \frac{1}{3}(0.375)(0.125) = 0.015625$, remaining mass $= 0.125(1 - 0.375) = 0.078125$.
    • $k=4$: $\frac{1}{4} R_4 (0.078125) = \frac{1}{4}(0.125)(0.078125) \approx 0.002441$.
    Summing gives $\text{ERR@}5 = 0.8750 + 0 + 0.015625 + 0.002441 + 0 \approx 0.8931$. Notice how $\text{ERR@}5$ is dominated by the grade-$3$ item at $k=1$ because $87.5\%$ of users are already satisfied on the very first result!

6. Beyond-Accuracy Recommender Metrics: Diversity, Novelty, Serendipity, Coverage & Gini

In production recommender systems, optimizing exclusively for click-through accuracy ($\text{nDCG@}K$ or $\text{Recall@}K$) produces popularity bias, filter bubbles (recommending 10 near-identical sequels of the same movie), and supplier starvation in two-sided marketplaces. Modern evaluation suites pair ranking accuracy with five structural beyond-accuracy metrics.

6.1 Intra-List Diversity (ILD)

Given a pairwise distance or dissimilarity function $d(i, j) \in [0, 1]$ between item embeddings or genre vectors (commonly cosine distance $d(i,j) = 1 - \frac{\mathbf{v}_i^\top \mathbf{v}_j}{\|\mathbf{v}_i\|\|\mathbf{v}_j\|}$ or Jaccard distance), Intra-List Diversity (ILD) averages the dissimilarity across all $\binom{K}{2}$ pairs in the slate $L_u(K)$:

$$\text{ILD}\bigl(L_u(K)\bigr) = \frac{2}{K(K - 1)} \sum_{k=1}^{K-1}\sum_{j=k+1}^{K} d(d_k, d_j)$$

Re-ranking algorithms such as Maximal Marginal Relevance (MMR) and Determinantal Point Processes (DPP) explicitly trade off relevance score $s(u, i)$ against $\text{ILD}$ via $\arg\max_{i \in R \setminus S}\bigl[\lambda\,s(u,i) - (1-\lambda)\max_{j \in S}\text{sim}(i,j)\bigr]$.

6.2 Self-Information Novelty (Surprisal) & Expected Popularity Complement

Let $P(i) = \frac{\text{interactions}(i)}{\sum_{j \in \mathcal{I}} \text{interactions}(j)}$ (or fraction of users who interacted with item $i$) be the historical popularity probability of item $i$. Recommending a ubiquitous blockbuster carries almost zero information, whereas recommending a long-tail item carries high Shannon self-information $-\log_2 P(i)$. The Mean Self-Information Novelty of slate $L_u(K)$ is:

$$\text{Novelty}\bigl(L_u(K)\bigr) = \frac{1}{K}\sum_{i \in L_u(K)} \bigl(-\log_2 P(i)\bigr)$$

A bounded variant is Expected Popularity Complement (EPC), which weights long-tail rarity $(1 - P(i))$ by rank discount and relevance.

6.3 Serendipity (Unexpectedness $\times$ Relevance)

Novelty alone can be gamed by recommending random obscure items that the user hates. Serendipity measures items that are both unexpected (not surfaced by a naive popularity/content baseline $L_u^{\text{prim}}$ or dissimilar to the user's historical profile $\mathcal{H}_u$) and relevant:

$$\text{Serendipity}\bigl(L_u(K)\bigr) = \frac{1}{K}\sum_{i \in L_u(K)} \mathbb{I}\bigl[i \notin L_u^{\text{prim}}(K)\bigr] \cdot \text{rel}(u, i), \qquad \text{or} \qquad \frac{1}{K}\sum_{i \in L_u(K)} \left(\min_{h \in \mathcal{H}_u} d(i, h)\right) \cdot \text{rel}(u, i)$$

6.4 Catalog Coverage, Shannon Entropy, and the Gini Index

Across all users $\mathcal{U}$, let $c(i) = \sum_{u \in \mathcal{U}} \mathbb{I}[i \in L_u(K)]$ count how many times catalog item $i \in \mathcal{I}$ was recommended, and let $p_i = \frac{c(i)}{|\mathcal{U}|\cdot K}$ be its empirical exposure share (with $|\mathcal{I}| = M$ items sorted so $p_{(1)} \le p_{(2)} \le \dots \le p_{(M)}$):

$$\text{Catalog Coverage} = \frac{\bigl|\bigcup_{u \in \mathcal{U}} L_u(K)\bigr|}{|\mathcal{I}|} \in (0, 1]$$
$$\text{Gini}(\mathbf{p}) = \frac{1}{M - 1}\sum_{j=1}^{M} (2j - M - 1)\,p_{(j)} \in [0, 1]$$

When every item in the catalog receives equal exposure ($p_i = 1/M$), $\text{Gini} = 0$. When a tiny handful of superstar items monopolize all recommendation impressions across all users, $\text{Gini} \to 1$.

7. Reference Python Implementation

The self-contained implementation below computes Precision@$K$, Recall@$K$, MRR, AP@$K$, DCG@$K$, IDCG@$K$, nDCG@$K$, RBP, ERR@$K$, Intra-List Diversity, Self-Information Novelty, and the Catalog Gini Coefficient, and verifies every number from Section 5. 

import math
from typing import Sequence

def precision_at_k(rels: Sequence[int], k: int) -> float:
    return sum(1 for r in rels[:k] if r > 0) / k

def recall_at_k(rels: Sequence[int], k: int, total_relevant: int) -> float:
    if total_relevant == 0:
        return 0.0
    return sum(1 for r in rels[:k] if r > 0) / total_relevant

def reciprocal_rank(rels: Sequence[int], k: int) -> float:
    for idx, r in enumerate(rels[:k], start=1):
        if r > 0:
            return 1.0 / idx
    return 0.0

def average_precision_at_k(rels: Sequence[int], k: int, total_relevant: int) -> float:
    if total_relevant == 0:
        return 0.0
    hits = 0
    sum_precisions = 0.0
    for idx, r in enumerate(rels[:k], start=1):
        if r > 0:
            hits += 1
            sum_precisions += hits / idx
    return sum_precisions / min(k, total_relevant)

def dcg_at_k(rels: Sequence[float], k: int, exponential: bool = True) -> float:
    total = 0.0
    for idx, r in enumerate(rels[:k], start=1):
        gain = (2.0 ** r - 1.0) if exponential else float(r)
        total += gain / math.log2(idx + 1.0)
    return total

def ndcg_at_k(rels: Sequence[float], all_corpus_rels: Sequence[float], k: int) -> float:
    actual = dcg_at_k(rels, k, exponential=True)
    ideal_sorted = sorted(all_corpus_rels, reverse=True)
    ideal = dcg_at_k(ideal_sorted, k, exponential=True)
    return actual / ideal if ideal > 0 else 0.0

def err_at_k(rels: Sequence[float], k: int, r_max: float = 3.0) -> float:
    p_unsatisfied = 1.0
    err = 0.0
    denom = 2.0 ** r_max
    for idx, r in enumerate(rels[:k], start=1):
        r_prob = (2.0 ** r - 1.0) / denom
        err += (1.0 / idx) * r_prob * p_unsatisfied
        p_unsatisfied *= (1.0 - r_prob)
    return err

def rbp_at_k(rels: Sequence[float], k: int, p: float = 0.8, r_max: float = 3.0) -> float:
    return (1.0 - p) * sum((r / r_max) * (p ** idx) for idx, r in enumerate(rels[:k]))

def gini_index(exposure_counts: Sequence[float]) -> float:
    m = len(exposure_counts)
    total = sum(exposure_counts)
    if m <= 1 or total == 0:
        return 0.0
    p_sorted = [c / total for c in sorted(exposure_counts)]
    return sum((2 * (j + 1) - m - 1) * p_j for j, p_j in enumerate(p_sorted)) / (m - 1)

if __name__ == "__main__":
    # Worked example from Section 5:
    top5_grades = [3, 0, 2, 1, 0]
    all_grades = [3, 0, 2, 1, 0, 0, 0, 2]  # includes grade-2 item at rank 8
    print(f"P@5      = {precision_at_k(top5_grades, 5):.4f}")
    print(f"Recall@5 = {recall_at_k(top5_grades, 5, total_relevant=4):.4f}")
    print(f"RR@5     = {reciprocal_rank(top5_grades, 5):.4f}")
    print(f"AP@5     = {average_precision_at_k(top5_grades, 5, total_relevant=4):.4f}")
    print(f"DCG@5    = {dcg_at_k(top5_grades, 5):.4f}")
    print(f"IDCG@5   = {dcg_at_k(sorted(all_grades, reverse=True), 5):.4f}")
    print(f"nDCG@5   = {ndcg_at_k(top5_grades, all_grades, 5):.4f}")
    print(f"ERR@5    = {err_at_k(top5_grades, 5, r_max=3.0):.4f}")

8. Self-Check Questions & Summary

Question 1: Why does two-stage retrieval use Recall@100 for Stage 1 and nDCG@10 for Stage 2?

Answer: In a modern two-stage architecture, the Stage-1 bi-encoder / ANN retriever narrows millions of catalog items down to $K=100$ candidates that are passed as an unordered candidate set to the Stage-2 cross-encoder reranker. Because Stage 2 will completely re-score and re-order those 100 items anyway, the internal ordering within the top-100 of Stage 1 does not matter—only whether the true relevant items survived the funnel ($\text{Recall@}100$). Conversely, Stage 2 produces the final user-facing slate where attention decays steeply with position, making $\text{nDCG@}10$ or $\text{ERR@}10$ the appropriate metric.

Question 2: Can swapping two items at ranks $k=2$ and $k=3$ change $\text{nDCG@}5$ while leaving $\text{MRR@}5$ unchanged?

Answer: Yes! If rank $k=1$ already holds a relevant item, $\text{MRR@}5 = 1/1 = 1.0$ regardless of how ranks $2 \dots 5$ are permuted. In contrast, $\text{nDCG@}5$ weights rank $2$ by $1/\log_2(3) \approx 0.6309$ and rank $3$ by $1/\log_2(4) = 0.5000$, so swapping a grade-2 item at $k=3$ with a grade-0 item at $k=2$ increases $\text{DCG@}5$ by $3(0.6309 - 0.5000) = +0.3928$.

Question 3: Why must $\text{IDCG@}K$ be computed over all relevant items in the corpus rather than only the items retrieved inside the model's top-$K$?

Answer: If $\text{IDCG@}K$ were computed only by sorting the model's own top-$K$ predictions, then a model that retrieved a single grade-1 item at rank $1$ and four grade-0 items at ranks $2 \dots 5$ (while missing ten grade-3 items in the corpus!) would achieve $\text{DCG@}5 = 1.0$ and $\text{IDCG}_{\text{wrong}}\text{@}5 = 1.0$, falsely scoring $\text{nDCG@}5 = 1.00$! Computing $\text{IDCG@}K$ over the entire ground-truth corpus ensures $\text{nDCG@}K$ penalizes missing high-grade items.
