    Classical NLP & Text Generation Metrics: Perplexity, Bits-Per-Byte (BPB), BLEU, ROUGE, METEOR, chrF++, WER/CER, and BERTScore

    How do we quantify the quality of a language model or sequence-to-sequence generator? We trace the progression from information-theoretic likelihood metrics (Perplexity and tokenizer-invariant Bits-Per-Byte), to lexical $n$-gram overlap and edit-distance metrics (BLEU with clipped precision and brevity penalty, ROUGE-N/L/W, METEOR, chrF++, CIDEr, and Levenshtein WER/CER), to contextual embedding alignment (BERTScore).

    1. Likelihood & Information Theory: Cross-Entropy, Perplexity (PPL), and Bits-Per-Byte (BPB)

    1.1 Autoregressive Cross-Entropy and Perplexity

    By the chain rule of probability, an autoregressive language model $p_\theta$ factorizes the joint probability of a token sequence $\mathbf{w}_{1:T} = (w_1, w_2, \dots, w_T)$ over vocabulary $\mathcal{V}$ as:

    1.2 Why Perplexity Breaks Across Tokenizers — and How Bits-Per-Byte (BPB) Fixes It

    The Tokenizer Trap: Suppose Model A uses a $32\text{k}$ BPE vocabulary that splits a $1000$-byte paragraph into $T_A = 250$ tokens with average loss $\mathcal{L}_A = 2.40\text{ nats/token}$ ($\text{PPL}_A = e^{2.40} = 11.02$), while Model B uses a $128\text{k}$ vocabulary that compresses the exact same paragraph into $T_B = 180$ longer tokens with average loss $\mathcal{L}_B = 3.10\text{ nats/token}$ ($\text{PPL}_B = e^{3.10} = 22.20$). Looking at $\text{PPL}$ makes Model A look twice as good! Yet the total negative log-likelihood assigned to the entire paragraph is $250 \times 2.40 = 600\text{ nats}$ for Model A vs. $180 \times 3.10 = 558\text{ nats}$ for Model B—Model B actually assigned a $e^{42} \approx 1.7\times 10^{18}$ times higher probability to the text!

    To compare language models across different tokenizers, vocabularies, or byte/character granularities, we normalize the total negative log-likelihood in base-$2$ bits by the raw UTF-8 byte length $N_{\text{bytes}}$ (or character length $N_{\text{chars}}$ for Bits-Per-Character, BPC):

    $$\text{BPB} = \frac{-\sum_{t=1}^{T} \log_2 p_\theta(w_t \mid w_{

    In our example above, Model A achieves $\text{BPB}_A = \frac{600}{1000 \ln 2} = 0.8656\text{ bits/byte}$, whereas Model B achieves $\text{BPB}_B = \frac{558}{1000 \ln 2} = 0.8050\text{ bits/byte}$ ($\downarrow$ lower is better), rightly identifying Model B as the superior probabilistic model.

    2. Machine Translation & Precision Overlap: BLEU, Clipped $n$-Grams, and Brevity Penalty

    Papineni et al. (2002) introduced BLEU (Bilingual Evaluation Understudy) to evaluate machine translation candidates $C$ against one or more human reference translations $\{R_1, \dots, R_M\}$.

    2.1 Modified Clipped $n$-Gram Precision ($p_n$)

    Why not just use standard unigram precision? Consider the degenerate candidate $C = \text{"the the the the the the the"}$ against reference $R_1 = \text{"the cat is on the mat"}$. Because `"the"` appears in $R_1$, naive unigram precision is $7/7 = 1.00$! BLEU prevents repetition gaming by clipping the count of each unique $n$-gram $g$ in $C$ at the maximum number of times $g$ appears in any single reference translation:

    $$\text{Count}_{\text{clip}}(g) = \min\!\Bigl(\text{Count}_C(g),\; \max_{m \in \{1,\dots,M\}} \text{Count}_{R_m}(g)\Bigr)$$
    $$p_n = \frac{\sum_{g \in \text{ngrams}_n(C)} \text{Count}_{\text{clip}}(g)}{\sum_{g \in \text{ngrams}_n(C)} \text{Count}_C(g)}$$

    Here $p_1$ measures lexical adequacy (word choice), while higher-order $p_2, p_3, p_4$ measure fluency and local word order.

    2.2 Brevity Penalty (BP) and Geometric Mean BLEU-$N$

    Because $p_n$ is purely a precision metric, a candidate consisting of just a single word $C = \text{"the"}$ achieves $p_1 = 1/1 = 1.0$. Rather than computing $n$-gram recall directly (which becomes ambiguous when multiple references paraphrase different clauses), BLEU multiplies by an exponential Brevity Penalty ($\text{BP}$) whenever the candidate length $c = |C|$ is shorter than the effective reference length $r$:

    $$\text{BP} = \begin{cases} 1 & \text{if } c > r, \\ \exp\!\left(1 - \frac{r}{c}\right) & \text{if } c \le r. \end{cases}$$

    Combining $\text{BP}$ with the weighted geometric mean of $p_1, \dots, p_N$ (conventionally $N=4$ with uniform weights $w_n = 1/4$) gives BLEU-$4$:

    $$\text{BLEU-}N = \text{BP} \cdot \exp\!\left(\sum_{n=1}^{N} w_n \ln p_n\right) = \text{BP} \cdot \left(\prod_{n=1}^{N} p_n^{w_n}\right)$$

    Important Practical Nuance: Because BLEU uses a geometric mean, if any $p_n = 0$ (e.g., no 4-gram match in a short single sentence), the entire raw sentence-BLEU collapses to $0$! Standard BLEU is defined at the corpus level (summing clipped counts and lengths across all sentences before dividing), whereas sentence-level BLEU requires add-$\epsilon$ or Chen & Cherry smoothing. Furthermore, because tokenization preprocessing changes BLEU scores by several points, modern benchmarks mandate SacreBLEU (Post 2018) with standardized internal tokenization.

    3. Summarization & Morphologically Rich Overlap: ROUGE-N/L, METEOR, chrF++, and CIDEr

    3.1 ROUGE-N and ROUGE-L (Longest Common Subsequence)

    Lin (2004) designed ROUGE (Recall-Oriented Understudy for Gisting Evaluation) for summarization, where ensuring that key facts from the reference summary $R$ are not omitted (recall) is just as important as precision:

    • ROUGE-$N$: Computes $n$-gram recall, precision, and $F_1$ between reference $R$ and candidate $C$:
      $$\text{ROUGE-}N_{\text{recall}} = \frac{\sum_{g \in \text{ngrams}_n(R)} \min\bigl(\text{Count}_C(g), \text{Count}_R(g)\bigr)}{\sum_{g \in \text{ngrams}_n(R)} \text{Count}_R(g)}$$
    • ROUGE-L (Longest Common Subsequence): Instead of contiguous $n$-grams, let $\text{LCS}(R, C)$ be the length of the longest in-order (but not necessarily contiguous!) sequence of words shared by $R$ and $C$, computed in $\mathcal{O}(|R|\cdot|C|)$ via dynamic programming:
      $$R_{\text{lcs}} = \frac{\text{LCS}(R, C)}{|R|}, \qquad P_{\text{lcs}} = \frac{\text{LCS}(R, C)}{|C|}, \qquad \text{ROUGE-L} = \frac{(1 + \beta^2) R_{\text{lcs}} P_{\text{lcs}}}{R_{\text{lcs}} + \beta^2 P_{\text{lcs}}}$$

    3.2 METEOR, chrF++, and CIDEr

    • METEOR (Banerjee & Lavie 2005): Aligns words via exact match, Porter stemming, and WordNet synonyms, computes a recall-heavy harmonic mean $F_{\text{mean}} = \frac{10 P R}{R + 9 P}$ ($\beta = 3$), and multiplies by $(1 - \text{Pen})$ where $\text{Pen} = \gamma\left(\frac{\text{chunks}}{\text{unigrams\_matched}}\right)^\theta$ penalizes non-contiguous word-order fragmentation.
    • chrF and chrF++ (Popović 2015, 2017): Computes the $F_\beta$ score ($\beta = 2$) of character $n$-grams ($n=1\dots 6$) plus word $1,2$-grams, providing dramatically higher correlation with human judgments on morphologically rich languages (German, Turkish, Russian, Czech) and code tokens where subword inflections break word-level BLEU.
    • CIDEr (Vedantam et al. 2015): Standard in image captioning; weights each $n$-gram by its Inverse Document Frequency ($\text{TF-IDF}$) across the dataset so rare descriptive nouns/verbs ("skateboard", "ollie") carry far more weight than boilerplate phrases ("there is a").

    4. Speech Recognition & OCR Edit Distance: Levenshtein WER and CER

    In Automatic Speech Recognition (ASR) and Optical Character Recognition (OCR), word order is strictly monotonic. Given a reference sequence of $N_{\text{ref}}$ words (or characters) and a hypothesis sequence, dynamic programming finds the minimum number of Substitutions ($S$), Deletions ($D$), and Insertions ($I$) required to transform the reference into the hypothesis:

    $$\text{WER} = \frac{S + D + I}{N_{\text{ref}}}, \qquad \text{CER} = \frac{S_{\text{char}} + D_{\text{char}} + I_{\text{char}}}{N_{\text{ref, char}}}$$

    Note that while $S + D \le N_{\text{ref}}$, the number of insertions $I$ is unbounded—so if a speech model hallucinates a long loop of repeated words during silence, $\text{WER}$ can easily exceed $1.0$ ($100\%$). When a bounded $[0, 1]$ metric is needed, Match Error Rate ($\text{MER}$) divides by $H + S + D + I = \text{total aligned operations}$.

    5. Contextual Semantic Similarity: BERTScore and Baseline Rescaling

    Every surface lexical metric ($\text{BLEU}$, $\text{ROUGE}$, $\text{WER}$) suffers from the synonymy blind spot: if the reference says "icy wind howled" and the candidate says "freezing breeze roared", $\text{BLEU} = 0$ and $\text{ROUGE} = 0$ despite near-identical meaning. Conversely, negating a single word ("is safe" vs. "is not safe") yields high $n$-gram overlap despite opposite meaning.

    Zhang et al. (2020) introduced BERTScore, which passes both the reference tokens $x = (x_1, \dots, x_m)$ and candidate tokens $\hat{x} = (\hat{x}_1, \dots, \hat{x}_k)$ through a pretrained bidirectional Transformer encoder to obtain unit-normalized contextual embeddings $\hat{\mathbf{e}}_{x_i}, \hat{\mathbf{e}}_{\hat{x}_j} \in \mathbb{S}^{d-1}$. Each reference token greedily matches to its highest-cosine candidate token (and vice versa), weighted by corpus Inverse Document Frequency $\text{idf}(w) = -\log\frac{\text{df}(w)+1}{M+1}$:

    $$R_{\text{BERT}} = \frac{\sum_{i=1}^{m} \text{idf}(x_i) \max_{j \in \{1,\dots,k\}} \hat{\mathbf{e}}_{x_i}^\top \hat{\mathbf{e}}_{\hat{x}_j}}{\sum_{i=1}^{m} \text{idf}(x_i)}, \qquad P_{\text{BERT}} = \frac{\sum_{j=1}^{k} \text{idf}(\hat{x}_j) \max_{i \in \{1,\dots,m\}} \hat{\mathbf{e}}_{x_i}^\top \hat{\mathbf{e}}_{\hat{x}_j}}{\sum_{j=1}^{k} \text{idf}(\hat{x}_j)}$$
    $$F_{\text{BERT}} = \frac{2\,P_{\text{BERT}}\,R_{\text{BERT}}}{P_{\text{BERT}} + R_{\text{BERT}}}$$

    Because anisotropic Transformer embeddings often have a high empirical lower bound $b \approx 0.80$ even on random unrelated sentence pairs, BERTScore applies linear baseline rescaling $\widehat{F}_{\text{BERT}} = \frac{F_{\text{BERT}} - b}{1 - b}$ so human-interpretable scores span $[0, 1]$.

    6. Complete Worked Numerical Examples

    6.1 Worked BLEU-2, ROUGE-L, and WER

    Consider Reference $R = \text{"the cat sat on the mat"}$ ($r = 6$ words) and Candidate $C = \text{"the the cat on mat"}$ ($c = 5$ words):

    1. Clipped Unigram Precision ($p_1$): Candidate unigram counts: $\text{Count}_C(\text{"the"}) = 2, \text{Count}_C(\text{"cat"}) = 1, \text{Count}_C(\text{"on"}) = 1, \text{Count}_C(\text{"mat"}) = 1$. Since `"the"` appears twice in $R$, $\text{Count}_{\text{clip}}(\text{"the"}) = \min(2, 2) = 2$. All other words match once, giving $p_1 = \frac{2+1+1+1}{5} = \frac{5}{5} = 1.0000$.
    2. Clipped Bigram Precision ($p_2$): Candidate bigrams ($4$ total): `("the","the")` (count $0$ in $R$), `("the","cat")` (count $1$ in $R$), `("cat","on")` (count $0$ in $R$), `("on","mat")` (count $0$ in $R$). Thus $p_2 = \frac{1}{4} = 0.2500$.
    3. Brevity Penalty ($\text{BP}$) and $\text{BLEU-2}$: Since $c = 5 \le r = 6$, $\text{BP} = \exp(1 - 6/5) = \exp(-0.2) \approx 0.8187$. Thus:
      $$\text{BLEU-2} = 0.8187 \times \sqrt{1.0000 \times 0.2500} = 0.8187 \times 0.5000 \approx 0.4094$$
    4. ROUGE-L: The Longest Common Subsequence between $R$ and $C$ is `("the", "cat", "on", "mat")` of length $\text{LCS} = 4$. Thus:
      $$R_{\text{lcs}} = \frac{4}{6} = 0.6667, \qquad P_{\text{lcs}} = \frac{4}{5} = 0.8000, \qquad F_{1,\text{lcs}} = \frac{2(4)}{6 + 5} = \frac{8}{11} \approx 0.7273$$
    5. Word Error Rate ($\text{WER}$): Transforming $R$ (`the cat sat on the mat`) into $C$ (`the the cat on mat`) requires $1$ Insertion (`the`), $2$ Deletions (`sat`, `the`), and $0$ Substitutions (or $1$ Substitution + $1$ Deletion = $2$ edits total: substitute `sat`$\to$`cat`, delete `cat`? Let's check exact DP: aligning `the [ins:the] cat [del:sat] on [del:the] mat` takes $3$ edits, whereas aligning `the [sub:cat->the] [sub:sat->cat] on [del:the] mat` also takes $3$ edits!). Wait: since $\text{LCS}(R, C) = 4$, in $R$ of length $6$ and $C$ of length $5$, replacing `cat sat` (2 words) with `the cat` (1 substitution + 0? No: `the` matches `the`, then `cat` matches `cat`, leaving `sat` and `the` to delete and one `the` to insert, or substituting `sat`? No, `cat` is before `sat` in $R$!). Thus the exact Levenshtein distance is $S + D + I = 3$, giving:
      $$\text{WER} = \frac{3}{6} = 0.5000 \quad (50.0\%)$$

    7. Reference Python Implementation

    import math
from collections import Counter
from typing import Sequence, Tuple

def perplexity_and_bpb(token_logprobs_nat: Sequence[float], num_utf8_bytes: int) -> Tuple[float, float]:
    t = len(token_logprobs_nat)
    ce_nat = -sum(token_logprobs_nat) / t
    ppl = math.exp(ce_nat)
    total_bits = -sum(lp / math.log(2.0) for lp in token_logprobs_nat)
    bpb = total_bits / num_utf8_bytes
    return ppl, bpb

def clipped_ngram_precision(cand: Sequence[str], ref: Sequence[str], n: int) -> float:
    cand_ngrams = [tuple(cand[i:i + n]) for i in range(len(cand) - n + 1)]
    ref_ngrams = [tuple(ref[i:i + n]) for i in range(len(ref) - n + 1)]
    if not cand_ngrams:
        return 0.0
    c_counts = Counter(cand_ngrams)
    r_counts = Counter(ref_ngrams)
    clipped = sum(min(cnt, r_counts[g]) for g, cnt in c_counts.items())
    return clipped / len(cand_ngrams)

def bleu_n(cand: Sequence[str], ref: Sequence[str], max_n: int = 2) -> float:
    c, r = len(cand), len(ref)
    if c == 0:
        return 0.0
    bp = 1.0 if c > r else math.exp(1.0 - r / c)
    precisions = [clipped_ngram_precision(cand, ref, n) for n in range(1, max_n + 1)]
    if any(p == 0 for p in precisions):
        return 0.0
    geo_mean = math.exp(sum(math.log(p) for p in precisions) / max_n)
    return bp * geo_mean

def lcs_length(a: Sequence[str], b: Sequence[str]) -> int:
    dp = [[0] * (len(b) + 1) for _ in range(len(a) + 1)]
    for i in range(1, len(a) + 1):
        for j in range(1, len(b) + 1):
            if a[i - 1] == b[j - 1]:
                dp[i][j] = dp[i - 1][j - 1] + 1
            else:
                dp[i][j] = max(dp[i - 1][j], dp[i][j - 1])
    return dp[-1][-1]

def rouge_l_f1(cand: Sequence[str], ref: Sequence[str]) -> float:
    lcs = lcs_length(ref, cand)
    return (2.0 * lcs) / (len(ref) + len(cand)) if (len(ref) + len(cand)) > 0 else 0.0

def word_error_rate(cand: Sequence[str], ref: Sequence[str]) -> float:
    dp = [[0] * (len(cand) + 1) for _ in range(len(ref) + 1)]
    for i in range(len(ref) + 1):
        dp[i][0] = i
    for j in range(len(cand) + 1):
        dp[0][j] = j
    for i in range(1, len(ref) + 1):
        for j in range(1, len(cand) + 1):
            cost = 0 if ref[i - 1] == cand[j - 1] else 1
            dp[i][j] = min(dp[i - 1][j] + 1, dp[i][j - 1] + 1, dp[i - 1][j - 1] + cost)
    return dp[-1][-1] / len(ref)

    ref = "the cat sat on the mat".split()
    cand = "the the cat on mat".split()
    print(f"BLEU-2     = {bleu_n(cand, ref, max_n=2):.4f}")
    print(f"ROUGE-L F1 = {rouge_l_f1(cand, ref):.4f}")
    print(f"WER        = {word_error_rate(cand, ref):.4f}")

    8. Self-Check Questions & Summary

    Question 1: Why does expanding a BPE tokenizer's vocabulary size from $32\text{k}$ to $128\text{k}$ almost always increase token-level Perplexity even when the model improves?

    Answer: With a $128\text{k}$ vocabulary, common multi-character words and morphemes are merged into single tokens. Predicting a whole word in a single softmax step over $128{,}000$ classes is harder per token than predicting 2–3 subword pieces over $32{,}000$ classes (where the second and third subword pieces have very low conditional entropy once the prefix is known). Normalizing total log-likelihood by UTF-8 byte length ($\text{BPB}$) eliminates this artifact.

    Question 2: Why is BLEU asymmetric between candidate $C$ and reference $R$, and why does it use a Brevity Penalty instead of $n$-gram recall?

    Answer: In machine translation benchmarks, a single source sentence often has $M = 4$ diverse reference translations using different synonyms and sentence structures. If a candidate $C$ used $n$-gram recall against the union of all 4 references, a good translation would be penalized for not using all 4 synonym choices simultaneously! Therefore, BLEU measures clipped precision against the references' max counts and uses the scalar Brevity Penalty $\exp(1 - r/c)$ as a recall proxy to prevent overly short translations.
