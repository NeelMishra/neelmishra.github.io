    4. Output Layer & Loss Gradients: MSE, Sigmoid + BCE, and the Softmax + Cross-Entropy Jacobian

    October 2026 · 17 min read

    Every backward pass begins at the final layer $\ell = L$ with the initial error sensitivity $\delta^{(L)} \triangleq \frac{\partial \mathcal{L}}{\partial z^{(L)}} \in \mathbb{R}^{d_L}$ (Equation BP1). Remarkably, whether we train a regression model with Linear + Mean Squared Error, a binary classifier with Sigmoid + Binary Cross-Entropy, or a $C$-way classifier with Softmax + Categorical Cross-Entropy, the output sensitivity collapses to the exact same residual vector $\delta^{(L)} = \hat{y} - y$. Here we derive all three backward Jacobians from first principles—including the full $C \times C$ Softmax Jacobian matrix—and prove why exponential-family generalized linear links guarantee this cancellation. (For forward-pass numerical stability and log-sum-exp tricks, see our companion Numerically Stable Sigmoid and Softmax & Temperature notes.)

    1. Identity output + Mean Squared Error (MSE)

    For continuous regression on $y \in \mathbb{R}^{d_L}$, the final layer applies the identity map $a_i^{(L)} = \hat{y}_i = z_i^{(L)}$ and minimizes the half squared Euclidean error:

    $$\mathcal{L}_{\text{MSE}}(\hat{y}, y) = \frac{1}{2}\|\hat{y} - y\|_2^2 = \frac{1}{2}\sum_{k=1}^{d_L} \bigl(\hat{y}_k - y_k\bigr)^2.$$

    Differentiating with respect to output coordinate $\hat{y}_i = a_i^{(L)}$ affects only the $k=i$ term in the sum:

    $$\frac{\partial \mathcal{L}_{\text{MSE}}}{\partial a_i^{(L)}} = \frac{1}{2} \cdot 2\bigl(a_i^{(L)} - y_i\bigr) = \hat{y}_i - y_i, \qquad \phi_L'\!\bigl(z_i^{(L)}\bigr) = \frac{d}{dz_i^{(L)}}\bigl(z_i^{(L)}\bigr) = 1.$$

    Multiplying the two factors via BP1 gives:

    $$\boxed{\delta_i^{(L)} = \frac{\partial \mathcal{L}_{\text{MSE}}}{\partial z_i^{(L)}} = \hat{y}_i - y_i \quad \Longleftrightarrow \quad \delta^{(L)} = \hat{y} - y \in \mathbb{R}^{d_L}.}$$

    Note on scaling conventions: If MSE is defined without the $\frac{1}{2}$ factor and averaged over $d_L$ outputs as $\mathcal{L} = \frac{1}{d_L}\sum_{k=1}^{d_L}(\hat{y}_k - y_k)^2$, then $\delta^{(L)} = \frac{2}{d_L}(\hat{y} - y)$.

    2. Logistic Sigmoid + Binary Cross-Entropy (BCE) and singularity cancellation

    For binary classification ($d_L = 1$, $y \in \{0, 1\}$) or multi-label classification ($y \in \{0, 1\}^{d_L}$), each output unit applies the logistic sigmoid $\hat{p}_i = a_i^{(L)} = \sigma\bigl(z_i^{(L)}\bigr) = \frac{1}{1 + e^{-z_i^{(L)}}}$ and minimizes the Binary Cross-Entropy loss:

    $$\mathcal{L}_{\text{BCE}}(\hat{p}, y) = -\sum_{k=1}^{d_L} \Bigl[y_k \log \hat{p}_k + (1 - y_k)\log(1 - \hat{p}_k)\Bigr].$$

    Let us compute the two factors of BP1 ($\delta_i^{(L)} = \frac{\partial \mathcal{L}}{\partial \hat{p}_i} \frac{\partial \hat{p}_i}{\partial z_i^{(L)}}$) separately and watch what happens when they meet:

    1. Derivative of BCE loss with respect to probability $\hat{p}_i$: $$\frac{\partial \mathcal{L}_{\text{BCE}}}{\partial \hat{p}_i} = -\left(\frac{y_i}{\hat{p}_i} - \frac{1 - y_i}{1 - \hat{p}_i}\right) = -\frac{y_i(1 - \hat{p}_i) - (1 - y_i)\hat{p}_i}{\hat{p}_i(1 - \hat{p}_i)} = \frac{\hat{p}_i - y_i}{\hat{p}_i(1 - \hat{p}_i)}.$$ Notice that if we computed $\frac{\partial \mathcal{L}_{\text{BCE}}}{\partial \hat{p}_i}$ in isolation in floating-point arithmetic, the denominator $\hat{p}_i(1-\hat{p}_i)$ would divide by zero whenever the model is confident ($\hat{p}_i \to 0$ or $\hat{p}_i \to 1$), causing `NaN` or `Inf`!
    2. Derivative of the logistic sigmoid $\hat{p}_i = \sigma\bigl(z_i^{(L)}\bigr)$ with respect to logit $z_i^{(L)}$: $$\frac{\partial \hat{p}_i}{\partial z_i^{(L)}} = \frac{d}{dz}\left(1 + e^{-z}\right)^{-1}\Big|_{z=z_i^{(L)}} = \frac{e^{-z_i^{(L)}}}{\bigl(1 + e^{-z_i^{(L)}}\bigr)^2} = \underbrace{\frac{1}{1 + e^{-z_i^{(L)}}}}_{\hat{p}_i} \cdot \underbrace{\frac{1 + e^{-z_i^{(L)}} - 1}{1 + e^{-z_i^{(L)}}}}_{1 - \hat{p}_i} = \hat{p}_i(1 - \hat{p}_i).$$
    3. Multiplying the two factors together (BP1): $$\delta_i^{(L)} = \frac{\partial \mathcal{L}_{\text{BCE}}}{\partial \hat{p}_i} \cdot \frac{\partial \hat{p}_i}{\partial z_i^{(L)}} = \frac{\hat{p}_i - y_i}{\cancel{\hat{p}_i(1 - \hat{p}_i)}} \cdot \cancel{\hat{p}_i(1 - \hat{p}_i)} = \hat{p}_i - y_i.$$
    $$\boxed{\delta_i^{(L)} = \frac{\partial \mathcal{L}_{\text{BCE}}}{\partial z_i^{(L)}} = \hat{p}_i - y_i = \sigma\!\bigl(z_i^{(L)}\bigr) - y_i \quad \Longleftrightarrow \quad \delta^{(L)} = \hat{p} - y \in \mathbb{R}^{d_L}.}$$
    Why Sigmoid + MSE stalls on wrong confident predictions while Sigmoid + BCE learns linearly:

    Suppose the true label is $y = 1$ but the initial logit is $z^{(L)} = -10$, so $\hat{p} = \sigma(-10) \approx 4.5 \times 10^{-5}$ (completely wrong and saturated). Under Sigmoid + MSE ($\mathcal{L} = \frac{1}{2}(\hat{p}-y)^2$), the logit gradient is $\delta^{(L)} = (\hat{p}-y)\hat{p}(1-\hat{p}) \approx -4.5 \times 10^{-5}$—vanishingly small because $\sigma'(-10)$ multiplies the error! Under Sigmoid + BCE, the $\hat{p}(1-\hat{p})$ factor cancels the logarithm derivative, yielding $\delta^{(L)} = \hat{p} - y \approx -1.0$: a strong, un-attenuated gradient proportional to the prediction error.

    3. Complete derivation of the $C \times C$ Softmax Jacobian and Categorical Cross-Entropy gradient

    For mutually exclusive $C$-class classification ($d_L = C$), the output head is the coupled vector-valued Softmax transformation mapping logits $z = z^{(L)} \in \mathbb{R}^C$ to the probability simplex $\Delta^{C-1}$:

    $$\hat{p}_k = a_k^{(L)} = \operatorname{Softmax}_k(z) = \frac{e^{z_k}}{\sum_{m=1}^C e^{z_m}} = \frac{e^{z_k}}{S(z)}, \qquad \text{where } S(z) \triangleq \sum_{m=1}^C e^{z_m}, \quad k \in \{1, \dots, C\}.$$

    Unlike element-wise activations where $\frac{\partial a_k^{(L)}}{\partial z_i^{(L)}} = 0$ for $k \neq i$, changing a single logit $z_i$ changes the partition denominator $S(z) = \sum_{m=1}^C e^{z_m}$ and therefore changes all $C$ output probabilities $\hat{p}_1, \dots, \hat{p}_C$ simultaneously! Consequently, the Jacobian of Softmax is a dense $C \times C$ matrix $J_{\operatorname{Softmax}}(z)$ with entries $\frac{\partial \hat{p}_k}{\partial z_i}$.

    Step 3a: Deriving the Softmax Jacobian $\frac{\partial \hat{p}_k}{\partial z_i}$ for diagonal ($k = i$) and off-diagonal ($k \neq i$) entries

    Note first that $\frac{\partial S(z)}{\partial z_i} = \frac{\partial}{\partial z_i} \sum_{m=1}^C e^{z_m} = e^{z_i}$. Now evaluate $\frac{\partial \hat{p}_k}{\partial z_i} = \frac{\partial}{\partial z_i}\!\left(\frac{e^{z_k}}{S(z)}\right)$ by splitting into two cases:

    • Case 1: Diagonal entry ($k = i$). Both numerator $e^{z_i}$ and denominator $S(z)$ depend on $z_i$. By the quotient rule: $$\frac{\partial \hat{p}_i}{\partial z_i} = \frac{\bigl(\frac{\partial}{\partial z_i} e^{z_i}\bigr) S(z) - e^{z_i} \bigl(\frac{\partial}{\partial z_i} S(z)\bigr)}{S(z)^2} = \frac{e^{z_i} S(z) - e^{z_i} e^{z_i}}{S(z)^2} = \frac{e^{z_i}}{S(z)} - \left(\frac{e^{z_i}}{S(z)}\right)^2 = \hat{p}_i - \hat{p}_i^2 = \hat{p}_i(1 - \hat{p}_i).$$
    • Case 2: Off-diagonal entry ($k \neq i$). The numerator $e^{z_k}$ is constant with respect to $z_i$ ($\frac{\partial e^{z_k}}{\partial z_i} = 0$), so only the denominator $S(z)$ contributes: $$\frac{\partial \hat{p}_k}{\partial z_i} = e^{z_k} \frac{\partial}{\partial z_i}\Bigl(S(z)^{-1}\Bigr) = -\frac{e^{z_k} \, e^{z_i}}{S(z)^2} = -\left(\frac{e^{z_k}}{S(z)}\right)\left(\frac{e^{z_i}}{S(z)}\right) = -\hat{p}_k \hat{p}_i.$$ (Intuitively: increasing logit $z_i$ increases $\hat{p}_i$ while stealing probability mass $-\hat{p}_k \hat{p}_i$ from every competing class $k \neq i$.)

    Using the Kronecker delta $\delta_{ki} = 1$ if $k=i$ and $0$ if $k \neq i$, both cases unify into one formula—and in matrix notation into a diagonal matrix minus a rank-1 outer product:

    $$\boxed{\frac{\partial \hat{p}_k}{\partial z_i} = \hat{p}_k\bigl(\delta_{ki} - \hat{p}_i\bigr) \quad \Longleftrightarrow \quad J_{\operatorname{Softmax}}(z) = \frac{\partial \hat{p}}{\partial z} = \operatorname{diag}(\hat{p}) - \hat{p}\hat{p}^\top \in \mathbb{R}^{C \times C}.}$$

    Notice two immediate structural properties of $J_{\operatorname{Softmax}}(z) = \operatorname{diag}(\hat{p}) - \hat{p}\hat{p}^\top$:

    1. It is symmetric ($J_{\operatorname{Softmax}}^\top = J_{\operatorname{Softmax}}$) and positive semi-definite.
    2. Multiplying by the all-ones vector $\mathbf{1}_C = [1,\dots,1]^\top$ gives $J_{\operatorname{Softmax}} \mathbf{1}_C = \hat{p} - \hat{p}(\hat{p}^\top \mathbf{1}_C) = \hat{p} - \hat{p}(1) = 0$. The vector $\mathbf{1}_C$ lies in the nullspace of $J_{\operatorname{Softmax}}$ because adding a constant shift $c \mathbf{1}_C$ to all logits leaves Softmax probabilities invariant!

    Step 3b: Contracting the Softmax Jacobian with the Categorical Cross-Entropy loss

    Now couple the Softmax output $\hat{p} \in \Delta^{C-1}$ with the Categorical Cross-Entropy loss against target distribution $y = (y_1, \dots, y_C)^\top$ (which may be either a one-hot vector $e_c$ or any soft probability distribution satisfying $\sum_{k=1}^C y_k = 1$, such as label smoothing or teacher distillation):

    $$\mathcal{L}_{\text{CE}}(\hat{p}, y) = -\sum_{k=1}^C y_k \log \hat{p}_k, \qquad \text{with} \qquad \frac{\partial \mathcal{L}_{\text{CE}}}{\partial \hat{p}_k} = -\frac{y_k}{\hat{p}_k}.$$

    Because logit $z_i$ affects all $C$ probabilities $\hat{p}_1, \dots, \hat{p}_C$, the multivariable chain rule sums over all $k = 1, \dots, C$:

    $$\begin{aligned} \delta_i^{(L)} = \frac{\partial \mathcal{L}_{\text{CE}}}{\partial z_i} &= \sum_{k=1}^C \frac{\partial \mathcal{L}_{\text{CE}}}{\partial \hat{p}_k} \, \frac{\partial \hat{p}_k}{\partial z_i} \\ &= \sum_{k=1}^C \left(-\frac{y_k}{\hat{p}_k}\right) \Bigl[\hat{p}_k\bigl(\delta_{ki} - \hat{p}_i\bigr)\Bigr] \\ &= -\sum_{k=1}^C y_k \bigl(\delta_{ki} - \hat{p}_i\bigr) \\ &= -\underbrace{\sum_{k=1}^C y_k \delta_{ki}}_{= y_i} + \hat{p}_i \underbrace{\sum_{k=1}^C y_k}_{= 1} \\ &= \hat{p}_i - y_i. \end{aligned}$$

    We can verify the exact same result using the log-sum-exp form $\mathcal{L}_{\text{CE}}(z, y) = -\sum_{k=1}^C y_k \bigl(z_k - \log \sum_{m=1}^C e^{z_m}\bigr) = -\sum_{k=1}^C y_k z_k + \bigl(\sum_{k=1}^C y_k\bigr) \log \sum_{m=1}^C e^{z_m}$: differentiating directly with respect to $z_i$ gives $-y_i + 1 \cdot \frac{e^{z_i}}{\sum_m e^{z_m}} = \hat{p}_i - y_i$ in a single step!

    $$\boxed{\delta_i^{(L)} = \frac{\partial \mathcal{L}_{\text{CE}}}{\partial z_i^{(L)}} = \hat{p}_i - y_i \quad \Longleftrightarrow \quad \delta^{(L)} = \operatorname{Softmax}\!\bigl(z^{(L)}\bigr) - y = \hat{p} - y \in \mathbb{R}^C.}$$

    4. Why all three canonical pairs yield $\delta^{(L)} = \hat{y} - y$: Exponential Families

    Is it a coincidence that Linear + MSE, Sigmoid + BCE, and Softmax + CE all produce $\delta^{(L)} = \hat{y} - y$? Not at all. In a canonical exponential family with natural parameter $\eta = z^{(L)}$, sufficient statistic $T(y) = y$, and log-partition (cumulant) function $A(\eta)$,

    $$p(y \mid \eta) = h(y) \exp\bigl(\eta^\top y - A(\eta)\bigr) \quad \Longrightarrow \quad \mathcal{L}(\eta, y) = -\log p(y \mid \eta) = A(\eta) - \eta^\top y - \log h(y).$$

    A fundamental theorem of exponential families states that the gradient of the log-partition function $A(\eta)$ with respect to the natural parameter $\eta$ is the mean of the sufficient statistic: $\nabla_\eta A(\eta) = \mathbb{E}[y \mid \eta] \triangleq \hat{y}$. Differentiating the negative log-likelihood with respect to $\eta = z^{(L)}$ therefore yields:

    $$\boxed{\delta^{(L)} = \nabla_{\eta} \mathcal{L}(\eta, y) = \nabla_{\eta} A(\eta) - y = \mathbb{E}[y \mid \eta] - y = \hat{y} - y.}$$
    Distribution Natural Parameter $\eta$ Log-Partition $A(\eta)$ Mean $\hat{y} = \nabla_\eta A(\eta)$ Logit Gradient $\delta^{(L)} = \nabla_\eta \mathcal{L}$
    Gaussian ($\sigma^2=1$) $z \in \mathbb{R}^d$ $\frac{1}{2}\|z\|_2^2$ $\hat{y} = z$ $z - y = \hat{y} - y$
    Bernoulli $z \in \mathbb{R}$ $\log(1 + e^z)$ $\hat{p} = \frac{1}{1+e^{-z}} = \sigma(z)$ $\sigma(z) - y = \hat{p} - y$
    Categorical $z \in \mathbb{R}^C$ $\log \sum_{m=1}^C e^{z_m}$ $\hat{p} = \operatorname{Softmax}(z)$ $\operatorname{Softmax}(z) - y = \hat{p} - y$
    Poisson $z \in \mathbb{R}$ $e^z$ $\hat{\lambda} = e^z$ $e^z - y = \hat{\lambda} - y$
