    1. Computational Graphs, Forward (JVP) vs. Reverse Mode (VJP), Checkpointing & Hessians

    October 2026 · 16 min read

    Backpropagation is reverse-mode automatic differentiation (AD) applied to a directed acyclic computational graph whose terminal node is a scalar loss $\mathcal{L} \in \mathbb{R}$. Here we derive the multivariable chain rule on general DAGs, prove why associating the Jacobian product from left to right (VJP) rather than right to left (JVP) reduces gradient cost by a factor of $P$, and see how the same AD machinery enables $O(\sqrt{L})$ activation checkpointing and $O(P)$ Hessian-Vector Products ($Hv$).

    1. The multivariable chain rule on a Directed Acyclic Graph (DAG)

    Let $G = (V, E)$ be a directed acyclic graph whose nodes $u_1, u_2, \dots, u_M$ are ordered topologically, with each node computed from its parents $\operatorname{Pa}(u_k) = \{u_i : (u_i \to u_k) \in E\}$ via $u_k = g_k\bigl(\{u_i\}_{i \in \operatorname{Pa}(u_k)}\bigr)$, ending at the scalar loss $u_M = \mathcal{L} \in \mathbb{R}$. When we perturb an earlier node $u_i$, that perturbation propagates forward along every outgoing edge to the children $\operatorname{Ch}(u_i) = \{u_k : (u_i \to u_k) \in E\}$. By the multivariable chain rule, the total sensitivity (or adjoint) $\bar{u}_i \triangleq \frac{\partial \mathcal{L}}{\partial u_i}$ sums over all immediate children of $u_i$:

    $$\boxed{\bar{u}_i \triangleq \frac{\partial \mathcal{L}}{\partial u_i} = \sum_{u_k \in \operatorname{Ch}(u_i)} \frac{\partial \mathcal{L}}{\partial u_k}\,\frac{\partial u_k}{\partial u_i} = \sum_{u_k \in \operatorname{Ch}(u_i)} \bar{u}_k \, \frac{\partial u_k}{\partial u_i}.}$$

    If we unrolled this recurrence into a sum over all directed paths $\gamma : u_i \rightsquigarrow \mathcal{L}$, a dense $L$-layer network of width $d$ would have $d^{L-1}$ paths—exponential in depth! Instead, evaluating $\bar{u}_i$ in reverse topological order ($i = M-1 \downarrow 1$) memoizes every child’s adjoint $\bar{u}_k$ before visiting $u_i$, traversing each edge in $E$ exactly once.

    2. Jacobian chains and Forward Mode (JVP) vs. Reverse Mode (VJP)

    For a sequential composition $f = f_L \circ f_{L-1} \circ \dots \circ f_1$ where $u^{(\ell)} = f_\ell\bigl(u^{(\ell-1)}\bigr) \in \mathbb{R}^{d_\ell}$ and $J_\ell \triangleq \frac{\partial u^{(\ell)}}{\partial u^{(\ell-1)}} \in \mathbb{R}^{d_\ell \times d_{\ell-1}}$, the total Jacobian is the matrix product

    $$J_f(x) = \frac{\partial u^{(L)}}{\partial u^{(0)}} = \underbrace{J_L}_{d_L \times d_{L-1}} \, \underbrace{J_{L-1}}_{d_{L-1} \times d_{L-2}} \, \cdots \, \underbrace{J_2}_{d_2 \times d_1} \, \underbrace{J_1}_{d_1 \times d_0} \in \mathbb{R}^{d_L \times d_0}.$$

    Matrix multiplication is associative, $(AB)C = A(BC)$, but the number of scalar multiplications depends drastically on parenthesization:

    • Forward Mode — Jacobian-Vector Product (JVP, Right-to-Left): Given a tangent vector $v^{(0)} = \dot{u}^{(0)} \in \mathbb{R}^{d_0}$, propagate $\dot{u}^{(\ell)} = J_\ell \dot{u}^{(\ell-1)}$ forward: $$J_f(x)\,v^{(0)} = J_L\Bigl(J_{L-1}\bigl(\cdots(J_2(J_1 v^{(0)}))\cdots\bigr)\Bigr).$$ One JVP pass computes one column of $J_f(x)$ (sensitivity of all outputs to one input coordinate). Recovering all $d_0$ derivatives requires $d_0$ separate passes!
    • Reverse Mode — Vector-Jacobian Product (VJP, Left-to-Right): Given an output cotangent/seed $\bar{u}^{(L)} \in \mathbb{R}^{d_L}$, propagate $\bar{u}^{(\ell-1)} = J_\ell^\top \bar{u}^{(\ell)}$ backward: $$\bar{u}^{(L)\top} J_f(x) = \Bigl(\bigl((\bar{u}^{(L)\top} J_L) J_{L-1}\bigr)\cdots J_2\Bigr) J_1.$$ When the output is a scalar loss ($d_L = 1$, $\bar{u}^{(L)} = [1]$), the left operand stays a $1 \times d_\ell$ row vector at every step!

    Forward Mode (Right-to-Left) $J_3 \bigl(J_2 J_1\bigr)$ $J_{21} = J_2 J_1$: $d_2 d_1 d_0$ $J_3 J_{21}$: $1 \cdot d_2 d_0$ $d^3 + d^2 = \mathbf{\Theta(d^3)}$
    Reverse Mode (Left-to-Right VJP) $\bigl(J_3 J_2\bigr) J_1$ $\bar{u}^{(1)\top} = J_3 J_2$: $1 \cdot d_2 d_1$ $\bar{u}^{(1)\top} J_1$: $1 \cdot d_1 d_0$ $d^2 + d^2 = \mathbf{2d^2 = \Theta(d^2)}$

    3. Activation memory & $O(\sqrt{L})$ Activation Checkpointing

    Because the local backward VJP $J_\ell(u^{(\ell-1)})^\top \bar{u}^{(\ell)}$ depends on the primal forward state $u^{(\ell-1)}$ (both to evaluate $\phi_\ell'(z^{(\ell)})$ and to multiply $\delta^{(\ell)} (a^{(\ell-1)})^\top$), standard backpropagation caches all $L$ layer activations in GPU RAM, costing $O(L)$ memory.

    Activation checkpointing (Griewank & Walther 2000; Chen et al. 2016) partitions the $L$ layers into $K = \sqrt{L}$ contiguous segments of length $S = \sqrt{L}$:

    1. During the forward pass, save only the $\sqrt{L}$ segment boundary activations $\bigl\{u^{(S)}, u^{(2S)}, \dots, u^{(L)}\bigr\}$ and discard interior states.
    2. During the backward pass at segment $m$, reload $u^{((m-1)S)}$, recompute the $S = \sqrt{L}$ interior states of segment $m$ with one local forward pass, backpropagate through segment $m$, and free them immediately.
    $$\boxed{\text{Peak Activation Memory} = \underbrace{\sqrt{L}}_{\text{saved boundaries}} + \underbrace{\sqrt{L}}_{\text{active segment}} = 2\sqrt{L} = O\bigl(\sqrt{L}\bigr), \qquad \text{Compute Overhead} = +1 \text{ forward pass } (\approx +33\% \text{ FLOPs}).}$$

    4. Higher-Order AD: Pearlmutter’s $O(P)$ Hessian-Vector Product ($Hv$)

    For a network with $P$ parameters $\theta \in \mathbb{R}^P$, the Hessian matrix $H = \nabla_\theta^2 \mathcal{L}(\theta) \in \mathbb{R}^{P \times P}$ has $P^2$ entries—impossible to materialize when $P = 10^9$. However, second-order methods (conjugate gradient, Lanczos eigenvalue estimation, curvature preconditioners) only need the Hessian-Vector Product (HVP) $H v \in \mathbb{R}^P$ for a direction $v \in \mathbb{R}^P$.

    Pearlmutter (1994) observed that $H v$ is the gradient (or forward JVP) of the scalar inner product $\langle \nabla_\theta \mathcal{L}(\theta), v \rangle$ holding $v$ constant:

    $$\boxed{(H v)_p = \sum_{q=1}^P \frac{\partial^2 \mathcal{L}(\theta)}{\partial \theta_p \partial \theta_q} v_q = \frac{\partial}{\partial \theta_p}\Bigl\langle \nabla_\theta \mathcal{L}(\theta), \, v \Bigr\rangle \quad \Longleftrightarrow \quad H v = \nabla_\theta \Bigl(\bigl\langle \nabla_\theta \mathcal{L}(\theta), \, v \bigr\rangle\Bigr) = \operatorname{JVP}_{\nabla_\theta \mathcal{L}}(\theta, v).}$$

    Composing forward-mode JVP over reverse-mode VJP (`jvp(grad(L), (theta,), (v,))` in JAX, or a double backward on `sum(g * v)` in PyTorch) computes the exact $H v \in \mathbb{R}^P$ in $O(P)$ time and $O(P)$ memory without ever forming $H$!

    Where to read about deep Jacobian conditioning: For how the unrolled product $\prod_{\ell} (W^{(\ell)})^\top \operatorname{diag}(\phi_\ell'(z^{(\ell)}))$ causes vanishing or exploding gradients and how variance-preserving initialization and Pre-LN residuals fix it, see Symmetry & Signal Propagation Across Depth and Post-LN vs. Pre-LN in Transformers.
