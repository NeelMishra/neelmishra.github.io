    1. Multivariable Chain Rule, Computational Graphs, and Forward (JVP) vs. Reverse Mode (VJP)

    October 2026 · 15 min read

    Backpropagation is not a heuristic specific to neural networks: it is reverse-mode automatic differentiation (AD) applied to a directed acyclic computational graph whose final node is a scalar loss $\mathcal{L} \in \mathbb{R}$. To understand why backpropagation works—and why associating the Jacobian product from left to right rather than right to left reduces gradient cost by a factor of $P$—we first derive the multivariable chain rule on general DAGs and compare Jacobian-Vector Products (JVPs) with Vector-Jacobian Products (VJPs).

    1. The multivariable chain rule on a Directed Acyclic Graph (DAG)

    A computational graph $G = (V, E)$ is a directed acyclic graph whose nodes $u_1, u_2, \dots, u_M$ are arranged in topological order. Each node $u_k$ is computed as an elementary differentiable function of its parent nodes $\operatorname{Pa}(u_k) = \{u_i : (u_i \to u_k) \in E\}$:

    $$u_k = g_k\bigl(\{u_i\}_{i \in \operatorname{Pa}(u_k)}\bigr).$$

    Suppose the final node $u_M = \mathcal{L} \in \mathbb{R}$ is a scalar loss. When we perturb an earlier node $u_i$ while holding all independent inputs outside the descendants of $u_i$ fixed, that perturbation propagates forward along every outgoing edge $(u_i \to u_k) \in E$ to the children $\operatorname{Ch}(u_i) = \{u_k : (u_i \to u_k) \in E\}$. By the multivariable chain rule, the total sensitivity (or adjoint) $\bar{u}_i \triangleq \frac{\partial \mathcal{L}}{\partial u_i}$ sums over all immediate children of $u_i$:

    $$\boxed{\bar{u}_i \triangleq \frac{\partial \mathcal{L}}{\partial u_i} = \sum_{u_k \in \operatorname{Ch}(u_i)} \frac{\partial \mathcal{L}}{\partial u_k}\,\frac{\partial u_k}{\partial u_i} = \sum_{u_k \in \operatorname{Ch}(u_i)} \bar{u}_k \, \frac{\partial u_k}{\partial u_i}.}$$
    Partial vs. total derivative notation in computational graphs:

    When a node $u_k = g_k(u_i, u_j)$ depends on $u_i$ both directly (as its first argument) and indirectly (because $u_j = h(u_i)$ is also a child of $u_i$), some texts write $\frac{d\mathcal{L}}{du_i}$ for the total sensitivity summed over all paths from $u_i$ to $\mathcal{L}$, and reserve $\frac{\partial u_k}{\partial u_i}$ for the local partial derivative of the primitive operation $g_k$ with respect to its direct argument $u_i$ holding $u_j$ fixed. Defining the adjoint symbol $\bar{u}_i \triangleq \frac{d\mathcal{L}}{du_i}$ (or $\delta^{(\ell)} \triangleq \nabla_{z^{(\ell)}} \mathcal{L}$ in layered networks) eliminates all ambiguity: $\bar{u}_i$ is the total derivative of $\mathcal{L}$ with respect to clamping node $u_i$, whereas $\frac{\partial u_k}{\partial u_i}$ is the local edge derivative on edge $(u_i \to u_k)$.

    Unrolling the recursion: the sum-over-paths theorem vs. dynamic programming

    If we expand $\bar{u}_k$ recursively all the way to the terminal node $u_M = \mathcal{L}$ (where $\bar{u}_M = \frac{\partial \mathcal{L}}{\partial \mathcal{L}} = 1$), the adjoint $\bar{u}_i$ equals the sum over all directed paths $\gamma = (u_i = v_0 \to v_1 \to \dots \to v_r = \mathcal{L})$ from $u_i$ to $\mathcal{L}$ of the product of local edge derivatives along $\gamma$:

    $$\bar{u}_i = \sum_{\gamma : u_i \rightsquigarrow \mathcal{L}} \;\prod_{m=1}^{r(\gamma)} \frac{\partial v_m}{\partial v_{m-1}}.$$

    In a dense neural network with $L$ layers of width $d$, every neuron in layer $1$ connects to every neuron in layer $2$, and so on, creating $d^{L-1}$ distinct directed paths from a single weight in layer $1$ to the output! Enumerating paths explicitly would take $O(d^L)$ exponential time. Instead, the DAG recurrence $\bar{u}_i = \sum_{u_k \in \operatorname{Ch}(u_i)} \bar{u}_k \frac{\partial u_k}{\partial u_i}$ evaluates nodes in reverse topological order ($i = M-1, M-2, \dots, 1$). Because each child’s adjoint $\bar{u}_k$ is already computed and memoized before we visit $u_i$, every directed edge $(u_i \to u_k)$ in the graph is traversed exactly once. Backpropagation is therefore dynamic programming on the DAG of local derivatives.

    2. Vector-valued compositions and the Jacobian chain rule

    Now organize the computational graph into sequential vector-valued transformations: let $f : \mathbb{R}^{d_0} \to \mathbb{R}^{d_L}$ be the composition of $L$ differentiable maps

    $$f = f_L \circ f_{L-1} \circ \dots \circ f_1, \qquad u^{(0)} = x \in \mathbb{R}^{d_0}, \qquad u^{(\ell)} = f_\ell\bigl(u^{(\ell-1)}\bigr) \in \mathbb{R}^{d_\ell}.$$

    For any map $f_\ell : \mathbb{R}^{d_{\ell-1}} \to \mathbb{R}^{d_\ell}$, its Jacobian matrix at $u^{(\ell-1)}$ is the $d_\ell \times d_{\ell-1}$ matrix of all first-order partial derivatives, where row $i$ corresponds to output coordinate $u_i^{(\ell)}$ and column $j$ corresponds to input coordinate $u_j^{(\ell-1)}$:

    $$J_\ell \triangleq \frac{\partial u^{(\ell)}}{\partial u^{(\ell-1)}} = \begin{bmatrix} \dfrac{\partial u_1^{(\ell)}}{\partial u_1^{(\ell-1)}} & \dfrac{\partial u_1^{(\ell)}}{\partial u_2^{(\ell-1)}} & \cdots & \dfrac{\partial u_1^{(\ell)}}{\partial u_{d_{\ell-1}}^{(\ell-1)}} \\[8pt] \vdots & \vdots & \ddots & \vdots \\[8pt] \dfrac{\partial u_{d_\ell}^{(\ell)}}{\partial u_1^{(\ell-1)}} & \dfrac{\partial u_{d_\ell}^{(\ell)}}{\partial u_2^{(\ell-1)}} & \cdots & \dfrac{\partial u_{d_\ell}^{(\ell)}}{\partial u_{d_{\ell-1}}^{(\ell-1)}} \end{bmatrix} \in \mathbb{R}^{d_\ell \times d_{\ell-1}}.$$

    By the Fréchet derivative chain rule, the total Jacobian $J_f(x) = \frac{\partial u^{(L)}}{\partial u^{(0)}} \in \mathbb{R}^{d_L \times d_0}$ of the composite map is the matrix product of the individual layer Jacobians:

    $$\boxed{J_f(x) = \frac{\partial u^{(L)}}{\partial u^{(0)}} = \underbrace{J_L}_{d_L \times d_{L-1}} \, \underbrace{J_{L-1}}_{d_{L-1} \times d_{L-2}} \, \cdots \, \underbrace{J_2}_{d_2 \times d_1} \, \underbrace{J_1}_{d_1 \times d_0} \in \mathbb{R}^{d_L \times d_0}.}$$

    3. Forward Mode (JVP) vs. Reverse Mode (VJP): the associativity of matrix multiplication

    Matrix multiplication is associative: $(AB)C = A(BC)$ produces the exact same mathematical matrix, but the number of scalar floating-point multiplications depends drastically on the parenthesization.

    Forward-Mode Automatic Differentiation: Jacobian-Vector Products (JVP)

    Fix an input perturbation direction (a tangent vector) $v^{(0)} = \dot{u}^{(0)} \in \mathbb{R}^{d_0}$, and parenthesize the Jacobian chain from right to left:

    $$J_f(x)\, v^{(0)} = J_L \Bigl( J_{L-1} \bigl( \cdots (J_2 (J_1 v^{(0)})) \cdots \bigr) \Bigr).$$

    Define the forward tangent recurrence $\dot{u}^{(\ell)} \triangleq J_\ell \dot{u}^{(\ell-1)} \in \mathbb{R}^{d_\ell}$ for $\ell = 1, \dots, L$. At each step we multiply a $d_\ell \times d_{\ell-1}$ Jacobian by a $d_{\ell-1} \times 1$ column vector—a matrix-vector product (JVP) that never materializes the full $J_f(x)$ matrix and runs alongside the forward pass:

    • Setting $v^{(0)} = e_j$ (the $j$-th standard basis vector in $\mathbb{R}^{d_0}$) computes the $j$-th column of $J_f(x)$: the sensitivity of all $d_L$ outputs to a single input coordinate $x_j$.
    • To recover the full Jacobian $J_f(x) \in \mathbb{R}^{d_L \times d_0}$ via forward mode, we must run $d_0$ separate JVP passes (one for each basis vector $e_1, \dots, e_{d_0}$).

    Reverse-Mode Automatic Differentiation: Vector-Jacobian Products (VJP)

    Now fix an output cotangent (or seed adjoint) row vector $\bar{u}^{(L)\top} \in \mathbb{R}^{1 \times d_L}$—or equivalently a column vector $\bar{u}^{(L)} \in \mathbb{R}^{d_L}$—and parenthesize the Jacobian chain from left to right:

    $$\bar{u}^{(L)\top} J_f(x) = \Bigl(\bigl((\bar{u}^{(L)\top} J_L) J_{L-1}\bigr) \cdots J_2\Bigr) J_1, \qquad \text{or in column form:} \qquad J_f(x)^\top \bar{u}^{(L)} = J_1^\top \Bigl(J_2^\top \bigl(\cdots J_{L-1}^\top (J_L^\top \bar{u}^{(L)})\bigr)\Bigr).$$

    Define the backward adjoint recurrence $\bar{u}^{(\ell-1)} \triangleq J_\ell^\top \bar{u}^{(\ell)} \in \mathbb{R}^{d_{\ell-1}}$ for $\ell = L, L-1, \dots, 1$. At each step we compute a Vector-Jacobian Product (VJP):

    • Setting $\bar{u}^{(L)} = e_i \in \mathbb{R}^{d_L}$ computes the $i$-th row of $J_f(x)$: the gradient of a single output $u_i^{(L)}$ with respect to all $d_0$ inputs simultaneously!
    • When the final output is a scalar loss ($d_L = 1$, $u^{(L)} = \mathcal{L} \in \mathbb{R}$) and we seed $\bar{u}^{(L)} = [1]$, a single reverse VJP sweep computes the entire gradient $\nabla_x \mathcal{L} \in \mathbb{R}^{d_0}$.

    4. Explicit FLOP complexity proof: why Reverse Mode wins for $d_L = 1$

    Recall that multiplying a matrix $A \in \mathbb{R}^{p \times q}$ by a matrix $B \in \mathbb{R}^{q \times r}$ requires $pqr$ scalar multiply-accumulates ($2pqr$ FLOPs). Consider a network with $L=3$ stages mapping $d_0 \to d_1 \to d_2 \to d_3 = 1$ (scalar loss), so $J_1 \in \mathbb{R}^{d_1 \times d_0}$, $J_2 \in \mathbb{R}^{d_2 \times d_1}$, and $J_3 \in \mathbb{R}^{1 \times d_2}$:

    Evaluation Strategy Parenthesization of $J_3 J_2 J_1$ Step 1 Mul-Adds Step 2 Mul-Adds Total Mul-Adds ($d_0=d_1=d_2=d$)
    Forward Mode (Right-to-Left) $J_3 \bigl(J_2 J_1\bigr)$ $J_{21} = J_2 J_1$: $d_2 d_1 d_0$ $J_3 J_{21}$: $1 \cdot d_2 d_0$ $d^3 + d^2 = \mathbf{\Theta(d^3)}$
    Reverse Mode (Left-to-Right) $\bigl(J_3 J_2\bigr) J_1$ $\bar{u}^{(1)\top} = J_3 J_2$: $1 \cdot d_2 d_1$ $\bar{u}^{(1)\top} J_1$: $1 \cdot d_1 d_0$ $d^2 + d^2 = \mathbf{2d^2 = \Theta(d^2)}$

    Notice the difference: right-to-left evaluation multiplies two dense matrices $J_2 J_1$ at cost $O(d^3)$, whereas left-to-right evaluation keeps the left operand as a $1 \times d_\ell$ row vector at every step, reducing every stage to a vector-matrix product costing $O(d^2)$—the exact same asymptotic complexity as the forward pass! More generally, for any differentiable map $F : \mathbb{R}^n \to \mathbb{R}^m$:

    • Forward mode (JVP) computes $J_F \in \mathbb{R}^{m \times n}$ in $n$ passes ($O(n) \times \text{cost}(F)$). Best when $n \ll m$ (e.g., differentiating a 3D trajectory $\mathbb{R} \to \mathbb{R}^{1000}$ with respect to time $t$, or computing directional derivatives $J_F v$ along a single vector $v$).
    • Reverse mode (VJP / Backpropagation) computes $J_F \in \mathbb{R}^{m \times n}$ in $m$ passes ($O(m) \times \text{cost}(F)$). Best when $m \ll n$—and in deep learning, $m = 1$ (the scalar loss $\mathcal{L}$) while $n = P \sim 10^6\text{–}10^{12}$ parameters!

    5. The price of reverse mode: storing forward activations

    Why does reverse mode require caching the forward activations $a^{(0)}, z^{(1)}, a^{(1)}, \dots, z^{(L)}$ in GPU RAM during training, whereas inference can discard $a^{(\ell-1)}$ immediately after computing $a^{(\ell)}$? Because the local Jacobian $J_\ell(u^{(\ell-1)})$ of a nonlinear or bilinear operation depends on the primal forward value $u^{(\ell-1)}$ at which the derivative is evaluated:

    • For an element-wise activation $a^{(\ell)} = \phi_\ell(z^{(\ell)})$, the backward VJP multiplies by $\phi_\ell'(z^{(\ell)})$, which requires knowing $z^{(\ell)}$ (or $a^{(\ell)}$).
    • For a linear multiplication $z^{(\ell)} = W^{(\ell)} a^{(\ell-1)} + b^{(\ell)}$, the derivative with respect to $W^{(\ell)}$ is proportional to $a^{(\ell-1)}$: $$\frac{\partial z_i^{(\ell)}}{\partial W_{ij}^{(\ell)}} = a_j^{(\ell-1)}.$$ To accumulate $\nabla_{W^{(\ell)}} \mathcal{L}$ during the backward pass at step $\ell$, the activation tensor $a^{(\ell-1)}$ computed during the forward pass must still be available in memory!

    With the computational graph and VJP foundations established, we are ready in Chapter 2 to write down the exact equations of an $L$-layer MLP and derive all four backpropagation equations (BP1–BP4) index by index.
