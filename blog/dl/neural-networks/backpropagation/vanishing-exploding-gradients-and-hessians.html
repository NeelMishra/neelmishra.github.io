    6. Deep Backpropagation Pathologies & Higher-Order Calculus: Vanishing/Exploding Jacobians, Residual Highways, Checkpointing, and Hessian-Vector Products

    October 2026 · 18 min read

    Why did training plain 50-layer feed-forward networks fail for decades even though backpropagation computes exact analytical gradients? Unrolling the backward sensitivity recursion BP2 across $L - \ell$ layers reveals a long matrix product of transposed weight matrices and diagonal activation derivatives. In this concluding chapter, we analyze the spectral mechanics of vanishing and exploding gradients, prove why residual connections $a^{(\ell)} = a^{(\ell-1)} + F_\ell(a^{(\ell-1)})$ create an unimpeded gradient highway, derive $O(\sqrt{L})$ activation checkpointing, and derive Pearlmutter’s $O(P)$ Hessian-Vector Product ($Hv$) algorithm.

    1. Unrolling BP2: the deep Jacobian product and singular value cascades

    Write the element-wise activation derivative at layer $k$ as the diagonal matrix

    $$D^{(k)} \triangleq \operatorname{diag}\!\Bigl(\phi_k'\!\bigl(z^{(k)}\bigr)\Bigr) = \begin{bmatrix} \phi_k'\bigl(z_1^{(k)}\bigr) & & 0 \\ & \ddots & \\ 0 & & \phi_k'\bigl(z_{d_k}^{(k)}\bigr) \end{bmatrix} \in \mathbb{R}^{d_k \times d_k}.$$

    Then the single-layer backward recursion BP2 ($\delta^{(k-1)} = D^{(k-1)} (W^{(k)})^\top \delta^{(k)}$) unrolls from the output layer $L$ down to any early hidden layer $\ell \in \{1, \dots, L-1\}$ as an explicit $(L - \ell)$-fold matrix product:

    $$\boxed{\delta^{(\ell)} = D^{(\ell)} \left( \prod_{k=\ell+1}^{L} \bigl(W^{(k)}\bigr)^\top D^{(k)} \right)^{\!\top\text{-ordered}} \delta^{(L)} = D^{(\ell)} \bigl(W^{(\ell+1)}\bigr)^\top D^{(\ell+1)} \bigl(W^{(\ell+2)}\bigr)^\top \cdots D^{(L-1)} \bigl(W^{(L)}\bigr)^\top \delta^{(L)}.}$$

    Taking the Euclidean norm $\|\cdot\|_2$ and applying sub-multiplicativity $\|AB\|_2 \le \|A\|_2 \|B\|_2$ (where $\|M\|_2 = \sigma_{\max}(M)$ is the spectral norm, i.e., largest singular value) gives the upper bound:

    $$\bigl\|\delta^{(\ell)}\bigr\|_2 \le \bigl\|D^{(\ell)}\bigr\|_2 \left( \prod_{k=\ell+1}^{L} \sigma_{\max}\!\bigl(W^{(k)}\bigr) \, \max_{i} \bigl|\phi_k'\bigl(z_i^{(k)}\bigr)\bigr| \right) \bigl\|\delta^{(L)}\bigr\|_2.$$

    More generally, if the per-layer Jacobian $J_k^\top = (W^{(k)})^\top D^{(k)}$ has singular values bounded around $\gamma$, then after $L - \ell$ layers the gradient norm scales exponentially with depth:

    • Vanishing gradients ($\gamma < 1$): $\bigl\|\delta^{(\ell)}\bigr\|_2 \sim \gamma^{L - \ell} \bigl\|\delta^{(L)}\bigr\|_2 \to 0$ exponentially fast as $L - \ell$ grows. Early layers receive virtually zero gradient signal and remain frozen near their random initialization while only the final few layers learn.
    • Exploding gradients ($\gamma > 1$): $\bigl\|\delta^{(\ell)}\bigr\|_2 \sim \gamma^{L - \ell} \bigl\|\delta^{(L)}\bigr\|_2 \to \infty$ exponentially fast, causing catastrophic parameter updates and `NaN` divergence unless mitigated by proper initialization (Xavier/Glorot $\operatorname{Var}(W_{ij}) = \frac{1}{d_{\text{in}}}$ or He/Kaiming $\operatorname{Var}(W_{ij}) = \frac{2}{d_{\text{in}}}$), normalization layers, and gradient clipping.

    2. Activation pathologies: why Sigmoid attenuates by $(1/4)^L$ and how ReLUs die

    Pathology 1: Logistic Sigmoid saturation ($\sigma'(z) \le \frac{1}{4}$)

    For the logistic sigmoid $\sigma(z) = \frac{1}{1+e^{-z}}$, its derivative is $\sigma'(z) = \sigma(z)\bigl(1 - \sigma(z)\bigr)$. Since $p(1-p) \le \frac{1}{4}$ for all $p \in (0, 1)$ (attaining its maximum $\frac{1}{4}$ only at $z = 0$ where $\sigma(0) = \frac{1}{2}$, and decaying exponentially as $e^{-|z|}$ in the tails):

    $$\bigl\|D^{(k)}\bigr\|_2 = \max_{i} \sigma'\!\bigl(z_i^{(k)}\bigr) \le \frac{1}{4}.$$

    If weights are initialized with standard normal variance so $\sigma_{\max}(W^{(k)}) \approx 1$, every layer multiplies the backward gradient by at most $\frac{1}{4}$. Across just $L = 10$ layers, $\bigl(\frac{1}{4}\bigr)^{10} \approx 9.5 \times 10^{-7}$—nearly seven orders of magnitude of attenuation! Furthermore, because $\sigma(z) \in (0, 1)$ is strictly positive, $a^{(\ell-1)} > 0$ in every coordinate, which forces every row of $\nabla_{W^{(\ell)}} \mathcal{L} = \delta^{(\ell)} (a^{(\ell-1)})^\top$ to have the exact same sign pattern (either all positive or all negative across $j$), creating zig-zagging optimization trajectories.

    Pathology 2: The Dying ReLU phenomenon ($\phi'(z) = 0$ for $z < 0$)

    ReLU ($\phi(z) = \max(0, z)$) eliminates the $\frac{1}{4}$ factor on its active half-line because $\phi'(z) = 1$ for all $z > 0$. However, for $z < 0$, both $\phi(z) = 0$ and $\phi'(z) = 0$. Suppose a large gradient step pushes a neuron’s bias $b_i^{(\ell)}$ or incoming weight row $W_{i,:}^{(\ell)}$ into a regime where $z_i^{(\ell)}(x) < 0$ for every sample $x$ in the training set:

    $$z_i^{(\ell)}(x) < 0 \;\;\forall x \quad \Longrightarrow \quad \delta_i^{(\ell)}(x) = \Bigl[\bigl(W^{(\ell+1)}\bigr)^\top \delta^{(\ell+1)}(x)\Bigr]_i \cdot \underbrace{\phi'\!\bigl(z_i^{(\ell)}(x)\bigr)}_{= 0} = 0 \;\;\forall x.$$

    By BP3 and BP4, $\frac{\partial \mathcal{L}}{\partial b_i^{(\ell)}} = \delta_i^{(\ell)} = 0$ and $\frac{\partial \mathcal{L}}{\partial W_{ij}^{(\ell)}} = \delta_i^{(\ell)} a_j^{(\ell-1)} = 0$ for every sample $x$. The neuron is permanently dead: its weights can never change again under first-order gradient descent! Smooth non-zero-tail activations ($\operatorname{LeakyReLU}$, $\operatorname{GELU}$, $\operatorname{SiLU}/\operatorname{Swish}$) keep a small non-zero derivative for $z < 0$ so negative units can recover.

    3. Residual connections: replacing multiplicative decay with an additive identity highway

    How do modern 100+ layer networks (ResNets and Transformers) avoid vanishing gradients completely? Instead of parameterizing layer $k$ as a direct map $u^{(k)} = F_k\bigl(u^{(k-1)}\bigr)$, He et al. (2015) introduced the residual block:

    $$u^{(k)} = u^{(k-1)} + F_k\!\bigl(u^{(k-1)}; \theta_k\bigr).$$

    Differentiating $u^{(k)}$ with respect to $u^{(k-1)}$ gives the single-block Jacobian

    $$\frac{\partial u^{(k)}}{\partial u^{(k-1)}} = I + J_{F_k}, \qquad \text{where } J_{F_k} \triangleq \frac{\partial F_k\bigl(u^{(k-1)}\bigr)}{\partial u^{(k-1)}}.$$

    Unrolling the chain rule from block $\ell$ to block $L$ yields:

    $$\boxed{\frac{\partial \mathcal{L}}{\partial u^{(\ell)}} = \left(\prod_{k=\ell+1}^{L} \bigl(I + J_{F_k}^\top\bigr)\right) \frac{\partial \mathcal{L}}{\partial u^{(L)}} = \frac{\partial \mathcal{L}}{\partial u^{(L)}} + \sum_{k=\ell+1}^{L} J_{F_k}^\top \frac{\partial \mathcal{L}}{\partial u^{(L)}} + \sum_{\ell < j < k \le L} J_{F_j}^\top J_{F_k}^\top \frac{\partial \mathcal{L}}{\partial u^{(L)}} + \cdots}$$

    Look at the leading term of the binomial expansion of $\prod_{k=\ell+1}^L (I + J_{F_k}^\top)$: the terminal gradient $\frac{\partial \mathcal{L}}{\partial u^{(L)}}$ is added directly to $\frac{\partial \mathcal{L}}{\partial u^{(\ell)}}$ with coefficient $I$, completely bypassing all intermediate weight matrices! Even if every residual branch Jacobian $J_{F_k}$ is small ($\|J_{F_k}\| \ll 1$), the identity term $I$ guarantees that gradients flow unimpeded from the loss all the way to layer $1$.

    4. Activation Checkpointing (Gradient Checkpointing): trading $1$ extra forward pass for $O(\sqrt{L})$ memory

    As we saw in Chapter 1, standard backpropagation caches all $L$ layer activations $\bigl\{u^{(1)}, \dots, u^{(L)}\bigr\}$ during the forward pass, consuming $O(L)$ activation memory. When training deep networks on long sequences or large batches, activation memory—not parameter memory—is the primary GPU RAM bottleneck.

    Activation checkpointing (Griewank & Walther 2000; Chen et al. 2016) divides the $L$ layers into $K = \sqrt{L}$ contiguous segments of length $S = \sqrt{L}$:

    1. During the initial forward pass, we store only the $K = \sqrt{L}$ segment boundary activations $\bigl\{u^{(S)}, u^{(2S)}, \dots, u^{(L)}\bigr\}$ and discard all interior activations inside each segment.
    2. During the backward pass, when we arrive at segment $m$, we retrieve its saved boundary input $u^{((m-1)S)}$, re-run the forward pass through just the $S = \sqrt{L}$ layers of segment $m$ to reconstruct its interior activations on the fly, backpropagate through segment $m$, and immediately free those interior activations.
    $$\boxed{\text{Peak Activation Memory} = \underbrace{\sqrt{L}}_{\text{saved boundaries}} + \underbrace{\sqrt{L}}_{\text{current segment}} = 2\sqrt{L} = O\bigl(\sqrt{L}\bigr), \qquad \text{Compute Overhead} = +1 \text{ forward pass } (\approx +33\% \text{ total FLOPs}).}$$

    5. Second-order automatic differentiation: Pearlmutter’s $O(P)$ Hessian-Vector Product ($Hv$)

    For a network with $P$ parameters $\theta \in \mathbb{R}^P$, the Hessian matrix $H = \nabla_\theta^2 \mathcal{L}(\theta) \in \mathbb{R}^{P \times P}$ has $P^2$ entries ($H_{pq} = \frac{\partial^2 \mathcal{L}}{\partial \theta_p \partial \theta_q}$). When $P = 10^9$, storing $H$ would require $10^{18}$ floats ($\approx 4$ exabytes)—completely impossible. Yet second-order optimizers (conjugate gradient, Lanczos eigenvalue estimation, Kronecker/Sophia curvature estimators, and influence functions) never need the matrix $H$ itself: they only need the Hessian-Vector Product (HVP) $H v \in \mathbb{R}^P$ for an arbitrary direction vector $v \in \mathbb{R}^P$.

    Pearlmutter (1994) observed that $H v$ is simply the directional derivative (or gradient) of the scalar dot product $\langle \nabla_\theta \mathcal{L}(\theta), v \rangle$ where $v$ is treated as a constant vector:

    $$\nabla_\theta \Bigl(\bigl\langle \nabla_\theta \mathcal{L}(\theta), \, v \bigr\rangle\Bigr)_p = \frac{\partial}{\partial \theta_p} \sum_{q=1}^P \frac{\partial \mathcal{L}(\theta)}{\partial \theta_q} v_q = \sum_{q=1}^P \frac{\partial^2 \mathcal{L}(\theta)}{\partial \theta_p \partial \theta_q} v_q = (H v)_p.$$
    $$\boxed{H v = \nabla_\theta^2 \mathcal{L}(\theta)\, v = \nabla_\theta \Bigl( \bigl\langle \nabla_\theta \mathcal{L}(\theta), \, v \bigr\rangle \Bigr) = \text{JVP}_{\nabla_\theta \mathcal{L}}(\theta, v) \in \mathbb{R}^P.}$$

    Therefore, we can compute the exact Hessian-vector product $H v \in \mathbb{R}^P$ without ever forming $H$ in $O(P)$ time and $O(P)$ memory (roughly $2\times$ the cost of a standard gradient calculation) in two ways:

    • Reverse-over-Reverse (PyTorch `autograd.grad` twice): First run reverse mode to build the differentiable computational graph of $g(\theta) = \nabla_\theta \mathcal{L}(\theta)$, form the scalar inner product $s(\theta) = \sum_{q=1}^P g_q(\theta) v_q$, and run a second reverse pass $\nabla_\theta s(\theta) = H v$.
    • Forward-over-Reverse (Pearlmutter’s $\mathcal{R}_v$-operator / JAX `jvp(grad(L), (theta,), (v,))`): Apply forward-mode tangent differentiation $\mathcal{R}_v\{\cdot\} = \frac{d}{d\varepsilon}\big|_{\varepsilon=0}(\cdot)(\theta + \varepsilon v)$ directly to the forward-backward equations of $\nabla_\theta \mathcal{L}(\theta)$, which avoids storing a second copy of the backward graph.

    6. Series synthesis: From feed-forward representations to reverse-mode credit assignment

    Across the Neural Networks parent series, the Feed-Forward Networks sub-series, and this Backpropagation sub-series, we have built the complete mathematical foundation of multilayer neural networks:

    1. Why depth and nonlinearity exist: Single linear layers can only slice $\mathbb{R}^{d_0}$ with a single hyperplane (failing on XOR), and stacking linear layers collapses by matrix associativity. Interleaving point-wise nonlinearities folds input space into exponentially many convex polytopes on which the network is locally affine.
    2. How the forward pass computes predictions: Mini-batch GEMMs $Z^{(\ell)} = A^{(\ell-1)}(W^{(\ell)})^\top + \mathbf{1}_B (b^{(\ell)})^\top$ followed by element-wise activations $A^{(\ell)} = \phi_\ell(Z^{(\ell)})$ and canonical exponential-family output heads map inputs to calibrated probability distributions in $2BP$ FLOPs.
    3. How backpropagation assigns credit in $O(1)$ passes: Seeding the output residual $\delta^{(L)} = \hat{y} - y$ (BP1), propagating sensitivities backward via transposed weights $\delta^{(\ell)} = ((W^{(\ell+1)})^\top \delta^{(\ell+1)}) \odot \phi_\ell'(z^{(\ell)})$ (BP2), and accumulating local outer products $\nabla_{W^{(\ell)}} \mathcal{L} = (G^{(\ell)})^\top A^{(\ell-1)}$ and column sums $\nabla_{b^{(\ell)}} \mathcal{L} = (G^{(\ell)})^\top \mathbf{1}_B$ (BP3–BP4) yields the exact gradient over all $P$ parameters in $4BP$ FLOPs.
