    From Perceptrons to Deep Representations: Half-Spaces, the XOR Obstruction, and Feature Space Folding

    October 2026 · 11 min read

    Understand why a single neuron is geometrically a hyperplane slicer, prove Novikoff’s finite-mistake bound for linearly separable data, prove algebraically why Minsky and Papert’s XOR problem breaks any single layer, and see how a hidden layer folds input space so a linear readout succeeds.

    1. A single neuron is an oriented hyperplane

    Both McCulloch–Pitts neurons (1943) and Rosenblatt’s Perceptron (1958) compute a weighted sum of an input vector $x \in \mathbb{R}^d$ plus a scalar bias $b \in \mathbb{R}$, followed by a threshold or activation function $\phi$:

    $$z(x) = w^\top x + b = \sum_{j=1}^d w_j x_j + b, \qquad \hat{y}(x) = \phi\bigl(w^\top x + b\bigr).$$

    Regardless of whether $\phi$ is a hard step function $\operatorname{sign}(z) \in \{-1, +1\}$, a logistic sigmoid $\sigma(z) \in (0, 1)$, or a ReLU $\max(0, z)$, the level sets of $z(x)$ are parallel hyperplanes orthogonal to $w$. Specifically:

    • Decision / activation boundary: The set $\mathcal{H} = \{x \in \mathbb{R}^d : w^\top x + b = 0\}$ is a $(d-1)$-dimensional affine hyperplane.
    • Normal orientation: For any two points $x_a, x_b \in \mathcal{H}$, we have $w^\top(x_a - x_b) = 0$, so $w$ is perpendicular to the boundary and points into the positive half-space $\mathcal{H}_+ = \{x : w^\top x + b > 0\}$.
    • Signed Euclidean distance to the hyperplane: Projecting any point $x \in \mathbb{R}^d$ orthogonally onto $\mathcal{H}$ gives the signed distance
      $$\operatorname{dist}(x, \mathcal{H}) = \frac{w^\top x + b}{\|w\|_2}, \qquad \operatorname{dist}(0, \mathcal{H}) = \frac{b}{\|w\|_2}.$$
      Thus $\|w\|_2$ controls the steepness of the transition across $\mathcal{H}$, while $-b / \|w\|_2$ sets the signed offset of the boundary from the origin along unit direction $w / \|w\|_2$.

    2. The Perceptron learning rule and Novikoff’s Convergence Theorem

    For binary labels $y_i \in \{-1, +1\}$ and augmented vectors $\tilde{x}_i = [x_i^\top, 1]^\top \in \mathbb{R}^{d+1}$, $\tilde{w} = [w^\top, b]^\top$, the classical Perceptron predicts $\hat{y}_i = \operatorname{sign}(w^\top x_i + b)$. Whenever it makes a mistake on example $(x_i, y_i)$—meaning $y_i (w^\top x_i + b) \le 0$—it updates:

    $$w^{(k+1)} = w^{(k)} + y_i x_i, \qquad b^{(k+1)} = b^{(k)} + y_i.$$

    Note that this is subgradient descent with step size $1$ on the Perceptron criterion $\mathcal{L}_{\text{perc}}(w, b) = \sum_{i=1}^n \max\bigl(0, -y_i(w^\top x_i + b)\bigr)$. Why can’t we train a multi-layer network with hard step functions $\operatorname{sign}(z)$ using gradient descent? Because $\frac{d}{dz}\operatorname{sign}(z) = 0$ everywhere except $z=0$ (where it is undefined), so no continuous sensitivity signal can propagate through hidden layers.

    Theorem (Novikoff, 1962 — Perceptron Mistake Bound).

    Suppose the training set $\{(\tilde{x}_i, y_i)\}_{i=1}^n$ lies inside a ball of radius $R = \max_i \|\tilde{x}_i\|_2$ and is strictly linearly separable by some unit vector $\tilde{w}^* \in \mathbb{R}^{d+1}$ ($\|\tilde{w}^*\|_2 = 1$) with margin $\gamma = \min_i y_i (\tilde{w}^{*\top} \tilde{x}_i) > 0$. Starting from $\tilde{w}^{(0)} = 0$, the Perceptron algorithm makes at most

    $$K \le \frac{R^2}{\gamma^2}\quad\text{mistakes before converging to a separating hyperplane.}$$

    Proof (by tracking alignment vs. norm growth): Let $\tilde{w}^{(k)}$ be the weight vector after $k$ mistakes. If the $(k+1)$-st mistake occurs on $(\tilde{x}_i, y_i)$, then $y_i (\tilde{w}^{(k)\top} \tilde{x}_i) \le 0$ and $\tilde{w}^{(k+1)} = \tilde{w}^{(k)} + y_i \tilde{x}_i$.

    1. Linear growth of alignment with $\tilde{w}^*$:
      $$\tilde{w}^{(k+1)\top} \tilde{w}^* = \tilde{w}^{(k)\top} \tilde{w}^* + y_i(\tilde{w}^{*\top}\tilde{x}_i) \ge \tilde{w}^{(k)\top}\tilde{w}^* + \gamma \implies \tilde{w}^{(k)\top}\tilde{w}^* \ge k\gamma.$$
    2. Sub-linear growth of $\|\tilde{w}^{(k)}\|_2$: Since $y_i \in \{-1,+1\}$ and $y_i(\tilde{w}^{(k)\top}\tilde{x}_i) \le 0$,
      $$\|\tilde{w}^{(k+1)}\|_2^2 = \|\tilde{w}^{(k)}\|_2^2 + \underbrace{2 y_i (\tilde{w}^{(k)\top}\tilde{x}_i)}_{\le\,0} + \|\tilde{x}_i\|_2^2 \le \|\tilde{w}^{(k)}\|_2^2 + R^2 \implies \|\tilde{w}^{(k)}\|_2^2 \le k R^2.$$
    3. Combine via Cauchy–Schwarz: Using $k\gamma \le \tilde{w}^{(k)\top}\tilde{w}^* \le \|\tilde{w}^{(k)}\|_2 \|\tilde{w}^*\|_2 \le \sqrt{k}\,R$:
      $$k^2 \gamma^2 \le k R^2 \implies k \le \frac{R^2}{\gamma^2}.\qquad\square$$

    3. The XOR obstruction: algebraic proof of linear impossibility

    In Perceptrons (1969), Minsky and Papert showed that a single linear threshold unit can represent $\operatorname{AND}$ ($x_1 + x_2 - 1.5 > 0$) and $\operatorname{OR}$ ($x_1 + x_2 - 0.5 > 0$), but cannot represent $\operatorname{XOR}$, where $y = x_1 \oplus x_2 \in \{0, 1\}$ on the four corners of the unit square $\{0, 1\}^2$:

    $$\begin{array}{c|cc|c} \text{Corner} & x_1 & x_2 & y = x_1 \oplus x_2 \\ \hline A & 0 & 0 & 0 \\ B & 0 & 1 & 1 \\ C & 1 & 0 & 1 \\ D & 1 & 1 & 0 \end{array}$$

    Theorem (Linear Inseparability of XOR). There do not exist any real numbers $w_1, w_2, b \in \mathbb{R}$ such that $w_1 x_1 + w_2 x_2 + b > 0$ when $x_1 \oplus x_2 = 1$ and $w_1 x_1 + w_2 x_2 + b \le 0$ when $x_1 \oplus x_2 = 0$.

    Proof: Suppose for contradiction that such $(w_1, w_2, b)$ exist. Evaluating the linear score $z(x_1, x_2) = w_1 x_1 + w_2 x_2 + b$ at the four corners yields four simultaneous inequalities:

    $$\begin{aligned} (0, 0) \mapsto y=0: &\quad b \le 0 & \text{(1)} \\ (1, 0) \mapsto y=1: &\quad w_1 + b > 0 & \text{(2)} \\ (0, 1) \mapsto y=1: &\quad w_2 + b > 0 & \text{(3)} \\ (1, 1) \mapsto y=0: &\quad w_1 + w_2 + b \le 0 & \text{(4)} \end{aligned}$$

    Adding the two strict positive inequalities (2) and (3) gives $w_1 + w_2 + 2b > 0$. Subtracting inequality (4) ($w_1 + w_2 + b \le 0$) yields $b > 0$, which directly contradicts inequality (1) ($b \le 0$). More generally, the convex hull of the positive points $\operatorname{conv}\{(0,1), (1,0)\}$ and the convex hull of the negative points $\operatorname{conv}\{(0,0), (1,1)\}$ intersect at the midpoint $(0.5, 0.5)$, and by the hyperplane separation theorem, two sets are linearly separable if and only if their convex hulls are disjoint. $\square$

    4. How a 2-unit hidden layer solves XOR by folding the plane

    A 2-layer feed-forward network solves XOR not by drawing a curved boundary in a single step, but by using a hidden layer $h = \phi(W^{(1)} x + b^{(1)}) \in \mathbb{R}^2$ to remap the four corners into a new coordinate space $(h_1, h_2)$ where the classes are linearly separable.

    With ReLU activation $\phi(z) = \max(0, z)$, choose hidden weights $W^{(1)} = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 1 \\ 1 & 1 \end{bmatrix}$, hidden biases $b^{(1)} = \begin{bmatrix} 0 \\ -1 \end{bmatrix}$, output weights $W^{(2)} = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & -2 \end{bmatrix}$, and output bias $b^{(2)} = 0$:

    $$h_1 = \max(0, x_1 + x_2), \qquad h_2 = \max(0, x_1 + x_2 - 1), \qquad \hat{y} = h_1 - 2h_2.$$

    Let us trace all four corners in a single matrix sweep (stacking the 4 inputs as rows of $X \in \mathbb{R}^{4 \times 2}$):

    $$X = \begin{bmatrix} 0 & 0 \\ 0 & 1 \\ 1 & 0 \\ 1 & 1 \end{bmatrix} \xrightarrow{Z^{(1)} = X(W^{(1)})^\top + \mathbf{1}(b^{(1)})^\top} \begin{bmatrix} 0 & -1 \\ 1 & 0 \\ 1 & 0 \\ 2 & 1 \end{bmatrix} \xrightarrow{H = \operatorname{ReLU}(Z^{(1)})} \begin{bmatrix} 0 & 0 \\ 1 & 0 \\ 1 & 0 \\ 2 & 1 \end{bmatrix} \xrightarrow{\hat{y} = H(W^{(2)})^\top} \begin{bmatrix} 0 \\ 1 \\ 1 \\ 0 \end{bmatrix}.$$

    Notice what the hidden layer did geometrically: because $h_2 = \max(0, x_1 + x_2 - 1)$ is zero on three of the four corners and only turns on at $(1,1)$, it creates a piecewise-linear hinge along the line $x_1 + x_2 = 1$, folding the $(1,1)$ corner out of the way so a single linear readout can separate the classes.

    5. Questions worth answering

    What happens in the XOR hidden layer if we remove the ReLU activation and use an identity activation $h = W^{(1)}x + b^{(1)}$?

    The two layers collapse into a single affine map $\hat{y} = W^{(2)}(W^{(1)}x + b^{(1)}) + b^{(2)} = (W^{(2)}W^{(1)})x + (W^{(2)}b^{(1)} + b^{(2)})$. Because an affine transformation preserves collinearity and convexity, the midpoint $(0.5, 0.5)$ of $(0,0)$ and $(1,1)$ still maps to the exact midpoint of the images of $(0,1)$ and $(1,0)$, so XOR remains linearly inseparable regardless of how many linear layers or hidden units you stack.

    Does Novikoff’s Perceptron convergence bound depend on the input dimension $d$?

    Remarkably, the bound $K \le R^2 / \gamma^2$ is dimension-free! It depends only on the geometric margin $\gamma$ and the radius $R$ of the data—a foundational result that later inspired maximum-margin Support Vector Machines (SVMs) and norm-based generalization bounds in deep networks.
