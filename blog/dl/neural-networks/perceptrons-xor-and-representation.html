    From Perceptrons to Deep Representations: Half-Spaces, the XOR Obstruction, and Feature Space Folding

    October 2026 · 14 min read

    Why can’t a single layer of neurons solve every classification problem? A single artificial neuron slices its input space $\mathbb{R}^d$ with a single flat hyperplane into two half-spaces. When classes are linearly separable, Rosenblatt’s Perceptron finds a separating hyperplane in at most $R^2/\gamma^2$ mistakes; when classes are intertwined—as in the parity problem XOR—a single hyperplane is provably impossible, and hidden layers must learn to fold the input space so non-adjacent regions land on the same side of a final linear boundary.

    1. The artificial neuron and half-space geometry

    McCulloch and Pitts (1943) and Frank Rosenblatt (1958) formulated the Perceptron as a linear threshold unit over an input vector $x = (x_1, \dots, x_d)^\top \in \mathbb{R}^d$ with weight vector $w = (w_1, \dots, w_d)^\top \in \mathbb{R}^d$ and scalar bias $b \in \mathbb{R}$:

    $$z(x) = w^\top x + b = \sum_{j=1}^d w_j x_j + b, \qquad \hat{y}(x) = \operatorname{sign}\bigl(z(x)\bigr) = \begin{cases} +1, & w^\top x + b \ge 0, \\ -1, & w^\top x + b < 0. \end{cases}$$

    Geometrically, the level set $H = \{x \in \mathbb{R}^d : w^\top x + b = 0\}$ is an affine hyperplane of dimension $d - 1$:

    • Normal vector $w$: For any two points $x, x' \in H$, $w^\top(x - x') = 0$, so $w$ is orthogonal to the hyperplane and points into the positive half-space $\mathcal{H}_+ = \{x : w^\top x + b > 0\}$.
    • Signed Euclidean distance to the boundary: The orthogonal projection of any point $x \in \mathbb{R}^d$ onto $H$ has signed distance
      $$\operatorname{dist}_{\text{signed}}(x, H) = \frac{w^\top x + b}{\|w\|_2}, \qquad \operatorname{dist}(0, H) = \frac{|b|}{\|w\|_2}.$$

    2. Rosenblatt’s Perceptron learning rule and Novikoff’s Convergence Theorem

    Absorbing the bias into an augmented vector $\tilde{x} = [x^\top, 1]^\top \in \mathbb{R}^{d+1}$ and $\tilde{w} = [w^\top, b]^\top \in \mathbb{R}^{d+1}$ (hereafter written simply as $x$ and $w$), suppose we have a training set $\mathcal{D} = \{(x^{(i)}, y^{(i)})\}_{i=1}^n$ with labels $y^{(i)} \in \{-1, +1\}$. Starting at $w^{(0)} = 0$, whenever the Perceptron misclassifies sample $(x^{(i)}, y^{(i)})$ at step $k$ (meaning $y^{(i)} (w^{(k)})^\top x^{(i)} \le 0$), it updates:

    $$w^{(k+1)} = w^{(k)} + y^{(i)} x^{(i)}.$$

    Notice why this works intuitively: after the update, the new score on that same example increases by $(w^{(k+1)})^\top (y^{(i)} x^{(i)}) = (w^{(k)})^\top (y^{(i)} x^{(i)}) + \|x^{(i)}\|_2^2$. Novikoff (1962) proved that if the dataset is linearly separable with margin $\gamma > 0$, this mistake-driven loop must halt in a finite, dimension-free number of updates.

    Theorem (Novikoff’s Perceptron Convergence Theorem, 1962).

    Suppose there exists a unit-norm separator $w^\star \in \mathbb{R}^{d+1}$ ($\|w^\star\|_2 = 1$) and a margin $\gamma > 0$ such that $y^{(i)} (w^\star)^\top x^{(i)} \ge \gamma$ for all $i \in \{1, \dots, n\}$, and let $R = \max_i \|x^{(i)}\|_2$. Then the Perceptron algorithm starting from $w^{(0)} = 0$ makes at most

    $$K \le \frac{R^2}{\gamma^2}$$

    mistakes before correctly classifying every example in $\mathcal{D}$.

    Complete proof: Bound the alignment $(w^{(k)})^\top w^\star$ from below and the squared norm $\|w^{(k)}\|_2^2$ from above after $k$ mistakes:

    1. Linear growth of alignment with $w^\star$: On the $k$-th mistake $(x^{(i)}, y^{(i)})$, taking the inner product with $w^\star$ gives
      $$\bigl(w^{(k)}\bigr)^\top w^\star = \bigl(w^{(k-1)} + y^{(i)} x^{(i)}\bigr)^\top w^\star = \bigl(w^{(k-1)}\bigr)^\top w^\star + \underbrace{y^{(i)} (w^\star)^\top x^{(i)}}_{\ge \gamma} \ge \bigl(w^{(k-1)}\bigr)^\top w^\star + \gamma.$$
      By induction from $w^{(0)} = 0$, after $K$ mistakes we have $\bigl(w^{(K)}\bigr)^\top w^\star \ge K\gamma$.
    2. Linear growth of squared Euclidean norm $\|w^{(K)}\|_2^2$: Because an update only occurs when $y^{(i)} (w^{(k-1)})^\top x^{(i)} \le 0$, expanding the squared norm yields
      $$\bigl\|w^{(k)}\bigr\|_2^2 = \bigl\|w^{(k-1)} + y^{(i)} x^{(i)}\bigr\|_2^2 = \bigl\|w^{(k-1)}\bigr\|_2^2 + \underbrace{2 y^{(i)} (w^{(k-1)})^\top x^{(i)}}_{\le 0} + \underbrace{\bigl\|x^{(i)}\bigr\|_2^2}_{\le R^2} \le \bigl\|w^{(k-1)}\bigr\|_2^2 + R^2.$$
      By induction from $w^{(0)} = 0$, after $K$ mistakes we have $\bigl\|w^{(K)}\bigr\|_2^2 \le K R^2$, or $\bigl\|w^{(K)}\bigr\|_2 \le \sqrt{K}\,R$.
    3. Combining via Cauchy–Schwarz: Since $\|w^\star\|_2 = 1$, Cauchy–Schwarz gives
      $$K\gamma \le \bigl(w^{(K)}\bigr)^\top w^\star \le \bigl\|w^{(K)}\bigr\|_2 \|w^\star\|_2 \le \sqrt{K}\,R \quad \Longrightarrow \quad K^2 \gamma^2 \le K R^2 \quad \Longrightarrow \quad K \le \frac{R^2}{\gamma^2}.\quad\square$$

    3. Minsky & Papert’s XOR obstruction: algebraic proof of linear inseparability

    In Perceptrons (1969), Marvin Minsky and Seymour Papert demonstrated that a single threshold neuron can represent $\operatorname{AND}$ ($x_1 + x_2 - 1.5 \ge 0$), $\operatorname{OR}$ ($x_1 + x_2 - 0.5 \ge 0$), and $\operatorname{NAND}$ ($-x_1 - x_2 + 1.5 \ge 0$), but cannot represent $\operatorname{XOR}$ (exclusive-OR / 2-bit parity).

    Algebraic proof by contradiction: Suppose there exist weights $w_1, w_2 \in \mathbb{R}$ and bias $b \in \mathbb{R}$ that separate the four corners of $\{0, 1\}^2$ according to $\operatorname{XOR}(x_1, x_2) = x_1 \oplus x_2$:

    $$\begin{aligned} (0, 0) \mapsto y = 0: &\quad w_1(0) + w_2(0) + b < 0 &&\implies b < 0, &\quad (1) \\ (1, 0) \mapsto y = 1: &\quad w_1(1) + w_2(0) + b \ge 0 &&\implies w_1 + b \ge 0, &\quad (2) \\ (0, 1) \mapsto y = 1: &\quad w_1(0) + w_2(1) + b \ge 0 &&\implies w_2 + b \ge 0, &\quad (3) \\ (1, 1) \mapsto y = 0: &\quad w_1(1) + w_2(1) + b < 0 &&\implies w_1 + w_2 + b < 0. &\quad (4) \end{aligned}$$

    Adding inequalities $(2)$ and $(3)$ gives $(w_1 + b) + (w_2 + b) \ge 0$, which rearranges to $w_1 + w_2 + b \ge -b$. Since $(1)$ states $b < 0$, we have $-b > 0$, forcing $w_1 + w_2 + b > 0$—a direct contradiction of $(4)$ ($w_1 + w_2 + b < 0$)!

    Geometrically, any half-space is a convex set. The line segment connecting the two positive corners $(1,0)$ and $(0,1)$ intersects the line segment connecting the two negative corners $(0,0)$ and $(1,1)$ at their common midpoint $(0.5, 0.5)$. Because the convex hulls of the two classes intersect, no single hyperplane in $\mathbb{R}^2$ can ever separate them.

    4. Solving XOR with a 2-layer network: how hidden layers fold feature space

    While a single layer cannot separate XOR in the raw coordinates $(x_1, x_2)$, a 2-layer network with a hidden ReLU layer $h = \operatorname{ReLU}\bigl(W^{(1)} x + b^{(1)}\bigr) \in \mathbb{R}^2$ maps the four corners into a new coordinate space $(h_1, h_2)$ where the classes are linearly separable:

    $$W^{(1)} = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 1 \\ 1 & 1 \end{bmatrix}, \quad b^{(1)} = \begin{bmatrix} 0 \\ -1 \end{bmatrix}, \quad W^{(2)} = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & -2 \end{bmatrix}, \quad b^{(2)} = 0.$$

    Evaluating all $B = 4$ points simultaneously as a batch matrix $X \in \mathbb{R}^{4 \times 2}$:

    $$X = \begin{bmatrix} 0 & 0 \\ 0 & 1 \\ 1 & 0 \\ 1 & 1 \end{bmatrix} \xrightarrow{Z^{(1)} = X(W^{(1)})^\top + \mathbf{1}(b^{(1)})^\top} \begin{bmatrix} 0 & -1 \\ 1 & 0 \\ 1 & 0 \\ 2 & 1 \end{bmatrix} \xrightarrow{H = \operatorname{ReLU}(Z^{(1)})} \begin{bmatrix} 0 & 0 \\ 1 & 0 \\ 1 & 0 \\ 2 & 1 \end{bmatrix} \xrightarrow{\hat{y} = H(W^{(2)})^\top} \begin{bmatrix} 0 \\ 1 \\ 1 \\ 0 \end{bmatrix}.$$

    5. Questions worth answering

    What happens in the XOR network if we replace $\operatorname{ReLU}$ with an identity activation $h = W^{(1)}x + b^{(1)}$?

    Without a nonlinearity, $\hat{y} = W^{(2)}(W^{(1)}x + b^{(1)}) = (W^{(2)}W^{(1)})x + W^{(2)}b^{(1)}$ collapses back into a single affine map (see Why Neural Networks Need Activation Functions). Because affine maps preserve midpoints, the midpoint $(0.5, 0.5)$ of $(0,0)$ and $(1,1)$ still coincides with the midpoint of $(0,1)$ and $(1,0)$, so XOR remains linearly inseparable.

    Does Novikoff’s Perceptron convergence bound $K \le R^2 / \gamma^2$ depend on the input dimension $d$?

    No—the bound is completely dimension-free! It depends only on the geometric margin $\gamma$ and the data radius $R$, a result that directly motivated maximum-margin Support Vector Machines and norm-based capacity bounds in deep learning.
