    Perceptrons, the XOR Problem, and Hidden Layers

    October 2026 · 7 min read

    A single neuron splits input space with one flat hyperplane. It can separate AND or OR, but it cannot separate XOR. Adding a hidden layer bends and folds the input space so that XOR becomes linearly separable.

    1. One neuron defines one hyperplane

    A single neuron takes an input vector $x = (x_1, \dots, x_d)^\top \in \mathbb{R}^d$, computes a weighted sum plus bias $z = w^\top x + b$, and passes it through an activation $\phi(z)$:

    $$z(x) = w^\top x + b = \sum_{j=1}^d w_j x_j + b, \qquad \hat{y}(x) = \phi\bigl(z(x)\bigr).$$

    The boundary $H = \{x \in \mathbb{R}^d : w^\top x + b = 0\}$ splits $\mathbb{R}^d$ into two half-spaces: $w^\top x + b > 0$ and $w^\top x + b < 0$. The weight vector $w$ is orthogonal to $H$, and the signed distance from any point $x$ to $H$ is $(w^\top x + b)/\|w\|_2$.

    2. Why one neuron cannot solve XOR

    A single threshold neuron can represent $\operatorname{AND}$ ($x_1 + x_2 - 1.5 \ge 0$) and $\operatorname{OR}$ ($x_1 + x_2 - 0.5 \ge 0$), but it cannot represent $\operatorname{XOR}$ ($x_1 \oplus x_2$).

    Algebraic proof: Suppose weights $w_1, w_2$ and bias $b$ separate the four corners of $\{0, 1\}^2$:

    $$\begin{aligned} (0, 0) \mapsto y = 0: &\quad b < 0, &\quad (1) \\ (1, 0) \mapsto y = 1: &\quad w_1 + b \ge 0, &\quad (2) \\ (0, 1) \mapsto y = 1: &\quad w_2 + b \ge 0, &\quad (3) \\ (1, 1) \mapsto y = 0: &\quad w_1 + w_2 + b < 0. &\quad (4) \end{aligned}$$

    Adding $(2)$ and $(3)$ gives $w_1 + w_2 + 2b \ge 0 \implies w_1 + w_2 + b \ge -b$. Since $(1)$ requires $b < 0$, we have $-b > 0$, so $w_1 + w_2 + b > 0$—contradicting $(4)$.

    Geometrically, any half-space is convex. The segment connecting the positive corners $(1,0)$ and $(0,1)$ crosses the segment connecting the negative corners $(0,0)$ and $(1,1)$ at $(0.5, 0.5)$, so no single line can separate them.

    3. Solving XOR with a 2-layer ReLU network

    Adding one hidden ReLU layer $h = \operatorname{ReLU}\bigl(W^{(1)} x + b^{(1)}\bigr) \in \mathbb{R}^2$ maps the four corners into a new coordinate space $(h_1, h_2)$ where they are linearly separable:

    $$W^{(1)} = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 1 \\ 1 & 1 \end{bmatrix}, \quad b^{(1)} = \begin{bmatrix} 0 \\ -1 \end{bmatrix}, \quad W^{(2)} = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & -2 \end{bmatrix}, \quad b^{(2)} = 0.$$

    Evaluating all four inputs $X \in \mathbb{R}^{4 \times 2}$ at once:

    $$X = \begin{bmatrix} 0 & 0 \\ 0 & 1 \\ 1 & 0 \\ 1 & 1 \end{bmatrix}, \qquad Z^{(1)} = X\bigl(W^{(1)}\bigr)^\top + \mathbf{1}\bigl(b^{(1)}\bigr)^\top = \begin{bmatrix} 0 & -1 \\ 1 & 0 \\ 1 & 0 \\ 2 & 1 \end{bmatrix}, \qquad H = \operatorname{ReLU}\!\bigl(Z^{(1)}\bigr) = \begin{bmatrix} 0 & 0 \\ 1 & 0 \\ 1 & 0 \\ 2 & 1 \end{bmatrix}, \qquad \hat{y} = H\bigl(W^{(2)}\bigr)^\top = \begin{bmatrix} 0 \\ 1 \\ 1 \\ 0 \end{bmatrix}.$$

    4. Key takeaway

    What happens in the XOR network if we replace $\operatorname{ReLU}$ with an identity activation $h = W^{(1)}x + b^{(1)}$?

    Without a nonlinearity, $\hat{y} = W^{(2)}(W^{(1)}x + b^{(1)}) = (W^{(2)}W^{(1)})x + W^{(2)}b^{(1)}$ collapses into a single linear layer (see Why Neural Networks Need Activation Functions), which still cannot separate XOR.
