    Why TD Often Learns Faster than Monte Carlo

    August 2026 · 18 min read · Sample-based Learning Methods, Week 3

    Same objective, different target. Both methods estimate $v_\pi$ from sampled experience. Their difference is entirely in what each update treats as the answer.

    The two updates side by side

    Monte Carlo

    $$V(S_t)\leftarrow V(S_t)+\alpha[G_t-V(S_t)]$$

    Target: the complete observed return. No bootstrapping; must wait for termination.

    TD(0)

    $$\begin{aligned}V(S_t)\leftarrow{}&V(S_t)+\alpha\big[R_{t+1}\\&+\gamma V(S_{t+1})-V(S_t)\big]\end{aligned}$$

    Target: one reward plus a successor estimate. Bootstraps; updates immediately.

    TD combines one property from DP and one from MC

    MethodLearns from experience?Needs a model?Bootstraps?Waits for termination?
    Dynamic programmingNoYesYesNo
    Monte CarloYesNoNoYes
    TDYesNoYesNo

    Why the TD target is less noisy

    $G_t$ contains every random action, reward, and transition remaining in the episode. A single early-state return can vary enormously across episodes. The TD target includes only one sampled transition; the rest is replaced by the smoother estimate $V(S_{t+1})$.

    This usually reduces variance, but the estimate can be wrong. TD trades some target bias for lower variance and faster reuse of information.

    The random-walk benchmark

    Consider five non-terminal states $A$ through $E$ between two terminal states. Entering the left terminal gives reward $0$; entering the right terminal gives reward $+1$. Both terminal state values are zero because no rewards occur after termination. Each move goes left or right with equal probability, so the non-terminal values are the probabilities of eventually reaching the right terminal:

    $$v_\pi(A),\ldots,v_\pi(E)=\left(\frac16,\frac26,\frac36,\frac46,\frac56\right).$$

    Online learning changes the data efficiency

    A continuing task may run for millions of steps without a natural endpoint. Vanilla MC cannot update because $G_t$ is never complete. TD remains well-defined after every transition.

    Even in episodic problems, updates made during the episode can influence later decisions and later estimates before another full trajectory is collected.

    What happens under repeated batch updates?

    If a fixed dataset is replayed until convergence, Monte Carlo converges to the empirical mean return observed after each state. TD converges to values that satisfy Bellman equations for the empirical one-step transition model contained in the data.

    The TD solution can be more data-efficient because it composes observed transitions. A transition from $A$ to $B$ can benefit from information learned about $B$ in entirely different episodes.

    When to prefer each estimator

    SituationUsually preferableReason
    Long or continuing episodesTDUpdates do not wait for termination.
    Successor values already usefulTDBootstrapping propagates information quickly.
    Short episodes and trustworthy complete outcomesMCThe target contains no value-estimate bias.
    Very inaccurate initial value estimatesMC can be safer earlyTD initially bootstraps from poor predictions.
    Need learning during interactionTDOne transition is sufficient.

    What TD does not solve

    • A poor representation can still make different situations look identical.
    • A bad step size can produce slow learning or instability.
    • Prediction still assumes experience from the policy being evaluated unless off-policy correction is added.
    • State values do not directly choose actions; Week 4 moves TD from prediction to control with action values.
