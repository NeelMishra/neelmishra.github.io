    Off-Policy Monte Carlo & Importance Sampling

    August 2026 · 24 min read · Coursera: Sample-based Learning Methods (University of Alberta), Week 2

    Can exploration be delegated? On-policy MC control forces the policy being learned to remain $\varepsilon$-soft. Off-policy learning separates the two jobs: one policy behaves and explores; another policy is evaluated or improved. Importance sampling is the correction that makes data from one distribution speak about the other.

    Target policy and behaviour policy

    Target policy $\pi$

    The policy whose value we want to estimate or whose behaviour we ultimately want. It may be greedy and deterministic.

    Behaviour policy $b$

    The policy that actually selects actions and generates the dataset. It is usually broader and deliberately exploratory.

    On-policy learning is the special case $b=\pi$. Off-policy learning allows $b\ne\pi$. This decoupling is useful far beyond exploration: one can learn from demonstrations, logs collected by an older system, multiple workers using different behaviours, or a replay buffer containing past policies.

    Coverage is non-negotiable

    For every state and action, the behaviour must cover the support of the target:

    $$ \boxed{\,\pi(a\mid s)>0\quad\Longrightarrow\quad b(a\mid s)>0\,}. $$

    If the target can choose an action that the behaviour never chooses, no amount of reweighting can invent the missing outcome. The probabilities need not match; they merely need the right support.

    One-way condition: $b$ may take actions that $\pi$ never takes. Those samples receive zero target weight. The forbidden case is the reverse: positive target probability divided by zero behaviour probability.

    Importance sampling from first principles

    Start with a random variable $X$. We want an expectation under target distribution $\pi$, but samples arrive from behaviour distribution $b$:

    $$ \mathbb E_\pi[f(X)]=\sum_x \pi(x)f(x). $$

    Multiply and divide by $b(x)$ wherever $\pi(x)>0$:

    $$ \begin{aligned} \mathbb E_\pi[f(X)] &=\sum_x b(x)\frac{\pi(x)}{b(x)}f(x)\\ &=\mathbb E_b\!\left[\rho(X)f(X)\right], \qquad \rho(x)=\frac{\pi(x)}{b(x)}. \end{aligned} $$

    The ratio does not change which samples were observed. It changes how loudly each one speaks. Events over-produced by $b$ get $\rho<1$; events under-produced by $b$ get $\rho>1$.

    The notes’ four-outcome example

    Let $X\in\{1,2,3,4\}$. The behaviour distribution heavily favours 1, while the target favours 2:

    Behaviour $b$

    10.85
    20.05
    30.05
    40.05

    Target $\pi$

    10.30
    20.40
    30.10
    40.20

    The true target mean is

    $$ \mathbb E_\pi[X]=1(0.3)+2(0.4)+3(0.1)+4(0.2)=2.2. $$

    Draw samples from $b$ below. A common $X=1$ gets ratio $0.3/0.85\approx0.353$. A rare $X=2$ gets ratio $0.4/0.05=8$. That rare event can swing the estimate violently — the defining variance problem of importance sampling.

    From one variable to a trajectory

    For the partial trajectory after state $S_t$ under policy $\pi$, the probability is

    $$ \Pr_\pi(\tau_{t:T}\mid S_t) =\prod_{k=t}^{T-1} \pi(A_k\mid S_k)\, p(S_{k+1},R_{k+1}\mid S_k,A_k). $$

    The same trajectory under $b$ has the same environment factors and different action probabilities. Their ratio is

    $$ \begin{aligned} \rho_{t:T-1} &=\frac{\Pr_\pi(\tau_{t:T}\mid S_t)} {\Pr_b(\tau_{t:T}\mid S_t)}\\ &=\prod_{k=t}^{T-1} \frac{\pi(A_k\mid S_k)\, \cancel{p(S_{k+1},R_{k+1}\mid S_k,A_k)}} {b(A_k\mid S_k)\, \cancel{p(S_{k+1},R_{k+1}\mid S_k,A_k)}}\\ &=\boxed{\prod_{k=t}^{T-1} \frac{\pi(A_k\mid S_k)}{b(A_k\mid S_k)}}. \end{aligned} $$

    The dynamics cancel because the environment responds the same way once a state and action are fixed. Off-policy correction needs policy probabilities, not a model of the environment.

    Ratios multiply. A mild per-step mismatch becomes a large trajectory mismatch over a long horizon. Ten ratios of 2 produce a weight of 1024; ten ratios of $1/2$ produce about 0.001. This is why long-horizon ordinary importance sampling can have brutal variance.

    Correcting Monte Carlo returns

    Target-policy value can now be written as an expectation under the behaviour policy:

    $$ \boxed{\,v_\pi(s) =\mathbb E_b\!\left[\rho_{t:T-1}G_t\mid S_t=s\right]\,}. $$

    Each return generated by $b$ is multiplied by the probability ratio of the decisions that followed that state. A trajectory that $\pi$ considers more likely than $b$ is amplified; one that $\pi$ considers less likely is suppressed.

    Ordinary versus weighted importance sampling

    Ordinary importance sampling

    $$V_n(s)=\frac1n\sum_{i=1}^{n}\rho_iG_i.$$

    Unbiased when the required expectations exist, but a single enormous ratio can dominate the estimate. Even bounded returns can yield unbounded weighted samples.

    Weighted importance sampling

    $$V_n(s)=\frac{\sum_{i=1}^{n}\rho_iG_i}{\sum_{i=1}^{n}\rho_i}.$$

    Biased at finite sample sizes but consistent, typically much lower variance, and bounded between observed returns when weights are non-negative.

    The interactive example reports both. Ordinary IS divides by the number of behaviour samples. Weighted IS normalises by the total target weight those samples carry.

    Off-policy every-visit MC prediction

    The reverse pass can compute returns and trajectory ratios together. The ordering matters: for a state value at time $t$, the ratio includes action $A_t$, so update $W$ before using the sample.

    input: target policy pi, behaviour policy b with coverage
initialise V(s) arbitrarily
initialise C(s) = 0                    # cumulative weight

for each episode generated by b:
    G = 0
    W = 1

    for t = T-1, T-2, ..., 0:
        G = gamma * G + R(t+1)
        W = W * pi(A(t)|S(t)) / b(A(t)|S(t))

        C(S(t)) += W
        if C(S(t)) > 0:
            V(S(t)) += W / C(S(t)) * (G - V(S(t)))

        if W == 0:
            break

return V

    This is the incremental weighted estimator. For ordinary importance sampling, replace the cumulative-weight update by a visit count and average the samples $WG$:

    $$ N(s)\leftarrow N(s)+1,\qquad V(s)\leftarrow V(s)+\frac{1}{N(s)}\big[WG-V(s)\big]. $$

    Why deterministic targets create zeros

    If $\pi$ is deterministic and the behaviour takes any non-target action, then $\pi(A_k\mid S_k)=0$. The entire ratio for all earlier states becomes zero. Their returns tell us nothing about what would have happened under the deterministic target, because the trajectory already diverged.

    This is correct but statistically harsh: many exploratory episodes contribute zero, while the rare episodes that match the target for a long time may receive large weights. Off-policy learning gains flexibility by paying in variance.

    Common mistakes

    • Reversing the ratio. The correction is target over behaviour, $\pi/b$, because samples are drawn from $b$ and must be transformed into a $\pi$ expectation.
    • Multiplying transition probabilities. They appear in both trajectory distributions and cancel.
    • Ignoring coverage. A ratio cannot repair an action that $b$ never takes.
    • Using only the current action ratio. A complete return depends on every later policy decision, so the trajectory ratio is a product through $T-1$.
    • Calling weighted IS unbiased. Its lower variance comes with finite-sample bias; its guarantee is consistency.

    Monte Carlo methods have now removed the model from prediction and control. They still wait for complete episodes and suffer from return variance. The next technical step in sample-based learning is to update before termination by combining sampling with bootstrapping.
