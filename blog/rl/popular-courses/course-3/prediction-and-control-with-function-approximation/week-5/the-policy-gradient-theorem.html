    The Policy Gradient Theorem

    September 2026 · 22 min read · Prediction and Control with Function Approximation, Week 5

    The term that blocked us disappears. The policy gradient theorem rewrites $\nabla r(\pi)$ as a $\mu$-weighted sum of policy gradients times action values. The stationary distribution still appears as a weighting — which we can sample — but its derivative never does.

    Where we were stuck

    The objective is a product of two things that both depend on $\boldsymbol\theta$, so the product rule splits its gradient in two:

    $$\nabla r(\pi)=\underbrace{\sum_s\big[\nabla\mu(s)\big]\sum_a\pi(a\mid s,\boldsymbol\theta)\sum_{s',r}p(s',r\mid s,a)r}_{\text{needs }\nabla\mu\text{: not sampleable}}\;+\;\underbrace{\sum_s\mu(s)\sum_a\big[\nabla\pi(a\mid s,\boldsymbol\theta)\big]\sum_{s',r}p(s',r\mid s,a)r}_{\text{fine}}.$$

    The second term we could estimate today: follow the policy, and the states you visit are already drawn from $\mu$. The first term is hopeless by direct attack. $\mu$ is defined implicitly, as the fixed point of the chain the policy induces, so $\nabla\mu(s)$ measures how a nudge to $\boldsymbol\theta$ reshuffles the agent's long-run occupancy of the entire state space. There is no sample of that.

    The theorem

    Policy gradient theorem (average reward). For a differentiable policy parameterization $\pi(a\mid s,\boldsymbol\theta)$ with steady-state distribution $\mu$, $$\nabla r(\pi)=\sum_s\mu(s)\sum_a\nabla\pi(a\mid s,\boldsymbol\theta)\,q_\pi(s,a).$$

    Compare it line by line with the expansion above and two things changed:

    1

    No $\nabla\mu$ anywhere. $\mu(s)$ survives only as a weight on the states. Since following the policy generates states distributed according to $\mu$, that weighting is exactly what sampling gives us for free.

    2

    Immediate reward became the action value. Where the naive term had $\mathbb E[R\mid s,a]$, the theorem has $q_\pi(s,a)$: the whole future, not one step. That is not a coincidence, it is the accounting. Changing $\pi$ in state $s$ shifts where the agent goes next, and the value of those consequences is precisely what $q_\pi$ measures. The intractable distribution term is not dropped — it is absorbed into the action values.

    The proof is a telescoping argument on $\nabla v_\pi$ and is worked out in the textbook; the useful part here is what the expression says, because every algorithm in the rest of this week is a way of sampling it.

    The episodic version carries a constant. In the episodic setting the same identity holds up to a factor proportional to the average episode length, and $\mu$ becomes the on-policy distribution of states within an episode. The constant can be folded into the step size, which is why the practical update looks identical in both settings.

    Reading $\nabla\pi(a\mid s,\boldsymbol\theta)$

    Take the innermost object first. For a fixed state and a fixed action, $\nabla\pi(a\mid s,\boldsymbol\theta)$ is a vector in parameter space: the direction to move $\boldsymbol\theta$ that most rapidly increases the probability of choosing $a$ in $s$. It exists as soon as the parameterization is differentiable, and for a softmax over preferences it is a short calculation rather than anything exotic.

    One structural fact makes everything else easier to read. Probabilities in a state must sum to one for every $\boldsymbol\theta$, so differentiating that constant gives

    $$\sum_a\nabla\pi(a\mid s,\boldsymbol\theta)=\nabla\sum_a\pi(a\mid s,\boldsymbol\theta)=\nabla 1=\mathbf 0.$$
    Probability is conserved. The individual gradients in a state cancel exactly. You cannot make one action more likely without making the others collectively less likely by the same amount — the policy update redistributes probability, it never creates it.

    Reading the weighted sum over actions

    Now the inner sum of the theorem, for one state:

    $$\mathbf d(s)\doteq\sum_a\nabla\pi(a\mid s,\boldsymbol\theta)\,q_\pi(s,a).$$

    Each action contributes its own "make me more likely" direction, scaled by how good that action is. Positive $q_\pi(s,a)$ pushes along that direction; negative $q_\pi(s,a)$ pushes against it, which means making that action less likely. The result is one vector saying how to reshuffle this state's action probabilities to raise the value collected from it.

    Concretely, put the agent in the middle of a small gridworld with a rewarding cell to the bottom-right. Up and left move away from it, so their action values are negative; down and right move towards it, so theirs are positive. The weighted sum then points in the direction that thins out up and left and thickens down and right — not by editing probabilities directly, but by moving $\boldsymbol\theta$.

    The baseline that changes nothing

    The zero-sum property has a consequence worth isolating, because it is the seed of every variance-reduction trick later in this course and in the PPO series. Let $b(s)$ be any quantity that does not depend on the action. Then

    $$\sum_a\nabla\pi(a\mid s,\boldsymbol\theta)\big[q_\pi(s,a)-b(s)\big]=\sum_a\nabla\pi(a\mid s,\boldsymbol\theta)\,q_\pi(s,a)-b(s)\underbrace{\sum_a\nabla\pi(a\mid s,\boldsymbol\theta)}_{=\ \mathbf 0}=\mathbf d(s).$$

    Shifting all action values in a state by a common amount leaves the gradient untouched. Try it with the baseline slider above: the arrow does not move. What matters is not whether an action is good in absolute terms, but whether it is better than the alternatives in that state. Choosing $b(s)=v_\pi(s)$ turns the weight into the advantage $q_\pi(s,a)-v_\pi(s)$, which is exactly what an actor-critic agent will use.

    Invariance is exact only for the full sum. Once we replace the sum over actions by a single sampled action, the baseline stops cancelling term by term — it cancels only in expectation. That is precisely why it is useful: the estimator keeps the same mean and gets a smaller variance.

    Putting the states back together

    The theorem finishes by averaging $\mathbf d(s)$ over the states the policy actually visits:

    $$\nabla r(\pi)=\sum_s\mu(s)\,\mathbf d(s)=\mathbb E_{\mu}\Big[\sum_a\nabla\pi(a\mid S,\boldsymbol\theta)\,q_\pi(S,a)\Big].$$

    Each state proposes a direction; states the agent occupies often get more say. The sum of those votes is the direction in which the policy's long-run reward rate increases fastest.

    What is left to do. The expression is exact but not yet an algorithm: it contains a sum over all states weighted by an unknown $\mu$, a sum over all actions, and the true $q_\pi$. Sampling states by following the policy handles the first, a sampling trick handles the second, and a learned critic handles the third. That is the next post.
