    The Objective for Learning Policies

    September 2026 · 20 min read · Prediction and Control with Function Approximation, Week 5

    The objective is the goal itself. Value-based methods optimized a proxy — squared error against a target — and hoped a good policy fell out. With a parameterized policy we can optimize the thing we actually care about: the long-run rate of reward, $r(\pi)$. The catch is that the distribution of states we average over moves whenever the policy moves.

    Which long-run reward?

    "As much reward as possible in the long run" has to be made precise before it can be differentiated. Three formulations are on the table, and they are the same three from earlier weeks:

    SettingReturnExtra choice it forces
    Episodic$G_t=\sum_{k=0}^{T}R_{t+k}$needs a terminal state; performance depends on the start distribution
    Continuing, discounted$G_t=\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\gamma^{k}R_{t+k}$you must pick $\gamma$, which silently picks a horizon
    Continuing, average reward$G_t=\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\big(R_{t+k}-r(\pi)\big)$none beyond the policy itself

    This week works entirely in the third row. The reason is the one from week 4: in a continuing task the discount factor is not a property of the problem but a knob we introduce, and different values of it can order two policies differently. The average reward objective compares policies by the rate of reward they sustain forever, so nothing about the ranking depends on a horizon we invented.

    The average reward objective

    Objective. For a policy $\pi_{\boldsymbol\theta}$ inducing steady-state distribution $\mu$, $$r(\pi)\doteq\sum_{s}\mu(s)\sum_{a}\pi(a\mid s,\boldsymbol\theta)\sum_{s',r}p(s',r\mid s,a)\,r.$$

    Read it from the inside out and it is three ordinary expectations nested inside one another.

    1

    Innermost: the expected reward of one state-action pair.

    $$\sum_{s',r}p(s',r\mid s,a)\,r=\mathbb E[R_t\mid S_t=s,A_t=a].$$

    This part belongs to the environment. No policy parameters appear in it, which is why it will pass through the gradient untouched.

    2

    Middle: average over the actions the policy would take.

    $$\sum_a\pi(a\mid s,\boldsymbol\theta)\,\mathbb E[R_t\mid S_t=s,A_t=a]=\mathbb E_\pi[R_t\mid S_t=s].$$

    This is where $\boldsymbol\theta$ enters explicitly, and it is the term the policy gradient theorem will eventually keep.

    3

    Outermost: average over where the agent actually spends its time.

    $$r(\pi)=\sum_s\mu(s)\,\mathbb E_\pi[R_t\mid S_t=s]=\mathbb E_{\mu,\pi}[R].$$

    $\mu$ is the steady-state distribution of the Markov chain induced by $\pi_{\boldsymbol\theta}$: the fraction of time the agent occupies each state once transients have died out. It is characterized by

    $$\mu(s')=\sum_s\mu(s)\sum_a\pi(a\mid s,\boldsymbol\theta)\,p(s'\mid s,a)\quad\text{for all }s'.$$

    $\boldsymbol\theta$ enters here too, implicitly, through that fixed-point equation. That is the whole difficulty of this week in one line.

    One sentence summary: $r(\pi)$ is the reward per step you would measure by running $\pi_{\boldsymbol\theta}$ forever and taking the average. In week 4 we estimated this quantity online, as the $\bar R$ baseline inside differential Sarsa. Now it is the score we are trying to maximize.

    Learning as gradient ascent

    Every earlier update in this course descended an error. This one ascends a reward:

    $$\boxed{\ \boldsymbol\theta_{t+1}\doteq\boldsymbol\theta_t+\alpha\,\widehat{\nabla r(\pi)}\ }$$

    Value learning (descent)

    $$\mathbf w\leftarrow\mathbf w-\tfrac{\alpha}{2}\nabla\overline{VE}(\mathbf w)$$

    Minimize an error, so step against the gradient. The target is external and the state distribution $\mu$ is fixed by the policy being evaluated.

    Policy learning (ascent)

    $$\boldsymbol\theta\leftarrow\boldsymbol\theta+\alpha\nabla r(\pi_{\boldsymbol\theta})$$

    Maximize a reward rate, so step along the gradient. There is no target at all, and $\mu$ changes with every step we take.

    Methods built on this template — estimate $\nabla r(\pi)$ from experience, take a step, repeat — are the policy gradient methods. Everything left to do this week is about producing that estimate.

    A two-state example you can turn by hand

    To see what "the objective" and "the distribution moves" mean concretely, here is the smallest continuing task with a single policy parameter. Two states, two actions, deterministic transitions:

    • In $A$, action go earns $+1$ and moves the agent to $B$; action wait earns $0$ and stays in $A$.
    • In $B$, action go earns $0$ and stays in $B$; action wait earns $+2$ and returns the agent to $A$.

    The policy has one parameter $\theta$ and uses the softmax over two actions, which is just the logistic function $p=\pi(\text{go}\mid\cdot)=\dfrac{e^{\theta}}{e^{\theta}+1}$, the same $p$ in both states. Balancing the flow between the two states, $\mu(A)\,p=\mu(B)(1-p)$, gives a steady state that is as simple as it gets:

    $$\mu(A)=1-p,\qquad \mu(B)=p,\qquad r(\pi)=(1-p)\big(p\cdot 1\big)+p\big((1-p)\cdot 2\big)=3p(1-p).$$

    The best this policy class can do is $p=\tfrac12$, worth $0.75$ reward per step. Both extremes earn nothing: always waiting parks the agent in $A$ collecting zeros, always going parks it in $B$ collecting zeros. Reward here comes from cycling, and the parameter controls how fast the agent cycles.

    Because the example is tiny we could differentiate $r$ in closed form: with $p=\sigma(\theta)$ and $r=3p(1-p)$, the chain rule gives $\partial r/\partial\theta=3(1-2p)\,p(1-p)$, which is positive below $p=\tfrac12$ and negative above it. An agent cannot do that. It does not know $p(s',r\mid s,a)$, and it certainly does not know $\mu$ — the whole point is to estimate the gradient from sampled transitions.

    Why the gradient is hard: the distribution moves too

    Write the objective as a product of the two $\boldsymbol\theta$-dependent pieces and apply the product rule:

    $$\nabla_{\boldsymbol\theta}\,r(\pi)=\sum_s\underbrace{\big[\nabla_{\boldsymbol\theta}\mu(s)\big]}_{\text{hard}}\sum_a\pi(a\mid s,\boldsymbol\theta)\,\mathbb E[R\mid s,a]\;+\;\sum_s\mu(s)\sum_a\underbrace{\big[\nabla_{\boldsymbol\theta}\pi(a\mid s,\boldsymbol\theta)\big]}_{\text{easy}}\mathbb E[R\mid s,a].$$

    The second term is fine. If the policy is a differentiable parameterization — a softmax over linear preferences, a network — then $\nabla_{\boldsymbol\theta}\pi(a\mid s,\boldsymbol\theta)$ is available by ordinary differentiation, and states drawn by following the policy are already distributed according to $\mu$.

    The first term is the problem. $\mu$ is not something we wrote down; it is the fixed point of the long-run interaction between the policy and the environment, so $\nabla_{\boldsymbol\theta}\mu(s)$ asks how a small change in the parameters redistributes the agent's time over the entire state space, forever. Nothing we can sample tells us that directly.

    Value error: $\mu$ is a constant

    $$\nabla_{\mathbf w}\overline{VE}=\sum_s\mu(s)\,\nabla_{\mathbf w}\big[v_\pi(s)-\hat v(s,\mathbf w)\big]^2$$

    The policy generating the data is fixed, so $\mu$ does not depend on $\mathbf w$. It slides straight out of the gradient, and sampling states on-policy is exactly sampling from $\mu$.

    Average reward: $\mu$ depends on $\boldsymbol\theta$

    $$\nabla_{\boldsymbol\theta}\sum_s\mu(s)\,(\cdots)\ne\sum_s\mu(s)\,\nabla_{\boldsymbol\theta}(\cdots)$$

    Changing the policy changes where the agent goes, so the weighting itself has a derivative. The inequality above is precisely the extra term.

    The resolution is a theorem, not a trick. The policy gradient theorem shows that the gradient of $r(\pi)$ can be written in a form that contains $\mu$ as a weighting but never contains $\nabla\mu$. The awkward first term does not need to be approximated or ignored — it disappears from the exact expression. That is the subject of the next post.

    Check yourself

    Practice

    • State the three return formulations and say which extra choice each one forces on you. recall
    • Expand $r(\pi)$ and identify which sum corresponds to which expectation. derivation
    • Verify $\mu(A)=1-p$ and $\mu(B)=p$ for the two-state example from the balance equation. calculation
    • Differentiate $r=3p(1-p)$ with $p=\sigma(\theta)$ and confirm the sign of the gradient at $\theta=-1$ and $\theta=+1$. calculation
    • Explain why $\mu$ can be pulled out of $\nabla_{\mathbf w}\overline{VE}$ but not out of $\nabla_{\boldsymbol\theta}r(\pi)$. reasoning
    • Simulate the two-state chain for a fixed $\theta$ and check the empirical state frequencies against $\mu$. implementation
