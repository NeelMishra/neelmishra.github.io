    Learning Policies Directly

    September 2026 · 20 min read · Prediction and Control with Function Approximation, Week 5

    A different place to put the parameters. Every control method so far estimated action values and then squeezed a policy out of them with $\varepsilon$-greedy. This week the parameters live in the policy itself: $\pi(a\mid s,\boldsymbol\theta)$ is a function we learn, and action values become optional machinery rather than the object of study.

    Where the policy used to come from

    Generalized policy iteration has been the skeleton of every control algorithm in this specialization. Estimate $q_\pi$, act greedily (or nearly greedily) with respect to the estimate, repeat. Under function approximation nothing about that changed: we replaced $Q(s,a)$ with $\hat q(s,a,\mathbf w)$, but the policy was still a derived object, computed on demand from values:

    $$\mathbf w\ \longrightarrow\ \hat q(s,a,\mathbf w)\ \longrightarrow\ \pi(a\mid s)=\begin{cases}1-\varepsilon+\varepsilon/|\mathcal A| & a=\arg\max_b \hat q(s,b,\mathbf w)\\ \varepsilon/|\mathcal A| & \text{otherwise.}\end{cases}$$

    The policy has no parameters of its own. It is a fixed rule applied to whatever the value estimates happen to say. Policy gradient methods break that chain and give the policy its own parameter vector.

    A good policy that never mentions values

    It is worth checking that a policy can be a sensible object on its own, because after four weeks of value functions it is easy to assume that "good policy" means "greedy in something".

    Take Mountain Car. The car is underpowered, so it cannot drive straight up the right hill; it has to rock back and forth to build energy. Here is a policy that does that, written down without a single value estimate:

    $$\pi(\text{right}\mid s)=1 \text{ if } \dot x \ge 0,\qquad \pi(\text{left}\mid s)=1 \text{ if } \dot x < 0.$$

    In words: always accelerate in the direction you are already moving. Push right while the car rolls right; once gravity wins and the car starts sliding back, push left, which drives it further up the left hill and gives it a longer run-up. Each swing adds energy, and after a few swings the car clears the right hill. This energy pumping policy is close to optimal, and describing it took one sentence and one inequality.

    Nothing about that policy required action values.

    It is a function of the state that returns an action. In week 4 we watched the same behaviour emerge from tile-coded Sarsa after hundreds of episodes of value learning, and the resulting cost-to-go surface was a complicated spiral. The policy is simple, the value function is not.

    We are not going to hand-code policies, of course — that only works when you already know the answer. The point is that the object we ultimately want can be much simpler than the object we have been estimating, so it may be worth learning it directly.

    Parameterizing the policy

    The machinery of function approximation transfers over unchanged. We keep the same idea — a fixed functional form controlled by a weight vector — and only change what the function outputs.

    Value parameterization

    $$(s,a)\ \xrightarrow{\ \mathbf w\ }\ \hat q(s,a,\mathbf w)\in\mathbb R$$

    Outputs a real number: an estimate of expected return. Unconstrained, so a linear function is fine.

    Policy parameterization

    $$(s,a)\ \xrightarrow{\ \boldsymbol\theta\ }\ \pi(a\mid s,\boldsymbol\theta)\in[0,1]$$

    Outputs a probability: how often to take $a$ in $s$. Constrained, so the functional form must be chosen with care.

    The convention in this course, and in the textbook, is $\mathbf w$ for value-function parameters and $\boldsymbol\theta$ for policy parameters. Keeping the letters separate matters later, when an actor-critic agent carries both vectors at once and updates them with different rules.

    The two constraints that rule out linear policies

    Whatever function we pick must produce a genuine probability distribution over the actions of every state:

    $$\pi(a\mid s,\boldsymbol\theta)\ge 0\quad\text{for all }a\in\mathcal A,\ s\in\mathcal S,\qquad\qquad \sum_{a\in\mathcal A}\pi(a\mid s,\boldsymbol\theta)=1\quad\text{for all }s\in\mathcal S.$$

    This is exactly why we cannot reuse the linear form $\boldsymbol\theta^\top\mathbf x(s,a)$ that served us so well for values. A dot product happily returns $-3.7$, and there is no way to constrain a linear map so that its outputs stay non-negative and sum to one for every state without effectively destroying the linearity. So the class of usable functions has to be restricted — not by restricting $\boldsymbol\theta$, but by pushing the raw scores through a function that enforces both constraints automatically.

    The softmax policy

    Softmax policy parameterization. Attach to each state-action pair a scalar action preference $h(s,a,\boldsymbol\theta)\in\mathbb R$, then define $$\pi(a\mid s,\boldsymbol\theta)\doteq\frac{e^{h(s,a,\boldsymbol\theta)}}{\sum_{b\in\mathcal A}e^{h(s,b,\boldsymbol\theta)}}.$$

    Both constraints are now structural rather than something we have to police during learning:

    1

    Non-negativity comes from the exponential. $e^{x}>0$ for every real $x$, so every action keeps strictly positive probability no matter how badly the preferences are set. In particular the policy is never exactly deterministic, which is where its exploration comes from.

    2

    Normalization comes from the denominator. Dividing by $\sum_b e^{h(s,b,\boldsymbol\theta)}$ forces the probabilities in each state to sum to one, by construction, for any value of $\boldsymbol\theta$.

    3

    The preference itself is unconstrained. Since softmax absorbs both constraints, $h$ can be anything differentiable. The linear choice $h(s,a,\boldsymbol\theta)=\boldsymbol\theta^\top\mathbf x(s,a)$ works with the stacked features and tile coding from weeks 3 and 4; a neural network with one output per action works just as well.

    Notice how neatly this recycles earlier work. The awkward part of policy representation — the probability constraints — is handled once by the softmax, and the part we already know how to do — building features and taking gradients of a differentiable score — is exactly what is left over.

    Preferences are not action values

    The preference $h(s,a,\boldsymbol\theta)$ looks like $\hat q(s,a,\mathbf w)$ and is emphatically not the same thing. An action value is a claim about future reward: "taking $a$ here is worth $-11.6$ on average". A preference is a claim about ranking only: it says how much the agent leans towards $a$ compared with the other actions available in the same state.

    The clean way to see this is that softmax is invariant to a constant shift. Add any $c$ to every preference in a state and

    $$\frac{e^{h(s,a,\boldsymbol\theta)+c}}{\sum_b e^{h(s,b,\boldsymbol\theta)+c}}=\frac{e^{c}e^{h(s,a,\boldsymbol\theta)}}{e^{c}\sum_b e^{h(s,b,\boldsymbol\theta)}}=\pi(a\mid s,\boldsymbol\theta).$$

    Adding $100$ to all four preferences changes nothing about the agent's behaviour. Add $100$ to all four action values and you have made a false statement about the world. Only differences of preferences carry information, which also means a policy parameterization has one redundant degree of freedom per state — harmless, and worth knowing about when you stare at learned preference values and wonder why they drift.

    A softmax policy is a distribution you sample from, not an $\arg\max$ you take. If the preferences rank $a_1>a_2>a_0$, the agent does not always play $a_1$; it draws an action from the categorical distribution above, so $a_2$ and $a_0$ still come up at their own rates. That sampling is the exploration — there is no separate $\varepsilon$ branch. Taking $\arg\max_a\pi(a\mid s,\boldsymbol\theta)$ would be a greedy decision rule built on top of the policy, and it is not what the agent does while learning.

    Softmax over preferences versus $\varepsilon$-greedy over values

    Both turn scores into a distribution, but they distribute the probability in completely different shapes.

    $\varepsilon$-greedy over $\hat q$softmax over $h$
    What it readsestimated returnslearned preferences
    Best action$1-\varepsilon+\varepsilon/|\mathcal A|$, a fixed capcan approach $1$ as the gap grows
    Near-tie with the beststill only $\varepsilon/|\mathcal A|$almost the same probability as the best
    Terrible action$\varepsilon/|\mathcal A|$, foreverexponentially suppressed
    Becoming greedyneeds a schedule on $\varepsilon$happens on its own as preferences spread

    The row that matters most is the third one. $\varepsilon$-greedy is blind to the margin: a runner-up worth $1.9$ against a best of $2.0$ gets exactly the same slice as an action worth $-2.6$. Softmax spends its exploration where it is informative, on the actions that are actually competitive.

    The "spread" slider is a stand-in for the scale of the preferences. Because $h$ is learned, an agent can shrink or grow that scale by itself: small preferences early on give a near-uniform policy, and large preferences later give a nearly deterministic one. That autonomy is one of the reasons to prefer parameterized policies, which is the subject of the next post.
