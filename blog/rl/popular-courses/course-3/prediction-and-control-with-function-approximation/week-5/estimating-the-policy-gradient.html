    Estimating the Policy Gradient

    September 2026 · 21 min read · Prediction and Control with Function Approximation, Week 5

    Two sums, two samples. The theorem gives an exact gradient containing a sum over every state and a sum over every action. Following the policy turns the first into a sample. A multiply-and-divide trick turns the second into a sample. What survives is a one-line update built from the action you actually took.

    Sampling the states

    Start from the theorem:

    $$\nabla r(\pi)=\sum_s\mu(s)\sum_a\nabla\pi(a\mid s,\boldsymbol\theta)\,q_\pi(s,a)=\mathbb E_{\mu}\Big[\sum_a\nabla\pi(a\mid S,\boldsymbol\theta)\,q_\pi(S,a)\Big].$$

    Enumerating all states is out of the question for the problems we care about, but we do not have to: $\mu$ is the distribution of states visited while following $\pi_{\boldsymbol\theta}$, so the trajectory

    $$S_0,A_0,R_1,S_1,A_1,R_2,\ldots,S_t,A_t,R_{t+1},\ldots$$

    hands us states drawn from exactly the right distribution. This is the same move that turned $\overline{VE}$ into stochastic gradient descent in week 2: replace an expectation by one sample of it and take a small step. The resulting all-actions update is

    $$\boldsymbol\theta_{t+1}\doteq\boldsymbol\theta_t+\alpha\sum_a\nabla\pi(a\mid S_t,\boldsymbol\theta_t)\,q_\pi(S_t,a),$$

    and it is an unbiased estimate of $\nabla r(\pi)$ because $\mathbb E[S_t]\sim\mu$ under the policy. Note what it still asks for: the action values of every action in $S_t$, not just the one we took.

    Sampling the actions

    The remaining sum is over actions, and the obvious wish is to sample it as well. The obstacle is that $\sum_a\nabla\pi(a\mid s,\boldsymbol\theta)\,q_\pi(s,a)$ is not an expectation: the terms are weighted by $\nabla\pi$, which is not a probability distribution (its entries sum to zero, as we proved last post). The fix is to manufacture the weights we want:

    1

    Multiply and divide by the policy.

    $$\sum_a\nabla\pi(a\mid s,\boldsymbol\theta)\,q_\pi(s,a)=\sum_a\pi(a\mid s,\boldsymbol\theta)\,\frac{\nabla\pi(a\mid s,\boldsymbol\theta)}{\pi(a\mid s,\boldsymbol\theta)}\,q_\pi(s,a).$$

    Nothing has changed mathematically, but the sum is now weighted by $\pi$, which is a distribution over actions. Division is safe: a softmax policy assigns strictly positive probability to every action.

    2

    Recognize the expectation.

    $$=\mathbb E_\pi\!\left[\frac{\nabla\pi(A\mid S,\boldsymbol\theta)}{\pi(A\mid S,\boldsymbol\theta)}\,q_\pi(S,A)\ \middle|\ S=s\right].$$

    An expectation over actions drawn from the policy — and the agent draws exactly such an action at every step anyway.

    3

    Take one sample. Using the action $A_t$ the agent actually chose,

    $$\boldsymbol\theta_{t+1}\doteq\boldsymbol\theta_t+\alpha\,\frac{\nabla\pi(A_t\mid S_t,\boldsymbol\theta_t)}{\pi(A_t\mid S_t,\boldsymbol\theta_t)}\,q_\pi(S_t,A_t).$$

    The update now mentions one state and one action. No enumeration anywhere.

    The log-likelihood form

    The ratio has a shorter name. Since $\nabla\ln f(x)=\nabla f(x)/f(x)$,

    $$\boxed{\ \boldsymbol\theta_{t+1}\doteq\boldsymbol\theta_t+\alpha\,\nabla\ln\pi(A_t\mid S_t,\boldsymbol\theta_t)\,q_\pi(S_t,A_t)\ }$$

    This vector, $\nabla\ln\pi(a\mid s,\boldsymbol\theta)$, is called the eligibility vector or score function. Three reasons the log form is the one everyone writes:

    Cheaperno separate division, and for a softmax the log collapses the normalizer into a subtraction
    Stablerdividing by a tiny $\pi$ is numerically nasty; differentiating a log is not
    Familiarit is the score function of maximum likelihood, weighted by how good the action was
    How to read the update. "Take the direction that would make this action more likely, and step that far along it in proportion to how valuable the action was." A positive $q_\pi$ reinforces what was just done; a negative one pushes probability away from it. That single sentence is the whole idea behind REINFORCE, actor-critic, and every policy-gradient method built on them.

    Unbiased, and noisy

    Sampling costs variance. In a state with $|\mathcal A|$ actions the one-sample estimator takes exactly one of $|\mathcal A|$ possible values, and the $1/\pi$ factor is what keeps it honest: rarely chosen actions are seen seldom but count for a lot when they are. Averaged over draws it returns the exact inner sum; on any single step it can point somewhere else entirely.

    Two practical consequences follow. First, policy gradient methods need small step sizes or many samples, because each update is a very rough guess at the right direction. Second, anything that shrinks the spread of those bars without moving their mean is worth having — which is exactly what a baseline does, and why $q_\pi(S_t,A_t)-\hat v(S_t,\mathbf w)$ will replace $q_\pi(S_t,A_t)$ in the next post.

    What is still missing

    Look at the boxed update and ask what an agent can actually compute at time $t$.

    $\nabla\ln\pi(A_t\mid S_t,\boldsymbol\theta_t)$

    Available. We chose the parameterization, so we can differentiate it. For a softmax over linear preferences this is a short closed-form expression, derived in the next post.

    $q_\pi(S_t,A_t)$

    Not available. The true action value of the current policy is unknown, and it changes as the policy changes.

    Any unbiased stand-in for $q_\pi(S_t,A_t)$ keeps the estimate honest. The Monte Carlo choice — wait for the return and use it — gives REINFORCE, but it needs episodes and carries enormous variance. The alternative is to learn an approximate value function alongside the policy with a TD method, using the tools of weeks 2 to 4: in the continuing average reward setting that means differential values learned by a semi-gradient TD update.

    actorthe parameterized policy $\pi(a\mid s,\boldsymbol\theta)$ that acts and gets updated by the gradient
    →
    critica learned value function that supplies the weight for that update

    That pairing is the actor-critic architecture, and building it is the next post.
