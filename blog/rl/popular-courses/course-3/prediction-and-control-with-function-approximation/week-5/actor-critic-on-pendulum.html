    Actor-Critic on Pendulum Swing-Up

    September 2026 · 20 min read · Prediction and Control with Function Approximation, Week 5

    Everything at once. Tile coding from week 3, stacked action features from week 4, average reward from week 4, and the softmax actor-critic from this week, applied to a continuous-state control problem that cannot be solved by brute force.

    The task

    A rigid pendulum hangs from a pivot. The agent applies a torque at that pivot and wants the pendulum standing straight up and staying there. The torque is too weak to lift the pendulum directly, so the only way up is to swing back and forth and add energy on each pass — the same underpowered structure as Mountain Car, rotated into a circle.

    State

    $$s\doteq\begin{bmatrix}\beta\\ \dot\beta\end{bmatrix}$$

    Angle from vertical and angular velocity, both real-valued. Two continuous dimensions, so tables are out and features are in.

    Actions

    $$a\in\{-1,\,0,\,+1\}$$

    Constant torque clockwise, none, or counter-clockwise. Discrete and small, so a softmax policy over three preferences is the natural choice.

    The reward is the negative absolute angle from upright,

    $$R_{t+1}=-\lvert\beta_{t+1}\rvert,$$

    so hanging straight down costs about $-\pi$ per step and balancing upright costs almost nothing. Angular velocity is clipped to $\lvert\dot\beta\rvert\le2\pi$; if the pendulum spins faster than that it is reset to hanging at rest, which stops the agent from solving the problem by simply spinning forever.

    There are no episodes and no terminal states. The interaction never ends, which is exactly the continuing setting the average reward objective was built for. There is no return to wait for, no episode boundary to align updates with, and the natural measure of performance is the reward rate: what fraction of the time is this thing upright?

    Features for the actor and the critic

    Two continuous state variables with known ranges is the ideal case for tile coding. The setup used below:

    ChoiceSettingWhy
    Ranges$\beta\in[-\pi,\pi)$ wrapping, $\dot\beta\in[-2\pi,2\pi]$the angle is circular, so its tiles wrap around instead of ending
    Tilings32 tilings, each $8\times8$broad tiles generalize, and many offsets restore resolution
    Critic$\hat v(s,\mathbf w)=\mathbf w^\top\mathbf x(s)$2048 weights, 32 of them active in any state
    Actor$h(s,a,\boldsymbol\theta)=\boldsymbol\theta^\top\mathbf x_h(s,a)$stacked, so $3\times2048$ parameters, 32 active per action

    Both functions are linear in the tile features and thoroughly nonlinear in $\beta$ and $\dot\beta$, which is the whole point of feature construction: the learning stays linear and cheap while the represented function can bend as needed. Large tiles are what make early learning fast here — a single visit teaches the agent about a whole region of the phase space — and the 32 offset tilings are what let it eventually distinguish "almost upright and slowing down" from "almost upright and still turning".

    Step sizes

    Three step sizes have to be set: $\alpha^{\mathbf w}$ for the critic, $\alpha^{\boldsymbol\theta}$ for the actor, and $\alpha^{\bar R}$ for the average reward estimate. As always with tile coding, the sensible unit is "per tiling": a step size of $1/32$ per active feature makes one update move the estimate by roughly the full TD error.

    Keep the critic ahead of the actor. A systematic sweep on this task lands on $\alpha^{\boldsymbol\theta}<\alpha^{\mathbf w}$, and the reason is structural rather than empirical. The actor's update is only as good as the TD error it is handed, and the TD error is only meaningful if $\hat v$ has caught up with the policy currently being followed. A critic that learns faster than the policy changes gives an accurate critique; the reverse gives confident nonsense.

    Applying the algorithm

    Apply the previous post's softmax actor and linear critic to the tile-coded pendulum state. At each step, sample a torque, observe the next state and reward, compute the differential TD error, and update the critic, actor, and reward-rate estimate.

    What the agent does, in three phases

    1

    Uniform random. With $\boldsymbol\theta=\mathbf 0$ every preference is zero, so all three torques are equally likely. The pendulum jiggles near the bottom, the reward sits near $-\pi$, and the critic quietly learns that everything down there is equally bad.

    2

    Pumping. The first thing the agent discovers is not balancing but energy: torques aligned with the current direction of motion produce states with smaller $\lvert\beta\rvert$ a few steps later, so their TD errors are positive and their preferences grow. Swings get wider, and the average reward climbs steeply.

    3

    Balancing. Near the top the same mechanism has the opposite effect: torques that would carry the pendulum past vertical now produce negative TD errors, so the policy learns to brake. The reward rate flattens close to zero and the remaining improvement is the agent trimming the last few tenths of a radian.

    A learned policy maps states to actions rather than memorizing a trajectory. After a perturbation, it can recover if the resulting states are covered well by its training experience.

    Recovery is not guaranteed. Once the agent spends most of its time balancing, it gets little new experience near the bottom. Test swing-up from several starting states as well as recovery from perturbations; good balance alone does not establish reliable recovery.

    What this example demonstrates

    No exploration parameterexploration comes from sampling the softmax and fades as preferences separate; there is no $\varepsilon$ anywhere
    No maximizationthe agent never computes $\max_a$; it scores three preferences and samples
    No episodesone endless stream of experience, with the average reward as the yardstick
    Linear learning, nonlinear behaviourevery update is a feature-scaled step; the swing-up policy it produces is anything but linear

    The remaining gap in the method is the action set. Three torques were enough here, but a real actuator has a continuum of them, and neither a softmax over three preferences nor a $\max_a$ over action values extends to that. The last post of the week fixes exactly this.
