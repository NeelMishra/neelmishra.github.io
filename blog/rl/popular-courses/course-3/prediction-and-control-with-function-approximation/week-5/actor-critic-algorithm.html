    The Actor-Critic Algorithm

    September 2026 · 10 min read · Prediction and Control with Function Approximation, Week 5

    The actor chooses; the critic supplies feedback. A policy selects an action, the environment returns a reward and next state, and a value function checks whether that outcome was better or worse than expected. The resulting TD error updates both the policy and the value function.

    1. What are we learning?

    This post uses the continuing, average-reward setting from week 4: there is no episode end or discount factor. We learn three things:

    • Actor: $\pi(a\mid s,\boldsymbol\theta)$, the policy that chooses actions.
    • Critic: $\hat v(s,\mathbf w)$, an estimate of the policy's differential state value.
    • Reward rate: $\bar R$, an estimate of the policy's long-run average reward $r(\pi)$.

    The previous post gave the sampled policy-gradient update

    $$\boldsymbol\theta\leftarrow\boldsymbol\theta+ \alpha^{\boldsymbol\theta}\,q_\pi(S_t,A_t)\, \nabla_{\boldsymbol\theta}\ln\pi(A_t\mid S_t,\boldsymbol\theta).$$

    The log-policy gradient tells us how to make the chosen action more likely. The unknown action value $q_\pi(S_t,A_t)$ supplies its weight. Actor-critic replaces that weight with feedback available after one transition.

    2. Build a one-step target

    First, connect state and action values. $q_\pi(s',a')$ fixes the first action, then follows $\pi$. $v_\pi(s')$ lets the policy choose that action too. The law of total expectation therefore gives

    $$\begin{aligned} v_\pi(s')&=\mathbb E_\pi[G_t\mid S_t=s']\\ &=\sum_{a'}\pi(a'\mid s')\, \underbrace{\mathbb E_\pi[G_t\mid S_t=s',A_t=a']}_{q_\pi(s',a')}. \end{aligned}$$

    Both values use the same differential return $G_t$. For example, action values $2$ and $6$ chosen with probabilities $1/4$ and $3/4$ give $v_\pi(s')=(1/4)2+(3/4)6=5$.

    Now substitute, step by step. Start with week 4's Bellman equation. Here $p(s',r\mid s,a)$ is the probability of next state $s'$ and reward $r$ after taking $a$ in $s$; $r(\pi)$ is the policy's average reward.

    $$\begin{aligned} q_\pi(s,a) &=\sum_{s',r}p(s',r\mid s,a)\left[ r-r(\pi)+\underbrace{\sum_{a'}\pi(a'\mid s')q_\pi(s',a')}_{v_\pi(s')\text{ by total expectation}}\right]\\ &=\sum_{s',r}p(s',r\mid s,a)\left[r-r(\pi)+v_\pi(s')\right]\\ &=\mathbb E_\pi\!\left[R_{t+1}-r(\pi)+v_\pi(S_{t+1})\mid S_t=s,A_t=a\right]. \end{aligned}$$

    The second line replaces only the next-action average with $v_\pi(s')$, using the identity proved above. The last line rewrites the probability-weighted sum over $(s',r)$ as a conditional expectation. The current action $a$ remains fixed; the next action follows $\pi$. Every equality here is exact—sampling and approximation enter below.

    Observe one transition and replace the unknown quantities with our estimates:

    $$\boxed{Y_t=R_{t+1}-\bar R+\hat v(S_{t+1},\mathbf w).}$$

    This is the reward above the usual rate, plus the estimated value of the next state. It is a sampled target for $q_\pi$, not an equality on every transition. Its conditional expectation equals $q_\pi$ when the critic and reward rate are exact; an inaccurate critic can introduce bootstrapping bias.

    Why week 4 looked different: Sarsa used a sampled next-action value $\hat q(S_{t+1},A_{t+1})$. Here $\hat v(S_{t+1})$ estimates the value averaged over the next action. Week 4 also displayed the TD error, which subtracts the current estimate from the target. That subtraction is our next step.

    3. Subtract the current state value

    The actor needs to know whether the outcome was better or worse than expected in $S_t$. Subtract the critic's current-state estimate as a baseline:

    $$\boxed{\begin{aligned} \delta_t&=Y_t-\hat v(S_t,\mathbf w)\\ &=R_{t+1}-\bar R+\hat v(S_{t+1},\mathbf w)-\hat v(S_t,\mathbf w). \end{aligned}}$$

    This is the differential TD error. The critic uses it to correct its state value; the actor uses it to adjust the action's probability. With exact values, $\mathbb E[\delta_t\mid s,a]=q_\pi(s,a)-v_\pi(s)$, the action's advantage over the policy's usual choice.

    Subtracting a state-only baseline leaves the expected policy update unchanged, because it does not depend on the chosen action. A useful baseline can reduce variance; it does not remove bias from an inaccurate bootstrapped target.

    Why does the baseline leave the expected update unchanged?

    Hold the critic fixed during the actor update. Since action probabilities sum to one,

    $$\begin{aligned} &\mathbb E_\pi[\hat v(s,\mathbf w)\nabla_{\boldsymbol\theta}\ln\pi(A\mid s,\boldsymbol\theta)]\\ &\quad=\hat v(s,\mathbf w)\sum_a\nabla_{\boldsymbol\theta}\pi(a\mid s,\boldsymbol\theta)\\ &\quad=\hat v(s,\mathbf w)\nabla_{\boldsymbol\theta}1=\mathbf0. \end{aligned}$$

    This holds even when the baseline itself is inaccurate. It is the same argument as in the policy-gradient theorem post.

    For comparison, Sarsa's error is $R_{t+1}-\bar R+\hat q(S_{t+1},A_{t+1})-\hat q(S_t,A_t)$. It trains an action-value critic; our error trains a state-value critic and also supplies the actor's feedback.

    4. See what the TD error means

    If $R_{t+1}=1$, $\bar R=0.5$, $\hat v(S_{t+1})=1.2$, and $\hat v(S_t)=0.6$, then $\delta_t=1.1$: the outcome exceeded the critic's prediction. If the current-state prediction were $2$, the same positive reward would give $\delta_t=-0.3$.

    A positive error encourages the chosen action; a negative error discourages it. The figure shows this directly for a tabular-softmax actor.

    The interaction is a repeating loop: actor chooses → environment responds → critic computes $\delta_t$ → both learn. The critic evaluates the current policy while the actor improves it, so each changes the other's learning problem.

    5. The complete continuing algorithm

    Initialize policy parameters $\boldsymbol\theta$, critic weights $\mathbf w$, $\bar R=0$, and an initial state $S$. Choose positive step sizes $\alpha^{\boldsymbol\theta}$, $\alpha^{\mathbf w}$, and $\alpha^{\bar R}$. Then repeat:

    One actor-critic step
    1Sample $A\sim\pi(\cdot\mid S,\boldsymbol\theta)$, take it, and observe reward $R$ and next state $S'$.
    2Compute $\delta=R-\bar R+\hat v(S',\mathbf w)-\hat v(S,\mathbf w)$.
    3Reward rate: $\bar R\leftarrow\bar R+\alpha^{\bar R}\delta$.
    4Critic: $\mathbf w\leftarrow\mathbf w+\alpha^{\mathbf w}\delta\nabla_{\mathbf w}\hat v(S,\mathbf w)$.
    5Actor: $\boldsymbol\theta\leftarrow\boldsymbol\theta+\alpha^{\boldsymbol\theta}\delta\nabla_{\boldsymbol\theta}\ln\pi(A\mid S,\boldsymbol\theta)$.
    6Set $S\leftarrow S'$ and repeat, without waiting for an episode to finish.

    Compute $\delta$ once, before any update. Treat it as fixed in the actor and critic updates. The critic step is semi-gradient TD: differentiate the current-state prediction, not the bootstrapped target. The reward-rate step is the TD-based estimator from week 4, not an exact running mean of raw rewards.

    The three step sizes control policy learning, value learning, and reward-rate tracking separately. If the actor changes its policy too quickly, the critic's feedback becomes stale; slowing the actor relative to the critic can help.

    6. What actor-critic buys you

    REINFORCE waits for a full sampled return. Actor-critic bootstraps after one transition, giving immediate feedback and often lower variance, at the cost of bias when the critic is inaccurate. Neither a universal variance ranking nor convergence is guaranteed.

    This post's loop is specifically for continuing average-reward tasks. Discounted episodic actor-critic uses $R_{t+1}+\gamma\hat v(S_{t+1})-\hat v(S_t)$ instead, with terminal-state handling and the policy weighting appropriate to that objective.
