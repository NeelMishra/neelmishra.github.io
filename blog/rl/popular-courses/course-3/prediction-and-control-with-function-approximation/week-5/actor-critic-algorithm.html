    The Actor-Critic Algorithm

    September 2026 · 22 min read · Prediction and Control with Function Approximation, Week 5

    You do not have to choose. Learning a policy directly does not retire value functions; it repurposes them. The parameterized policy becomes the actor that selects actions, and a learned value function becomes the critic that judges them. One TD error drives both.

    The gap in the sampled update

    The sampled policy gradient update from the last post was

    $$\boldsymbol\theta_{t+1}=\boldsymbol\theta_t+\alpha\,\nabla\ln\pi(A_t\mid S_t,\boldsymbol\theta_t)\,q_\pi(S_t,A_t),$$

    and the only unusable piece is $q_\pi(S_t,A_t)$. A target with conditional expectation $q_\pi(s,a)$ given $S_t=s,A_t=a$ can replace it in the expected update. A practical choice is a one-step bootstrapped target, derived from the average-reward Bellman equation in week 4:

    $$q_\pi(S_t,A_t)\ \approx\ R_{t+1}-\bar R+\hat v(S_{t+1},\mathbf w).$$

    Why does this look different from week 4?

    Week 4 used an action-value critic, $\hat q$, in differential Sarsa. Here the critic estimates the state value, $\hat v$. The average-reward Bellman equation is the same; we average over the next action to obtain the state-value form.

    For a fixed policy $\pi$, using a consistent choice of the additive constant for differential values,

    $$v_\pi(s')=\sum_{a'}\pi(a'\mid s')q_\pi(s',a').$$

    Why is the state value an average of action values? $v_\pi(s')$ asks for the expected return starting in $s'$ and letting the policy choose the action. $q_\pi(s',a')$ fixes that first action to $a'$, then follows the same policy afterwards. The policy chooses each possible first action with probability $\pi(a'\mid s')$, so we average its corresponding expected return using that probability.

    Formally, condition on the first action and apply the law of total expectation:

    $$\begin{aligned} v_\pi(s') &=\mathbb E_\pi[G_t\mid S_t=s']\\ &=\sum_{a'}\underbrace{\Pr_\pi(A_t=a'\mid S_t=s')}_{\pi(a'\mid s')} \underbrace{\mathbb E_\pi[G_t\mid S_t=s',A_t=a']}_{q_\pi(s',a')}\\ &=\sum_{a'}\pi(a'\mid s')q_\pi(s',a'). \end{aligned}$$

    Here $s'$ is just the name of the state where this calculation starts; $t$ is an arbitrary time index. In this average-reward setting, both value functions use the same differential-return definition from week 4. Thus this is an exact relationship between the true values, not a bootstrapping approximation.

    For two actions with values $2$ and $6$, chosen with probabilities $1/4$ and $3/4$, the state value is $v_\pi(s')=(1/4)2+(3/4)6=5$. It reflects what this policy will do, rather than automatically taking the best action's value, $6$.

    Substitute this identity into week 4's Bellman equation:

    $$\begin{aligned} q_\pi(s,a) &=\sum_{s',r}p(s',r\mid s,a)\left[r-r(\pi)+\sum_{a'}\pi(a'\mid s')q_\pi(s',a')\right]\\ &=\sum_{s',r}p(s',r\mid s,a)\left[r-r(\pi)+v_\pi(s')\right]\\ &=\mathbb E_\pi\!\left[R_{t+1}-r(\pi)+v_\pi(S_{t+1})\mid S_t=s,A_t=a\right]. \end{aligned}$$

    The current action $a$ is still fixed: it determines the reward and next-state distribution. Only the next action is averaged out inside $v_\pi(S_{t+1})$. This is why a state-value critic can supply a target for the current action value.

    During learning, replace the true reward rate $r(\pi)$ by $\bar R$, replace $v_\pi$ by $\hat v$, and use one observed transition instead of the outer expectation. This produces the quoted target

    $$Y_t^{\mathrm{AC}}=R_{t+1}-\bar R+\hat v(S_{t+1},\mathbf w).$$

    The $\approx$ above means “use this sampled estimate,” not equality on every transition. With exact $v_\pi$ and $\bar R=r(\pi)$, this target is conditionally unbiased for $q_\pi(S_t,A_t)$. An inaccurate bootstrapped critic can introduce bias; replacing an expectation by one sample introduces sampling noise.

    There is also a second difference: week 4 displayed a TD error, not just its target. Its formula separates as

    $$\delta_t^{\mathrm{Sarsa}}= \underbrace{R_{t+1}-\bar R+\hat q(S_{t+1},A_{t+1},\mathbf w)}_{Y_t^{\mathrm{Sarsa}}:\ \text{target}} -\underbrace{\hat q(S_t,A_t,\mathbf w)}_{\text{current estimate}}.$$

    Sarsa samples $A_{t+1}$ from its current policy. For exact values, averaging over this action gives $\mathbb E_\pi[q_\pi(S_{t+1},A_{t+1})\mid S_{t+1}]=v_\pi(S_{t+1})$. A separately learned $\hat v$ approximates that policy average; it need not equal a separately learned $\sum_a\pi(a\mid s)\hat q(s,a)$ during training.

    For example, suppose the next state's action values are $2$ and $6$, selected with probabilities $1/4$ and $3/4$. Its state value is $5$. If $R_{t+1}=3$ and $\bar R=1$, the Sarsa target is either $4$ or $8$, with mean $7$. The state-value target is $3-1+5=7$. They agree after averaging over the next action, not sample by sample.

    Target versus TD error

    The quoted expression has no subtraction of the current value because it is a target for $q_\pi$. In the next section, subtracting the baseline $\hat v(S_t,\mathbf w)$ gives the state-value TD error $\delta_t=Y_t^{\mathrm{AC}}-\hat v(S_t,\mathbf w)$. Sarsa subtracts $\hat q(S_t,A_t,\mathbf w)$ to train an action-value critic; actor-critic subtracts $\hat v(S_t,\mathbf w)$ to train a state-value critic and give the actor an advantage-like signal. Both retain $R_{t+1}-\bar R$ and have no discount factor.

    So the actor-critic target is the differential reward for this step plus the estimated value of where we landed. Substituting it gives the bootstrapped update:

    $$\boldsymbol\theta_{t+1}=\boldsymbol\theta_t+\alpha^{\boldsymbol\theta}\,\nabla\ln\pi(A_t\mid S_t,\boldsymbol\theta_t)\big[R_{t+1}-\bar R+\hat v(S_{t+1},\mathbf w)\big].$$

    $\hat v(\cdot,\mathbf w)$ is the critic. It can be trained by any state-value method we already know — here, average reward semi-gradient TD — and it delivers feedback immediately, at every step, instead of at the end of an episode.

    Subtracting the baseline turns the target into a TD error

    We proved in the policy gradient theorem post that subtracting any action-independent $b(s)$ leaves the gradient unchanged. Take $b(S_t)=\hat v(S_t,\mathbf w)$, the critic's opinion of the state we are in:

    $$\boldsymbol\theta_{t+1}=\boldsymbol\theta_t+\alpha^{\boldsymbol\theta}\,\nabla\ln\pi(A_t\mid S_t,\boldsymbol\theta_t)\underbrace{\big[R_{t+1}-\bar R+\hat v(S_{t+1},\mathbf w)-\hat v(S_t,\mathbf w)\big]}_{\textstyle \delta_t,\ \text{the TD error}}.$$

    The bracket is exactly the differential TD error the critic already computes. Nothing new has to be calculated, and the sampled update becomes $\boldsymbol\theta_{t+1}=\boldsymbol\theta_t+\alpha^{\boldsymbol\theta}\delta_t\nabla\ln\pi(A_t\mid S_t,\boldsymbol\theta_t)$.

    1

    The subtracted term vanishes in expectation. Conditioning on $S_t=s$ and using $\sum_a\nabla\pi(a\mid s,\boldsymbol\theta)=\mathbf 0$,

    $$\mathbb E_\pi\big[\nabla\ln\pi(A\mid s,\boldsymbol\theta)\,\hat v(s,\mathbf w)\big]=\hat v(s,\mathbf w)\sum_a\pi(a\mid s,\boldsymbol\theta)\frac{\nabla\pi(a\mid s,\boldsymbol\theta)}{\pi(a\mid s,\boldsymbol\theta)}=\hat v(s,\mathbf w)\sum_a\nabla\pi(a\mid s,\boldsymbol\theta)=\mathbf 0.$$

    So the baselined update has the same expected value as the one without it. It is not an approximation.

    2

    What changes is the variance. Without the baseline the weight is a raw value estimate, which can be large and of one sign for long stretches; with it, the weight is a difference from expectation, small and centred near zero once the critic is decent. Less noise per step means a usable step size can be larger and learning is faster.

    3

    And the interpretation sharpens. $\delta_t>0$ means the action turned out better than the critic expected in that state, so the update raises its probability; $\delta_t<0$ means it disappointed, so the update lowers it. The critic is not telling the actor what is good in absolute terms, it is telling it what was surprising.

    How the two halves interact

    actor: $\pi(a\mid s,\boldsymbol\theta)$samples $A_t$ in $S_t$ and is updated by $\alpha^{\boldsymbol\theta}\delta_t\nabla\ln\pi$
    →
    environmentreturns $R_{t+1}$ and $S_{t+1}$
    →
    critic: $\hat v(s,\mathbf w)$forms $\delta_t$, updates itself by $\alpha^{\mathbf w}\delta_t\nabla\hat v$, and hands $\delta_t$ back to the actor

    Both components learn at the same time, and each one's problem is defined by the other. The critic is evaluating a policy that keeps changing, and the actor is following advice from a value function that is still wrong. This is generalized policy iteration once more, except that the policy improvement step is now a small gradient step rather than a greedification.

    Two things are worth noticing while dragging the sliders. The sign of the whole update is set by $\delta_t$ alone, not by whether the reward was positive: a $+1$ reward with $\bar R=0.5$ and a critic that expected more can still lower the action's probability. And whatever probability the chosen action gains is taken from the others, because $\nabla\ln\pi$ redistributes rather than creates.

    The algorithm

    Actor-critic for continuing tasks, average reward
    1input differentiable $\pi(a\mid s,\boldsymbol\theta)$ and differentiable $\hat v(s,\mathbf w)$
    2parameters: step sizes $\alpha^{\boldsymbol\theta}>0$, $\alpha^{\mathbf w}>0$, $\alpha^{\bar R}>0$
    3initialize $\bar R\leftarrow0$, and $\mathbf w\in\mathbb R^{d}$, $\boldsymbol\theta\in\mathbb R^{d'}$ arbitrarily, e.g. to $\mathbf 0$
    4initialize $S\in\mathcal S$
    5loop forever (one iteration per time step):
    6$A\sim\pi(\cdot\mid S,\boldsymbol\theta)$
    7take action $A$, observe $R$ and $S'$
    8$\delta\leftarrow R-\bar R+\hat v(S',\mathbf w)-\hat v(S,\mathbf w)$
    9$\bar R\leftarrow\bar R+\alpha^{\bar R}\delta$
    10$\mathbf w\leftarrow\mathbf w+\alpha^{\mathbf w}\delta\,\nabla\hat v(S,\mathbf w)$
    11$\boldsymbol\theta\leftarrow\boldsymbol\theta+\alpha^{\boldsymbol\theta}\delta\,\nabla\ln\pi(A\mid S,\boldsymbol\theta)$
    12$S\leftarrow S'$

    Line by line, everything in it has appeared before. Lines 8–10 use the state-value version of differential semi-gradient TD, learning $\hat v$ and tracking the average reward; week 4 used the corresponding action-value Sarsa update. Line 11 is the sampled policy gradient update of the previous post with $\delta$ standing in for the baselined action value. The only genuinely new thing is that the two share a step and a scalar.

    Three step sizes, three jobs. $\alpha^{\mathbf w}$ controls how fast the critic tracks the current policy, $\alpha^{\boldsymbol\theta}$ how fast the policy moves, and $\alpha^{\bar R}$ how fast the reward-rate baseline adapts. A common failure is an actor that outruns its critic: the policy changes so fast that $\hat v$ is always evaluating a policy that no longer exists, and the TD errors it produces stop being informative. Keeping $\alpha^{\boldsymbol\theta}$ noticeably smaller than $\alpha^{\mathbf w}$ is the usual remedy.

    There is no episode boundary anywhere in the loop, which is the point of the continuing formulation: the agent can run indefinitely and keep improving. Everything here also transfers to episodic tasks by replacing the differential TD error with the discounted one.

    Where this sits

    weight on $\nabla\ln\pi$needs episodesvariance
    REINFORCEthe full sampled returnyeshigh
    REINFORCE with baselinereturn minus $\hat v(S_t)$yeslower
    Actor-criticone-step TD error $\delta_t$nolowest of the three, but bootstrapped

    The trade is the same one TD always makes against Monte Carlo: bootstrapping introduces bias while the critic is inaccurate, and buys a large reduction in variance plus the ability to learn online, one step at a time, forever.
