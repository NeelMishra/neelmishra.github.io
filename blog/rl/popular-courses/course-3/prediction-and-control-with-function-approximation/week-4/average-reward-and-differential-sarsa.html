    Average Reward & Differential Sarsa

    September 2026 · 26 min read · Prediction and Control with Function Approximation, Week 4

    The discount factor is not a neutral hyperparameter. In a continuing task it silently picks a horizon, and the optimal policy can flip depending on where you set it. Average reward removes the choice: maximize the rate of reward, and every future step counts equally.

    Discounting encodes a horizon

    In continuing tasks we are often interested in long-horizon performance. Discounting works against that: it makes near rewards worth more than distant ones, so an agent will happily take a short-term gain over a larger long-term one. Whether it does depends entirely on $\gamma$.

    That is uncomfortable, because $\gamma$ is supposed to be a modelling convenience. The next example makes the problem concrete.

    A two-ring example

    Consider a continuing MDP shaped like a figure eight. Two rings of five states meet at a single junction state $S$.

    • Every state other than $S$ offers a single action, so there is no decision to make there. The agent simply keeps going around whichever ring it entered.
    • At $S$ the agent picks a ring. There are therefore exactly two deterministic policies: $\pi_L$, which always goes left, and $\pi_R$, which always goes right.
    • All rewards are $0$ except one transition per ring. The left ring pays $+1$ on the transition leaving $S$. The right ring pays $+2$ on the transition arriving back at $S$, five steps later.

    The discounted values

    Both policies return to $S$ after exactly five steps, so each value is a geometric series with ratio $\gamma^5$. Under $\pi_L$ the reward arrives immediately; under $\pi_R$ it arrives four steps later:

    $$v_L(S)=1+\gamma^5 v_L(S)=\frac{1}{1-\gamma^{5}},\qquad v_R(S)=2\gamma^{4}+\gamma^5 v_R(S)=\frac{2\gamma^{4}}{1-\gamma^{5}}.$$

    Now evaluate them at two ordinary settings of $\gamma$:

    $\gamma$$v_L(S)$$v_R(S)$Preferred policy
    $0.5$$\approx 1.0$$\approx 0.1$$\pi_L$, the smaller but immediate reward
    $0.9$$\approx 2.4$$\approx 3.2$$\pi_R$, the larger but delayed reward

    The optimal policy changed because a hyperparameter changed. We can find the exact tipping point by comparing the two closed forms; the common denominator cancels:

    $$v_R(S)>v_L(S)\iff 2\gamma^{4}>1\iff \boxed{\gamma>2^{-1/4}\approx0.841.}$$

    The threshold depends on the problem

    Nothing about $0.841$ is fundamental. Make the rings longer and the delayed reward gets discounted harder, so the threshold climbs. With rings of $n$ steps the same algebra gives $\gamma>2^{-1/(n-1)}$, and with 100-step rings that is

    $$\gamma>2^{-1/99}\approx0.993.$$
    The escape route is blocked. The only way to guarantee that the agent maximizes reward over the long run is to keep pushing $\gamma$ towards $1$. But $\gamma=1$ is not available in a continuing task, because the undiscounted return is generally infinite. Large $\gamma$ is also expensive: returns become larger and higher in variance, which makes them harder to learn.

    The average reward objective

    Instead of tuning a horizon, drop the horizon. Define the value of a policy as its long-run rate of reward:

    $$\boxed{r(\pi)\doteq\lim_{h\to\infty}\frac{1}{h}\sum_{t=1}^{h}\mathbb E\big[R_t\mid S_0,A_{0:t-1}\sim\pi\big].}$$

    Read it as: run the agent for $h$ steps, add up the reward it collects, divide by $h$. As $h$ grows this settles down to the reward per step the policy sustains. An agent maximizing $r(\pi)$ weighs a reward a million steps from now exactly as much as the next one.

    The same quantity, from the inside out

    The limit definition is awkward to compute with. Under mild conditions it can be rewritten as three nested expectations over the steady-state distribution $\mu_\pi$.

    1

    Inner: average immediate reward for a state-action pair.

    $$\bar r(s,a)\doteq\mathbb E[R\mid S=s,A=a]=\sum_{s',r}p(s',r\mid s,a)\,r.$$
    2

    Middle: average over the policy's action choice in that state.

    $$\bar r_\pi(s)\doteq\sum_a\pi(a\mid s)\,\bar r(s,a).$$
    3

    Outer: average over how often the policy visits each state.

    $$r(\pi)=\sum_s\mu_\pi(s)\,\bar r_\pi(s).$$

    Putting them together gives the triple sum:

    $$\boxed{r(\pi)=\sum_s\mu_\pi(s)\sum_a\pi(a\mid s)\sum_{s',r}p(s',r\mid s,a)\,r.}$$

    Apply it to the rings. Each policy cycles deterministically through five states, so $\mu_\pi$ is uniform over that ring's five states and only one of the five transitions pays anything:

    $$r(\pi_L)=\frac{1}{5}=0.2,\qquad r(\pi_R)=\frac{2}{5}=0.4.$$

    The average-reward objective prefers $\pi_R$, and it does so without any hyperparameter, at any ring length. That matches the intuition the discounted formulation only reproduces once $\gamma$ is pushed high enough.

    The differential return

    The objective settles how to compare policies. It does not yet tell us how to assign values to states, and we cannot simply sum undiscounted rewards, because that sum diverges. The fix is to measure each reward relative to the rate the policy sustains:

    $$G_t\doteq\big(R_{t+1}-r(\pi)\big)+\big(R_{t+2}-r(\pi)\big)+\big(R_{t+3}-r(\pi)\big)+\cdots$$
    The differential return measures how much more reward the agent collects from here on than it would collect on average under the same policy. Its terms average to zero, so the sum has a chance of staying finite.

    Working it out on the ring

    Take $\pi_L$, so $r(\pi)=0.2$. Starting at $S$ and going left, the reward sequence is $1,0,0,0,0$ repeating, and the differential terms are $0.8,-0.2,-0.2,-0.2,-0.2$. The partial sums cycle rather than converge:

    Step12345then repeats
    Term $R-r(\pi)$$+0.8$$-0.2$$-0.2$$-0.2$$-0.2$…
    Partial sum$0.8$$0.6$$0.4$$0.2$$0.0$…

    The partial sums oscillate through $0.8,0.6,0.4,0.2,0$ forever. Their Cesàro sum, the limit of the average of the partial sums, is well defined:

    $$G_t=\frac{0.8+0.6+0.4+0.2+0.0}{5}=0.4.$$

    Now compare the two actions available at $S$, still under $\pi_L$ afterwards. Going right once costs four steps of $-0.2$ and then collects $2-0.2=1.8$, and after that the agent is back at $S$ behaving like $\pi_L$:

    $$\underbrace{4\times(-0.2)+1.8}_{\text{the detour through the right ring}}\;+\;\underbrace{0.4}_{\text{left ring forever after}}=1.0+0.4=1.4.$$

    Take left, then $\pi_L$

    $G_t=0.4$

    Take right, then $\pi_L$

    $G_t=1.4$

    So an agent currently following $\pi_L$ can look at its own differential returns and conclude that it should switch to the right ring. That is policy improvement, driven entirely by average-reward quantities.

    Differential returns compare actions, not policies

    Redo the calculation under $\pi_R$, where $r(\pi)=0.4$. Going right forever gives terms $-0.4,-0.4,-0.4,-0.4,1.6$, partial sums $-0.4,-0.8,-1.2,-1.6,0$, and a Cesàro sum of $-0.8$. Taking left once first costs $0.6-1.6=-1.0$ before rejoining, giving $-1.8$.

    Policy followed afterwards$r(\pi)$Take left nowTake right nowBetter action
    $\pi_L$$0.2$$0.4$$1.4$right
    $\pi_R$$0.4$$-1.8$$-0.8$right

    Both rows agree that "right" is the better action, which is what we want. But look at the raw numbers across rows: the differential returns under $\pi_R$ are lower than those under $\pi_L$, even though $\pi_R$ has twice the average reward.

    Do not compare differential returns across policies. A differential return says how much better an action is than the average under a given policy, and the baseline $r(\pi)$ that was subtracted differs from policy to policy. Differential returns can only rank actions when the same policy is followed at every subsequent step. To rank policies, compare their average rewards.

    One more property is worth stating, because it explains why $r(\pi)$ has to be estimated accurately rather than approximated loosely. The differential return converges only when the subtracted constant is exactly the true average reward. Subtract something smaller and the terms have a positive mean, so the sum diverges to $+\infty$; subtract something larger and it diverges to $-\infty$.

    Differential value functions

    With a well-defined return, value functions follow the usual pattern. The differential action-value function is the expected differential return:

    $$q_\pi(s,a)=\mathbb E_\pi\big[G_t\mid S_t=s,A_t=a\big],$$

    and it satisfies a Bellman equation that looks exactly like the discounted one with two edits: subtract $r(\pi)$ from the reward, and delete $\gamma$.

    $$\boxed{q_\pi(s,a)=\sum_{s',r}p(s',r\mid s,a)\Big(r-r(\pi)+\sum_{a'}\pi(a'\mid s')\,q_\pi(s',a')\Big).}$$

    Interpreted directly: $q_\pi(s,a)$ captures how much more reward the agent will collect by starting in $s$ with action $a$ than it would collect on average from a typical state under that policy.

    Differential semi-gradient Sarsa

    The control algorithm now needs one extra piece of state. It has to estimate $r(\pi)$ online, because the update subtracts it from every sampled reward.

    Differential semi-gradient Sarsa for estimating $\hat q\approx q_*$
    1input a differentiable parameterization $\hat q:\mathcal S\times\mathcal A\times\mathbb R^{d}\to\mathbb R$
    2parameters: step sizes $\alpha>0$, $\beta>0$
    3initialize $\mathbf w\in\mathbb R^{d}$ arbitrarily, e.g. $\mathbf w=\mathbf 0$
    4initialize the average reward estimate $\bar R\in\mathbb R$ arbitrarily, e.g. $\bar R=0$
    5initialize state $S$ and action $A$
    6for each step:
    7take $A$, observe $R,S'$
    8choose $A'$ from $\hat q(S',\cdot,\mathbf w)$, e.g. $\varepsilon$-greedy
    9$\delta\leftarrow R-\bar R+\hat q(S',A',\mathbf w)-\hat q(S,A,\mathbf w)$
    10$\bar R\leftarrow\bar R+\beta\,\delta$
    11$\mathbf w\leftarrow\mathbf w+\alpha\,\delta\,\nabla\hat q(S,A,\mathbf w)$
    12$S\leftarrow S'$, $A\leftarrow A'$

    Compare it line by line with episodic semi-gradient Sarsa from the first post in this week:

    Episodic semi-gradient SarsaDifferential semi-gradient Sarsa
    TD error$R+\gamma\hat q(S',A',\mathbf w)-\hat q(S,A,\mathbf w)$$R-\bar R+\hat q(S',A',\mathbf w)-\hat q(S,A,\mathbf w)$
    Discounting$\gamma$ multiplies the bootstrapnone; the baseline replaces it
    Extra parameters$\alpha$$\alpha$ and $\beta$
    Extra statenone$\bar R$, the running average reward
    Episodesresets at terminationnone; the loop never ends

    Two ways to track $\bar R$

    The obvious estimator is an ordinary incremental average of the observed rewards:

    $$\bar R\leftarrow\bar R+\beta\big(R-\bar R\big).$$

    It is unbiased and easy to justify, but it only sees the raw reward stream, which can be very noisy. The version in the pseudocode above uses the TD error instead:

    $$\bar R\leftarrow\bar R+\beta\,\delta.$$

    Since $\delta$ already has the current value estimates subtracted from it, much of the reward's step-to-step fluctuation has been explained away by $\hat q$. In practice this variant performs better because it has lower variance, and it is the form usually given for differential Sarsa.

    Takeaway

    Average reward replaces "how much discounted reward can I collect" with "what rate of reward can I sustain". Differential returns make values finite by subtracting that rate, and the algorithms carry over with two edits: drop $\gamma$, and learn $\bar R$ alongside $\mathbf w$.
