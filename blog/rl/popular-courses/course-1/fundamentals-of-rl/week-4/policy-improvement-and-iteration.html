    Policy Improvement & Policy Iteration

    August 2026 · 22 min read · Coursera: Fundamentals of Reinforcement Learning (University of Alberta), Week 4

    From prediction to control. Part 1 computed $v_\pi$ for a policy we were handed. Now we use those values to answer the control question: can a one-step greedy change produce a policy that is guaranteed to be no worse? The policy improvement theorem says yes, and policy iteration turns that theorem into an algorithm.

    The information hidden inside $v_\pi$

    A state value says how good it is to be in $s$ and then follow $\pi$. To compare the actions available in that state, expose the first action:

    $$ \begin{aligned} q_\pi(s,a) &= \sum_{s'}\sum_r p(s',r\mid s,a)\\[-2pt] &\qquad\cdot\big[r+\gamma v_\pi(s')\big]. \end{aligned} $$

    This is a one-step lookahead through the model. For each candidate action, inspect every reward and successor it can produce, then score the successor using the old policy's value function. Nothing has been changed yet: $q_\pi(s,a)$ asks, what if I take $a$ once and follow $\pi$ afterwards?

    The old policy's value is its own weighted average of those action values:

    $$ v_\pi(s)=\sum_a\pi(a\mid s)\,q_\pi(s,a). $$

    An average cannot exceed its largest member. Therefore at least one action satisfies $q_\pi(s,a)\ge v_\pi(s)$. A greedy policy chooses such an action in every state:

    $$ \begin{aligned} \pi'(s) &\in\arg\max_a q_\pi(s,a)\\ &= \arg\max_a\sum_{s'}\sum_r\\[-2pt] &\qquad p(s',r\mid s,a) \big[r+\gamma v_\pi(s')\big]. \end{aligned} $$

    The policy improvement theorem

    We have two policies:

    • $\pi$ is the old policy, whose value function $v_\pi$ and action values $q_\pi$ are known.
    • $\pi'$ is the new policy, built by choosing actions that look better according to $q_\pi$.

    For clarity, take $\pi'$ to be deterministic, so $\pi'(s)$ denotes the action it chooses in state $s$.

    What are we trying to prove? The premise below only says that taking one action from $\pi'$ and then returning to the old policy $\pi$ is no worse than using $\pi$ immediately. The theorem must turn that local statement into the global conclusion that following $\pi'$ at every future decision is no worse: $v_{\pi'}(s)\ge v_\pi(s)$.

    The one-step premise is

    $$ q_\pi(s,\pi'(s))\ge v_\pi(s)\qquad\text{for every }s, $$

    and the desired conclusion is

    $$ \boxed{\,v_{\pi'}(s)\ge v_\pi(s)\qquad\text{for every }s\,}. $$

    The proof repeatedly replaces one more future $\pi$ decision by a $\pi'$ decision:

    One switch$\pi'$ now, then $\pi$ forever
    →
    $k$ switches$\pi'$ for $k$ decisions, then $\pi$
    →
    Full switch$\pi'$ at every future decision
    Step 1: improve the first decision. Start from $s$, take the action prescribed by $\pi'$, and then evaluate the successor as though the old policy $\pi$ will be followed from there: $$ \begin{aligned} v_\pi(s) &\le q_\pi(s,\pi'(s))\\ &= \mathbb E\!\left[ R_{t+1}+\gamma v_\pi(S_{t+1})\right.\\[-2pt] &\qquad\left. \mid S_t=s,\ A_t=\pi'(s) \right]. \end{aligned} $$ The first action now comes from $\pi'$, but the tail $v_\pi(S_{t+1})$ still means “follow $\pi$ afterwards.”
    Step 2: improve the decision after that. The premise holds at every possible successor $S_{t+1}$: $$v_\pi(S_{t+1})\le q_\pi\!\left(S_{t+1},\pi'(S_{t+1})\right).$$ Replace the old-policy tail in Step 1 by that larger quantity. Now the first two decisions use $\pi'$: $$ \begin{aligned} v_\pi(s) \le \mathbb E_{\pi'}\!\left[ R_{t+1}+\gamma R_{t+2}\right.\\[-2pt] \left. +\gamma^2v_\pi(S_{t+2}) \mid S_t=s \right]. \end{aligned} $$ The subscript $\pi'$ here governs the two explicit transitions. The final $v_\pi(S_{t+2})$ still represents following the old policy after those two decisions.
    Step 3: keep unrolling. After replacing the first $k$ decisions, the trajectory follows $\pi'$ up to $S_{t+k}$ and only then falls back to $\pi$: $$ \begin{aligned} v_\pi(s) &\le \mathbb E_{\pi'}\!\left[ \sum_{i=0}^{k-1}\gamma^iR_{t+i+1}\right.\\[-2pt] &\qquad\left. +\gamma^k v_\pi(S_{t+k}) \mid S_t=s \right]. \end{aligned} $$ The finite sum is the reward collected during the first $k$ decisions under $\pi'$. The last term is the remaining old-policy tail.

    Why this becomes $v_{\pi'}(s)$

    For $\gamma<1$ and bounded values, the remaining old-policy tail is squeezed to zero:

    $$ \left|\gamma^k v_\pi(S_{t+k})\right| \le \gamma^k\lVert v_\pi\rVert_\infty \longrightarrow0. $$

    Now look only at which policy chooses each decision:

    $$ \begin{aligned} k=1 &: \quad \pi',\ \pi,\ \pi,\ \pi,\ldots\\ k=2 &: \quad \pi',\ \pi',\ \pi,\ \pi,\ldots\\ k=3 &: \quad \pi',\ \pi',\ \pi',\ \pi,\ldots\\ k\to\infty &: \quad \pi',\ \pi',\ \pi',\ \pi',\ldots \end{aligned} $$

    As $k$ grows, the old policy $\pi$ is pushed farther and farther away. Any particular future decision eventually lies inside the first $k$ decisions, so it is chosen by $\pi'$. In the limit, no old-policy decisions remain, and the old-policy tail already went to zero.

    What remains is the complete return $G_t$ produced by following $\pi'$ forever. Its expected value is, by definition, $v_{\pi'}(s)$. Therefore

    $$ \boxed{ v_\pi(s) \le \mathbb E_{\pi'}[G_t\mid S_t=s] = v_{\pi'}(s) }. $$

    For an episodic problem in which $\pi'$ reaches termination with probability $1$, the same conclusion is even more direct: once a trajectory reaches terminal time $T$, the tail is $v_\pi(S_T)=0$, and the explicit rewards before it are the complete return under $\pi'$.

    Why greedification satisfies the premise

    The old state value is a probability-weighted average of the old action values. The largest action value cannot be smaller than that average:

    $$ \begin{aligned} q_\pi(s,\pi'(s)) &= \max_a q_\pi(s,a)\\ &\ge \sum_a\pi(a\mid s)q_\pi(s,a)\\ &=v_\pi(s). \end{aligned} $$

    This inequality proves only the one-step premise: use $\pi'$ once, then $\pi$. The unrolling argument above is what converts that local premise into the full-policy conclusion $v_{\pi'}\ge v_\pi$.

    Strict improvement needs reachability. If the inequality is strict at a state that can matter from $s$, then $v_{\pi'}(s)>v_\pi(s)$. A strict improvement in an unreachable corner of the MDP need not change the value of every other state.

    Policy iteration

    One greedy step improves a policy but need not make it optimal. The obvious response is to alternate the two operations:

    Policy $\pi_k$the current behaviour
    →
    Evaluatecompute $v_{\pi_k}$
    →
    Improvegreedify to obtain $\pi_{k+1}$
    Policy Iteration — estimate $\pi\approx\pi_*$ and $V\approx v_*$
    Input
    model $p(s',r\mid s,a)$, discount $\gamma$, and evaluation tolerance $\theta>0$
    Initialise
    $V(s)$ and a deterministic action $\pi(s)\in\mathcal A(s)$ arbitrarily for every state $s$
    1 repeat
    2 policy evaluation: evaluate the current $\pi$ by repeatedly applying
    $$ \begin{aligned} V(s)\leftarrow{}& \sum_{s',r}p(s',r\mid s,\pi(s))\\[-2pt] &\qquad\big[r+\gamma V(s')\big] \end{aligned} $$
    3 until the largest state-value change satisfies $\Delta<\theta$
    4 policy improvement: $\texttt{policy-stable}\leftarrow\texttt{true}$
    5 for each state $s$:
    6 $\texttt{old-action}\leftarrow\pi(s)$
    7 replace $\pi(s)$ by a greedy action, keeping $\texttt{old-action}$ if it is tied:
    $$ \begin{aligned} \pi(s) &\leftarrow \arg\max_a\\[-2pt] &\qquad \sum_{s',r}p(s',r\mid s,a)\\[-2pt] &\qquad\big[r+\gamma V(s')\big] \end{aligned} $$
    8 if $\pi(s)\ne\texttt{old-action}$, set $\texttt{policy-stable}\leftarrow\texttt{false}$
    9 until $\texttt{policy-stable}=\texttt{true}$
    Output
    the stable policy $\pi$ and its value table $V$

    The evaluation phase is exactly the iterative policy-evaluation algorithm from Part 1, run for the current policy. The improvement phase performs one model-based lookahead for every action and keeps a maximiser.

    Why must this terminate in a finite MDP? In the exact-evaluation argument, each improvement is either strict or the policy is already greedy with respect to its own value. A finite MDP has only finitely many deterministic policies (at most $|\mathcal A|^{|\mathcal S|}$ when every action is available everywhere), so a sequence of strict improvements cannot continue forever. When an exactly evaluated policy is unchanged by greedification, it satisfies the Bellman optimality equation and is optimal. With a numerical tolerance $\theta$, both “stable” and “optimal” should be read to the corresponding approximation accuracy.

    Ties need a stable rule. If several actions maximise the lookahead, changing from one tied action to another is not a strict improvement and can make a naive implementation report instability forever. Keep the old action when it is among the maximisers, or use a fixed deterministic tie-break.

    Must evaluation finish every time?

    The clean proof imagines exact evaluation: march all the way to $v_{\pi_k}$, then improve. In practice that is often wasteful. If a few sweeps already reveal which actions are better, improving now can move the policy in the right direction before the values have fully settled.

    Exact policy iteration

    Evaluate nearly to convergence, then improve. Fewer policy changes, more work per change.

    Modified policy iteration

    Perform a fixed or adaptive number of evaluation sweeps, then improve. More policy changes, much cheaper evaluation phases.

    Generalized policy iteration

    Generalized policy iteration (GPI) is the umbrella idea: one process pushes values toward consistency with the current policy; another pushes the policy toward greediness with respect to the current values. They do not need to take turns cleanly or finish their jobs before the other moves.

    Start from an arbitrary pair $(\pi_0,V_0)$. An evaluation move holds the policy fixed and moves $V$ toward $v_\pi$. An improvement move holds the current values fixed and moves $\pi$ toward a policy greedy with respect to $V$. Each move changes the target of the other process, so the path naturally zigzags.

    At the funnel tip, the values are consistent with the policy, $V=v_\pi$, and the policy is greedy with respect to those same values, $\pi=\operatorname{greedy}(V)$. A policy greedy with respect to its own value is optimal, so the common fixed point is $(v_*,\pi_*)$.

    This is one of the most reusable mental models in reinforcement learning. Actor–critic methods, for example, run an approximate evaluation process (the critic) beside an approximate improvement process (the actor). Week 4's tabular algorithms are the cleanest place to see the pattern before approximation enters.
