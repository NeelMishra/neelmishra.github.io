    Iterative Policy Evaluation

    August 2026 · 20 min read · Coursera: Fundamentals of Reinforcement Learning (University of Alberta), Week 4

    Where we are. Week 3 derived the Bellman equations and described optimal value functions. Week 4 asks the computational question: given a complete model of an MDP, how do we actually find those values? We begin with prediction — evaluating one fixed policy — and turn an equation into an algorithm.

    Prediction before control

    Dynamic programming separates reinforcement learning into two jobs:

    • Prediction / policy evaluation: a policy $\pi$ is fixed; compute its value function $v_\pi$.
    • Control: search for a policy that maximises value. That will be the subject of the next two posts.

    The word dynamic programming is slightly misleading here: there is no program being generated. It means solving a large problem by repeatedly reusing solutions to overlapping subproblems. For a finite MDP, each state's value is one such subproblem, and successor-state values are the reusable partial answers.

    The price of exact DP: we must know the model $p(s',r\mid s,a)$. The algorithm sums over every possible one-step outcome instead of learning from sampled experience. Later methods remove this requirement.

    From Bellman equation to update rule

    For a fixed policy, the Bellman expectation equation is

    $$ v_\pi(s)=\sum_a\pi(a\mid s)\sum_{s'}\sum_r p(s',r\mid s,a)\big[r+\gamma v_\pi(s')\big]. $$

    It is a simultaneous system: the unknown value of $s$ is written using the unknown values of its successors. We could assemble a matrix and solve the linear system exactly, but direct solves become expensive and ignore the sparse, local structure of most MDPs. Iterative policy evaluation takes the more useful route: start with any guess $V_0$, use it on the right-hand side, and treat the result as the next guess.

    $$ \boxed{\,V_{k+1}(s)=\sum_a\pi(a\mid s)\sum_{s'}\sum_r p(s',r\mid s,a)\big[r+\gamma V_k(s')\big]\,} $$

    This is a full backup: it replaces the estimate at $s$ by the expected immediate reward plus the discounted estimates of every possible successor. Apply it to every state once and we have completed one sweep.

    The Bellman operator makes convergence visible

    It helps to name the right-hand side. The Bellman expectation operator $T_\pi$ maps one entire value function to another:

    $$ \begin{aligned} (T_\pi V)(s) &= \sum_a\pi(a\mid s)\sum_{s'}\sum_r\\[-2pt] &\qquad p(s',r\mid s,a)\\[-2pt] &\qquad\cdot\big[r+\gamma V(s')\big]. \end{aligned} $$

    Then iterative policy evaluation is only $V_{k+1}=T_\pi V_k$: feed the current table $V_k$ into the operator and call the resulting table $V_{k+1}$. The true value function satisfies the Bellman equation, so applying the same operator does not change it:

    $$ v_\pi=T_\pi v_\pi. $$

    That is why $v_\pi$ is called a fixed point. The remaining question is the important one: why should repeatedly applying $T_\pi$ to an arbitrary initial guess $V_0$ move toward this fixed point rather than away from it?

    First: what are $U$ and $V$?

    $U$ and $V$ are simply two arbitrary candidate value functions on the same state space. Think of them as two different tables of guesses. They are not policies, actions, or special values:

    State$U(s)$$V(s)$$|U(s)-V(s)|$
    $s_1$$8$$5$$3$
    $s_2$$1$$2$$1$
    $s_3$$-2$$0$$2$

    The maximum norm measures the worst disagreement anywhere in the two tables:

    $$ \lVert U-V\rVert_\infty \;=\;\max_x |U(x)-V(x)|. $$

    I use $x$ inside the maximum to emphasise that it ranges over all states. In the small table above, $\lVert U-V\rVert_\infty=3$. Consequently, for every particular successor $s'$,

    $$ |U(s')-V(s')|\le \lVert U-V\rVert_\infty. $$

    This is one source of the $\le$ sign: no individual difference can be larger than the maximum of all differences.

    Now compare the two backups at one fixed state

    Apply the same policy-evaluation backup to $U$ and $V$, then subtract. At this point $s$ is fixed; we are comparing the two new numbers produced for that one state:

    $$ \begin{aligned} &(T_\pi U)(s)-(T_\pi V)(s)\\[3pt] &= \sum_a\pi(a\mid s)\sum_{s'}\sum_r\\[-2pt] &\qquad p(s',r\mid s,a) \Big([r+\gamma U(s')]\\[-2pt] &\qquad\qquad-[r+\gamma V(s')]\Big)\\[3pt] &= \gamma\sum_a\pi(a\mid s)\sum_{s'}\sum_r\\[-2pt] &\qquad p(s',r\mid s,a)\big(U(s')-V(s')\big). \end{aligned} $$

    The reward $r$ disappears because the same immediate reward term appears in both backups: $r-r=0$. The only disagreement comes from the successor estimates, and discounting multiplies that disagreement by $\gamma$.

    To keep the next lines readable, name the joint one-step probability weight

    $$ w_s(a,s',r) \;=\; \pi(a\mid s)\,p(s',r\mid s,a). $$

    For the fixed starting state $s$, these weights are non-negative and sum to $1$. The difference above is therefore just

    $$ \begin{aligned} &(T_\pi U)(s)-(T_\pi V)(s)\\ = &\gamma\sum_{a,s',r}w_s(a,s',r)\\[-2pt] &\qquad\cdot\big(U(s')-V(s')\big). \end{aligned} $$

    Where each $\le$ comes from

    Take the absolute value of the one-state difference. The first inequality is the triangle inequality: the absolute value of a weighted sum cannot exceed the weighted sum of the absolute values. The weights are non-negative because they are probabilities.

    $$ \begin{aligned} &\left|(T_\pi U)(s)-(T_\pi V)(s)\right|\\[3pt] &= \gamma\left| \sum_{a,s',r}w_s(a,s',r) \big(U(s')-V(s')\big) \right|\\[3pt] &\le \gamma\sum_{a,s',r}w_s(a,s',r) \left|U(s')-V(s')\right|. \end{aligned} $$

    Next, replace every particular successor difference by the worst possible difference, $\lVert U-V\rVert_\infty$:

    $$ \begin{aligned} &\left|(T_\pi U)(s)-(T_\pi V)(s)\right|\\ &\le \gamma\sum_{a,s',r}w_s(a,s',r) \lVert U-V\rVert_\infty\\ &= \gamma\,\lVert U-V\rVert_\infty \underbrace{\sum_{a,s',r}w_s(a,s',r)}_{=\,1}\\ &= \gamma\,\lVert U-V\rVert_\infty. \end{aligned} $$

    Why does the underbraced quantity equal $1$? For each action $a$, the environment probabilities over all $(s',r)$ sum to $1$. The policy probabilities over all actions also sum to $1$:

    $$ \begin{aligned} \sum_{a,s',r}w_s(a,s',r) &= \sum_a\pi(a\mid s)\\[-2pt] &\qquad\cdot\sum_{s',r}p(s',r\mid s,a)\\ &= \sum_a\pi(a\mid s)\cdot1 =1. \end{aligned} $$

    So the backup is taking a probability-weighted average of successor disagreements. An average cannot exceed the largest disagreement, and $\gamma$ then scales it down.

    Concrete reading. If two candidate tables differ by at most $3$ anywhere and $\gamma=0.9$, then after one Bellman backup they can differ by at most $0.9\times3=2.7$ anywhere. It does not matter how the policy and transition probabilities distribute their weight: they form an average, so they cannot manufacture a disagreement larger than the worst one they were given.

    Only now take the maximum over states

    The previous bound holds for every state $s$. Therefore it also holds for whichever state has the largest post-backup disagreement:

    $$ \boxed{ \begin{aligned} &\lVert T_\pi U-T_\pi V\rVert_\infty\\ &= \max_s\left|(T_\pi U)(s)-(T_\pi V)(s)\right|\\ &\le \gamma\lVert U-V\rVert_\infty \end{aligned} }. $$

    This is exactly what it means for $T_\pi$ to be a $\gamma$-contraction: after applying the operator, the maximum distance between any two candidate value functions is at most $\gamma$ times what it was before.

    Finally, choose the two functions we actually care about

    The contraction statement was proved for arbitrary $U$ and $V$, so now choose

    $$ U=V_k,\qquad V=v_\pi. $$

    Use the algorithm update $V_{k+1}=T_\pi V_k$ and the fixed-point identity $v_\pi=T_\pi v_\pi$:

    $$ \begin{aligned} \lVert V_{k+1}-v_\pi\rVert_\infty &= \lVert T_\pi V_k-T_\pi v_\pi\rVert_\infty\\ &\le \gamma\lVert V_k-v_\pi\rVert_\infty. \end{aligned} $$

    Apply the same one-step bound repeatedly:

    $$ \begin{aligned} \lVert V_k-v_\pi\rVert_\infty &\le \gamma\lVert V_{k-1}-v_\pi\rVert_\infty\\ &\le \gamma^2\lVert V_{k-2}-v_\pi\rVert_\infty\\ &\;\;\vdots\\ &\le \gamma^k\lVert V_0-v_\pi\rVert_\infty \longrightarrow 0. \end{aligned} $$

    Because $\gamma^k\to0$ when $\gamma<1$, the worst error goes to zero regardless of the initial table $V_0$. This is the mathematical reason the algorithm works, not just an empirical observation. It also proves uniqueness: if two different fixed points existed, the operator would have to shrink the positive distance between them even though applying it changes neither one, which is impossible.

    The $\gamma=1$ episodic case needs a different argument: convergence is still guaranteed for a proper policy whose trajectories reach a terminal state, because probability mass eventually drains into the terminal states. The gridworld below is of that kind.

    Two arrays or one?

    Synchronous sweep

    Read every successor from the frozen array $V_k$ and write every result to a separate array $V_{k+1}$. After the sweep, swap the arrays.

    Benefit: the mathematics matches the update literally, so results do not depend on state order.

    In-place sweep

    Keep one array and overwrite $V(s)$ immediately. States later in the sweep may consume values updated moments ago.

    Benefit: half the memory and usually faster propagation. It still converges under the standard DP conditions.

    The notes use two arrays first because they expose the idea cleanly, then point out that the one-array version often converges faster. Neither changes the fixed point; they only change the route taken to reach it.

    The algorithm

    Here is the in-place form. Every mathematical object is the same one used in the Bellman equation above; the algorithm merely repeats that backup until the value table stops moving.

    Iterative Policy Evaluation — estimate $V\approx v_\pi$
    Input
    policy $\pi$, model $p(s',r\mid s,a)$, discount $\gamma$, and tolerance $\theta>0$
    Initialise
    $V(s)$ arbitrarily for every $s$, while fixing $V(s)=0$ for terminal states
    1 repeat
    2 $\Delta\leftarrow0$
    3 for each non-terminal state $s\in\mathcal S$:
    4 $v\leftarrow V(s)$
    5 replace $V(s)$ with its Bellman expectation backup:
    $$ \begin{aligned} V(s)\leftarrow{}& \sum_a\pi(a\mid s)\sum_{s'}\sum_r\\[-2pt] &p(s',r\mid s,a)\big[r+\gamma V(s')\big] \end{aligned} $$
    6 $\Delta\leftarrow\max\!\big(\Delta,\lvert v-V(s)\rvert\big)$
    7 until $\Delta<\theta$
    Output
    $V\approx v_\pi$

    $\Delta$ is the largest change made anywhere in the latest sweep. The test $\Delta<\theta$ does not mean the values are magically exact; it says the Bellman updates have become smaller than the requested numerical tolerance. A smaller $\theta$ buys a closer approximation at the price of more sweeps.

    Terminal states are boundary conditions, not ordinary states. Their return after termination is defined as zero. Keep $V(\text{terminal})=0$ rather than repeatedly backing it up.

    Worked example: the 4×4 gridworld

    Now make every symbol concrete. The grid has terminal states in the top-left and bottom-right corners. Each non-terminal state offers four actions: up, right, down, left. The policy chooses each with probability $1/4$. Every transition earns $-1$, $\gamma=1$, and walking into a wall leaves the agent in the same state.

    Consider the state immediately right of the top-left terminal, $s_1$. Its four outcomes are: up hits the wall and returns to $s_1$; right reaches $s_2$; down reaches $s_5$; left terminates. A synchronous backup is therefore

    $$ V_{k+1}(s_1)=\tfrac14\Big[(-1+V_k(s_1))+(-1+V_k(s_2))+(-1+V_k(s_5))+(-1+0)\Big]. $$

    With $V_0=0$, every non-terminal action has target $-1$, so the first sweep sets every non-terminal cell to $-1$. Later sweeps propagate the cost of wandering before termination. Step through the exact synchronous algorithm:

    What the negative values mean

    Because every move costs $-1$, $\gamma=1$, and the episode stops at a terminal state, the return is simply minus the number of remaining steps:

    $$ G_t=-1-1-\cdots-1=-T_{\text{remaining}}, \qquad v_\pi(s)=-\mathbb{E}_\pi[T_{\text{remaining}}\mid S_t=s]. $$

    A value of $-14$ means the random policy needs 14 more moves on average, not that the state itself carries a reward of $-14$. This distinction matters: rewards belong to transitions; values summarise entire future trajectories.

    Four implementation traps

    • Updating the terminal state. Doing so invents rewards after the episode has ended.
    • Dropping wall collisions. A blocked action is still an outcome: it produces reward $-1$ and returns to the same state.
    • Mixing update styles accidentally. Two arrays give synchronous evaluation; one array gives in-place evaluation. Both are valid, but a hand calculation must say which one it uses.
    • Stopping on an average change. One unstable state can hide inside a small average. Use the maximum absolute change $\Delta$.
