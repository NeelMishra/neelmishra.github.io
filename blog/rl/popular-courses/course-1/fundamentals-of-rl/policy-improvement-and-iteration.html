    Policy Improvement & Policy Iteration

    August 2026 · 22 min read · Coursera: Fundamentals of Reinforcement Learning (University of Alberta), Week 4

    From prediction to control. Part 1 computed $v_\pi$ for a policy we were handed. Now we use those values to answer the control question: can a one-step greedy change produce a policy that is guaranteed to be no worse? The policy improvement theorem says yes, and policy iteration turns that theorem into an algorithm.

    The information hidden inside $v_\pi$

    A state value says how good it is to be in $s$ and then follow $\pi$. To compare the actions available in that state, expose the first action:

    $$ q_\pi(s,a)=\sum_{s'}\sum_r p(s',r\mid s,a)\big[r+\gamma v_\pi(s')\big]. $$

    This is a one-step lookahead through the model. For each candidate action, inspect every reward and successor it can produce, then score the successor using the old policy's value function. Nothing has been changed yet: $q_\pi(s,a)$ asks, what if I take $a$ once and follow $\pi$ afterwards?

    The old policy's value is its own weighted average of those action values:

    $$ v_\pi(s)=\sum_a\pi(a\mid s)\,q_\pi(s,a). $$

    An average cannot exceed its largest member. Therefore at least one action satisfies $q_\pi(s,a)\ge v_\pi(s)$. A greedy policy chooses such an action in every state:

    $$ \pi'(s)\in\arg\max_a q_\pi(s,a) =\arg\max_a\sum_{s',r}p(s',r\mid s,a)\big[r+\gamma v_\pi(s')\big]. $$

    The policy improvement theorem

    The theorem is stronger than “greedy seems sensible.” It says that if a new deterministic policy $\pi'$ chooses actions satisfying

    $$ q_\pi(s,\pi'(s))\ge v_\pi(s)\qquad\text{for every }s, $$

    then $\pi'$ is at least as good everywhere:

    $$ \boxed{\,v_{\pi'}(s)\ge v_\pi(s)\qquad\text{for every }s\,}. $$

    The proof works by repeatedly replacing the old continuation with the new policy, one future decision at a time.

    Step 1: improve the first decision. Since $\pi'$ satisfies the premise, $$v_\pi(s)\le q_\pi(s,\pi'(s)) =\mathbb{E}_{\pi'}\!\left[R_{t+1}+\gamma v_\pi(S_{t+1})\mid S_t=s\right].$$
    Step 2: improve the decision after that. The same premise holds at every possible successor $S_{t+1}$, so $$v_\pi(S_{t+1})\le \mathbb{E}_{\pi'}\!\left[R_{t+2}+\gamma v_\pi(S_{t+2})\mid S_{t+1}\right].$$ Substitute this upper bound into Step 1: $$v_\pi(s)\le \mathbb{E}_{\pi'}\!\left[R_{t+1}+\gamma R_{t+2} +\gamma^2v_\pi(S_{t+2})\mid S_t=s\right].$$
    Step 3: keep unrolling. Repeating the substitution pushes $v_\pi$ arbitrarily far into the future: $$v_\pi(s)\le \mathbb{E}_{\pi'}\!\left[R_{t+1}+\gamma R_{t+2}+\cdots+ \gamma^k v_\pi(S_{t+k})\mid S_t=s\right].$$ For $\gamma<1$ the tail vanishes as $k\to\infty$; for a proper episodic task it vanishes at termination. What remains is exactly $v_{\pi'}(s)$.

    Greedification satisfies the premise automatically because

    $$ q_\pi(s,\pi'(s))=\max_a q_\pi(s,a) \ge \sum_a\pi(a\mid s)q_\pi(s,a)=v_\pi(s). $$
    Strict improvement needs reachability. If the inequality is strict at a state that can matter from $s$, then $v_{\pi'}(s)>v_\pi(s)$. A strict improvement in an unreachable corner of the MDP need not change the value of every other state.

    Policy iteration

    One greedy step improves a policy but need not make it optimal. The obvious response is to alternate the two operations:

    Policy $\pi_k$the current behaviour
    →
    Evaluatecompute $v_{\pi_k}$
    →
    Improvegreedify to obtain $\pi_{k+1}$
    initialise V(s) and a deterministic policy pi(s) arbitrarily

repeat:
    # 1. Policy evaluation
    repeatedly apply the Bellman expectation backup for pi
    until the largest change delta is below theta

    # 2. Policy improvement
    policy_stable = true
    for each state s:
        old_action = pi(s)
        pi(s) = argmax_a sum_s',r p(s',r|s,a)
                              [r + gamma V(s')]
        if pi(s) != old_action:
            policy_stable = false
until policy_stable

return pi, V

    Why must this terminate in a finite MDP? In the exact-evaluation argument, each improvement is either strict or the policy is already greedy with respect to its own value. A finite MDP has only finitely many deterministic policies (at most $|\mathcal A|^{|\mathcal S|}$ when every action is available everywhere), so a sequence of strict improvements cannot continue forever. When an exactly evaluated policy is unchanged by greedification, it satisfies the Bellman optimality equation and is optimal. With a numerical tolerance $\theta$, both “stable” and “optimal” should be read to the corresponding approximation accuracy.

    Ties need a stable rule. If several actions maximise the lookahead, changing from one tied action to another is not a strict improvement and can make a naive implementation report instability forever. Keep the old action when it is among the maximisers, or use a fixed deterministic tie-break.

    Must evaluation finish every time?

    The clean proof imagines exact evaluation: march all the way to $v_{\pi_k}$, then improve. In practice that is often wasteful. If a few sweeps already reveal which actions are better, improving now can move the policy in the right direction before the values have fully settled.

    Exact policy iteration

    Evaluate nearly to convergence, then improve. Fewer policy changes, more work per change.

    Modified policy iteration

    Perform a fixed or adaptive number of evaluation sweeps, then improve. More policy changes, much cheaper evaluation phases.

    Generalized policy iteration

    Generalized policy iteration (GPI) is the umbrella idea: one process pushes values toward consistency with the current policy; another pushes the policy toward greediness with respect to the current values. They do not need to take turns cleanly or finish their jobs before the other moves.

    This is one of the most reusable mental models in reinforcement learning. Actor–critic methods, for example, run an approximate evaluation process (the critic) beside an approximate improvement process (the actor). Week 4's tabular algorithms are the cleanest place to see the pattern before approximation enters.
