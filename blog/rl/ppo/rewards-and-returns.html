    Rewards, Sparse Signals & Discounted Returns

    July 2026 · 19 min read

    Part 1 ended with the objective $J(\theta) = \mathbb{E}_{\tau \sim \pi_\theta}[R(\tau)]$ and a promissory note: $R(\tau)$, the reward, was left as a black box. This post opens it. We answer two questions that must be settled before any gradient can be taken — where the scalar reward actually comes from, and how a per-step signal is assembled from it — and in doing so we meet the single hardest feature of RL on language: the reward is one number, arriving once, at the very end.

    Where we are. We have a policy, states, actions, and trajectories, and a goal of maximising expected reward. What we do not yet have is the reward itself, nor a way to turn one end-of-episode scalar into something a per-token update can use. By the end of this post we will have defined the return $G_t$ and its backward recursion — the quantity Part 3 will use as the weight in the first policy-gradient loss.

    Reward Models from Human Preferences

    The most general source of reward, and the one that put the “HF” in RLHF, is a learned reward model. You cannot write a program that scores “how helpful is this essay”, but you can ask people which of two essays they prefer, and preferences are cheap to collect. So we gather triples $(x, y_w, y_l)$: a prompt $x$, a response $y_w$ a human ranked higher (the winner), and a response $y_l$ ranked lower (the loser). From these we fit a scalar-valued model $r_\psi(x, y)$.

    The fitting objective is the Bradley–Terry model of pairwise comparison. It posits that the probability a rater prefers $y_w$ over $y_l$ is a logistic function of the score gap:

    $$ P\big(y_w \succ y_l \mid x\big) = \sigma\big(r_\psi(x, y_w) - r_\psi(x, y_l)\big), \qquad \sigma(z) = \frac{1}{1 + e^{-z}}. $$

    Training maximises the likelihood of the observed human choices, which is the same as minimising the negative log-likelihood:

    $$ \mathcal{L}_{\text{RM}}(\psi) = -\,\mathbb{E}_{(x, y_w, y_l)}\Big[\log \sigma\big(r_\psi(x, y_w) - r_\psi(x, y_l)\big)\Big]. $$

    Three facts about this model matter downstream. First, the reward model is usually the SFT model with its head swapped: take the same transformer, discard the vocabulary-projection layer, and attach a single linear unit on the final token's hidden state that emits one number. It already understands language; you are only teaching it to score.

    Second, and easy to miss, the absolute scale of $r_\psi$ is meaningless. Look at the loss: it depends only on the difference $r_\psi(x, y_w) - r_\psi(x, y_l)$. Add any constant $c$ to every score and the difference — and therefore the loss — is unchanged. The reward model is identified only up to an additive shift, so a raw score of “$4.2$” means nothing on its own; only comparisons between responses to the same prompt carry information.

    Third, it is a learned approximation of human judgement, not human judgement itself — and anything learned can be exploited. A policy optimised hard against $r_\psi$ will discover inputs where the model over-scores, producing text that games the proxy rather than pleasing a person. That failure has a name, reward hacking, and a defence we build in Part 9.

    import torch.nn.functional as F

# r_win, r_lose: reward-model scores for the preferred and rejected
# responses to the SAME prompt, each of shape (batch,)
def bt_loss(r_win, r_lose):
    # Bradley-Terry: P(win preferred) = sigmoid(r_win - r_lose).
    # Minimise the negative log-likelihood of the human's choice.
    return -F.logsigmoid(r_win - r_lose).mean()

    Verifiable and Programmatic Rewards

    When the task has a checkable answer, you can skip the learned model entirely and compute reward with a program. Did the final number match the known solution? Do the unit tests pass? Does the output parse as valid JSON in the requested schema? Each is a deterministic function returning, usually, a $0$ or a $1$.

    Verifiable rewards have two great virtues. They are cheap — once the checker is written it costs nothing per sample — and they are hard to fool in the way a reward model is: fluent, confident prose earns nothing if the answer is wrong, because the grader looks only at correctness. This is precisely why they drive the training of reasoning models, where the objective is to be right, not merely to sound right. The trade-off is that the signal is binary and sparse: it tells you the final answer was wrong but not which step went astray, and it offers no gradient of partial credit to climb.

    Un-gameable is too strong. Verifiable does not mean exploit-proof. A policy can still satisfy the letter of the check while violating its intent — hard-coding a test's expected output, or exploiting a loose answer parser. What verifiable rewards remove is the learned-proxy failure mode, not every specification gap. The checker is exactly as robust as you wrote it.

    Process Rewards

    Between a single terminal score and nothing lies a third option: reward the intermediate steps. A process reward model scores each step of a multi-step solution — each line of a proof, each hop of a chain of reasoning — rather than only the final answer. The appeal is direct: it densifies the signal, handing the optimiser feedback at many points instead of one, which sharpens credit assignment enormously.

    The cost is equally direct. Somebody, or some automated process, must produce step-level labels — judgements of whether each intermediate step was correct or on-track. That is far more expensive than labelling only the final answer, and defining what makes a single step “good” in isolation is itself subtle. Process rewards trade annotation effort for a denser learning signal, and whether the trade pays depends on how badly sparsity is hurting you.

    Sparsity and the Credit-Assignment Problem

    Set process rewards aside and take the common case: one scalar, at the end. Make it concrete. A model writes a 400-token solution to a hard problem and earns a reward of $+1$ for getting the answer right. That single number is now the only feedback about 400 separate token decisions. Which tokens deserve the credit? The framing of a key lemma, three-quarters of the way in, that made everything click? Or the 380 routine tokens of connective tissue around it? The reward does not say. It cannot say. It is one number.

    This is the credit-assignment problem, and it is the central difficulty of RL on language. In the naive approach of Part 3, that single $+1$ gets attached to every token equally, as if each of the 400 were equally responsible for success — which is obviously false and, as we will see, catastrophic for the variance of the gradient. The next four posts exist almost entirely to attack this problem: the value function (Part 4) predicts how much reward a position is worth so we can measure surprise, the advantage (Part 5) turns that into a per-token “better or worse than expected”, and GAE (Part 6) tunes the bias–variance trade in doing so. Everything hinges on distributing one distant scalar across many decisions more intelligently than “split it evenly”.

    From Rewards to Returns

    The first, most basic tool for spreading reward across time is the return: the total reward accumulated from a step onward, with later rewards optionally discounted. Formally, the discounted return from step $t$ is

    $$ G_t = \sum_{k=0}^{T-t-1} \gamma^{k}\, r_{t+k} = r_t + \gamma r_{t+1} + \gamma^2 r_{t+2} + \cdots $$

    Written out, $G_t$ telescopes into a clean recursion. Peel off the first term:

    $$ \begin{aligned} G_t &= r_t + \sum_{k=1}^{T-t-1} \gamma^{k}\, r_{t+k} = r_t + \gamma \sum_{k=1}^{T-t-1} \gamma^{k-1}\, r_{t+k} \\[3pt] &= r_t + \gamma \sum_{j=0}^{T-t-2} \gamma^{j}\, r_{(t+1)+j} = r_t + \gamma\, G_{t+1}. \end{aligned} $$

    That last equality, $G_t = r_t + \gamma\, G_{t+1}$, is worth more than it looks. It says every return in a trajectory can be computed in a single backward pass: start at the end with $G_{T-1} = r_{T-1}$, then sweep leftward, each step folding in one reward. Every later post that needs returns, advantages, or GAE leans on exactly this recursion, so it is worth having in code once:

    def compute_returns(rewards, gamma):
    # rewards: list of per-step rewards r_0 .. r_{T-1}
    # returns G_t built from the recursion  G_t = r_t + gamma * G_{t+1}
    T = len(rewards)
    G = [0.0] * T
    running = 0.0
    for t in reversed(range(T)):        # sweep from the end backward
        running = rewards[t] + gamma * running
        G[t] = running
    return G                            # one pass, O(T)

    What is $\gamma$ doing? It has two jobs. Mathematically, for problems that never terminate, $\gamma \lt 1$ is what makes the infinite sum converge — a bounded reward times a geometric series stays finite. Behaviourally, $\gamma$ encodes a preference for sooner reward: at $\gamma = 0.9$ a reward ten steps away is worth only $0.9^{10} \approx 0.35$ of the same reward now.

    Here is where language departs from classical RL. LLM episodes are finite, short, and terminal-reward-only. There is no infinite horizon to tame, and there is no honest reason to prefer an earlier token over a later one when both were necessary to reach a correct answer. So in practice $\gamma$ is set to exactly $1.0$, or a whisker below. To see why anything smaller is actively harmful, put the only reward at the final step, $r_{T-1} = R$ and all else zero. Then the sum collapses to a single surviving term:

    $$ G_t = \gamma^{\,T-1-t}\, R. $$

    With $\gamma \lt 1$ this exponentially discounts the only signal you have. The first token, farthest from the end, sees its credit shrunk by the largest power of $\gamma$, as though it were less responsible for the outcome purely because it came first — a bias with no justification when the reward is terminal.

    A worked return

    Take a five-step trajectory whose rewards are $(r_0, \dots, r_4) = (0, 0, 0, 0, 1)$ — the terminal-reward case in miniature. Applying $G_t = \gamma^{4-t}$, the returns for three choices of $\gamma$ are:

    Read the columns. At $\gamma = 1.0$ every token inherits the full $+1$: all five were jointly necessary, so all five share the credit equally. At $\gamma = 0.99$ the profile is nearly flat — the first token still carries $0.96$ — which is why a value just below one is a harmless safety margin for longer sequences. At $\gamma = 0.9$ the first token's credit has collapsed to $0.66$, a claim that the opening move mattered a third less than the closing one, for no reason the problem supports. That is the discount penalising early tokens for their distance from the end, and it is exactly the artefact $\gamma \approx 1$ avoids.

    Where This Leaves Us

    The black box is open. The reward $R(\tau)$ is a scalar from one of three sources — a preference-trained model, a programmatic verifier, or a per-step process model — and in the common case it is a single number at the end of the episode. From the per-step rewards we defined the return

    $$ G_t = \sum_{k=0}^{T-t-1} \gamma^{k}\, r_{t+k} = r_t + \gamma\, G_{t+1}, \qquad \gamma \approx 1 \text{ for language.} $$

    $G_t$ is our first, crude answer to credit assignment: it is the total reward a token can still be held responsible for. It is crude because it hands the same terminal $+1$ to every token that preceded it, which we already flagged as a variance disaster in waiting. In Part 3 we finally take the gradient of $J(\theta)$, discover that $G_t$ is exactly the weight that falls out of the derivation, and watch precisely how weighting by the raw return goes wrong — setting up the baseline that Part 4 subtracts.
