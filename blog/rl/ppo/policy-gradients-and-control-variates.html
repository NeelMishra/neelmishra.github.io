    Policy Gradients: Why a Log Probability Times an Advantage Works

    September 2026 · 12 min read + experiments

    Derive the actor gradient, work through a two-action example, and see why an unbiased baseline need not reduce variance.

    Read the explanationTry the calculation

    Start with J: what are we trying to improve?

    A policy $\pi_\theta(a\mid s)$ gives the probability of choosing action $a$ in state $s$. Its parameters $\theta$ might be neural-network weights. Running this policy produces an episode, also called a trajectory, $\tau=(s_0,a_0,R_0,\ldots,s_T)$. Here $R_t$ is the reward received after action $a_t$, and $T$ is the number of actions before the episode ends.

    One episode supplies a return $G_0$: add its rewards, discounting later ones by powers of $\gamma$. The objective $J(\theta)$ is the average of that return over all the episodes this policy could generate:

    $$\begin{aligned} G_0(\tau) &= \sum_{t=0}^{T-1}\gamma^t R_t, \\ J(\theta) &= \mathbb{E}_{\tau\sim p_\theta}[G_0(\tau)] \\ &= \sum_\tau p_\theta(\tau)G_0(\tau). \end{aligned}$$

    In the last line, $p_\theta(\tau)$ is the probability of a complete trajectory. Each trajectory contributes its probability times its return. We write a sum for discrete trajectories; continuous variables require integrals. The previous chapter’s reward → return → objective figure separates these quantities and explains how to estimate $J$ from repeated episodes.

    $J(\theta)$ measures performance; $\nabla_\theta J(\theta)$ tells us how that performance changes with the parameters. The gradient contains one partial derivative per parameter. Gradient ascent takes a small step in that direction, with learning rate $\alpha>0$:

    $$\theta_{\mathrm{new}}=\theta+\alpha\nabla_\theta J(\theta).$$

    We rarely know the exact gradient, so we estimate it from sampled experience. The question this chapter answers is why a sampled return multiplied by a log-probability gradient provides such an estimate.

    What does logp actually mean?

    logp is a code variable for the natural logarithm of the probability the policy assigns to the action we actually took:

    $$\mathrm{logp}_t=\log\pi_\theta(a_t\mid s_t).$$

    For example, if the policy assigns probability 0.3 to right and we sample right, then logp is $\log(0.3)\approx-1.204$. If we sample left instead, it is $\log(0.7)\approx-0.357$. These negative numbers are ordinary log probabilities for discrete actions; they do not indicate negative rewards. Making right more likely raises its log probability towards zero.

    The Coursera RL notes in this blog derive this in Course 3, Week 5: the log-likelihood form. Those notes write $\ln\pi$ and call its gradient the eligibility vector or score function. Here $\log$ also means natural log. The course uses a continuing average-reward objective, whereas this chapter uses episodic return; the log-derivative identity is the same.

    Why the logarithm appears in the gradient of J

    Assume the starting-state distribution and the environment’s reward and transition rules do not depend on $\theta$. For a fixed trajectory, its return is then fixed. Changing the policy changes how likely that trajectory is. So differentiate the probability in the objective:

    $$\nabla_\theta J(\theta)=\sum_\tau G_0(\tau)\nabla_\theta p_\theta(\tau).$$

    For positive differentiable probabilities, the chain rule gives $\nabla_\theta\log p_\theta=(\nabla_\theta p_\theta)/p_\theta$. Multiply both sides by $p_\theta$ and substitute:

    $$\begin{aligned} \nabla_\theta J(\theta) &= \sum_\tau p_\theta(\tau)G_0(\tau)\nabla_\theta\log p_\theta(\tau) \\ &= \mathbb{E}_{\tau\sim p_\theta}\!\left[ G_0(\tau)\nabla_\theta\log p_\theta(\tau) \right]. \end{aligned}$$

    The sum now has probability weights, so it is an expectation we can estimate by sampling trajectories. This step assumes the usual regularity conditions for interchanging differentiation and expectation; finite tasks with smooth, strictly positive action probabilities are a simple setting where it applies.

    Why does a trajectory’s log gradient become a sum over actions?

    Write $d_0$ for the fixed starting-state distribution and $P(s_{t+1},R_t\mid s_t,a_t)$ for the environment’s joint distribution of the next state and reward. The trajectory probability factors as

    $$p_\theta(\tau)=d_0(s_0)\prod_{t=0}^{T-1}\pi_\theta(a_t\mid s_t)P(s_{t+1},R_t\mid s_t,a_t).$$

    Taking the logarithm turns the product into a sum. Differentiating removes the $d_0$ and $P$ terms because they have no $\theta$ dependence for a fixed trajectory. Only the policy log probabilities remain. No derivative through the environment is required.

    $$\nabla_\theta\log p_\theta(\tau)=\sum_{t=0}^{T-1}\nabla_\theta\log\pi_\theta(a_t\mid s_t).$$

    Substituting gives the trajectory form of the policy gradient:

    $$\nabla_\theta J(\theta)=\mathbb{E}_{\tau\sim p_\theta}\!\left[ G_0(\tau)\sum_{t=0}^{T-1}\nabla_\theta\log\pi_\theta(a_t\mid s_t) \right].$$

    Each observed episode supplies a return and a sum of log-probability gradients. Averaging their product over fresh episodes estimates the gradient of $J$. The return supplies the weight; the log-probability gradient supplies the direction. See Spinning Up’s policy-gradient derivation for a complementary presentation.

    Give each action only the rewards it can affect

    Rewards earned before action $a_t$ can be removed from its weight: conditioned on the history, they are constants, and the expected policy score at that history is zero. Write $G_t=\sum_{k=t}^{T-1}\gamma^{k-t}R_k$ for the return from action $a_t$ onward. With the discounted start-state objective above, this gives the exact finite-episode identity

    $$\nabla_\theta J(\theta)=\mathbb E_{\tau\sim p_\theta}\!\left[\sum_{t=0}^{T-1}\gamma^t\nabla_\theta\log\pi_\theta(a_t\mid s_t)G_t\right].$$

    The outer $\gamma^t$ belongs here because $G_t$ starts discounting at time $t$, whereas $J$ discounts from time 0. Another presentation absorbs this weighting into a discounted state-occupancy distribution. Many practical PPO implementations uniformly average rollout transitions and use discounting only inside their return estimator, including our lab. That is a practical surrogate convention; it is not an algebraic proof that the outer factor disappears. The GAE paper carefully distinguishes the performance objective and discounted estimators. Schulman et al., GAE.

    Work through both possible samples

    Use the one-step bandit from chapter 1: left pays 1, right pays 3. With right probability $p$, the exact objective is $J(p)=(1-p)\times1+p\times3=1+2p$. We can differentiate it directly, then check whether the sampled policy gradient agrees.

    Let a trainable logit $z$ set $p=\operatorname{sigmoid}(z)$. Since $dp/dz=p(1-p)$, the chain rule gives

    $$\frac{dJ}{dz}=\frac{dJ}{dp}\frac{dp}{dz}=2p(1-p).$$

    At $p=0.3$, the exact derivative is $2(0.3)(0.7)=0.42$. Increasing $z$ slightly therefore increases expected reward. Now compute the gradient from each possible sampled action. Differentiating its log probability gives

    $$\begin{aligned} \frac{d}{dz}\log\pi_z(\mathrm{right}) &= \frac{1}{p}\frac{dp}{dz}=1-p, \\ \frac{d}{dz}\log\pi_z(\mathrm{left}) &= -\frac{1}{1-p}\frac{dp}{dz}=-p. \end{aligned}$$

    The log probability itself and its derivative are different quantities. Here are both, followed by the return-weighted derivative used for learning:

    Two possible samples at p = 0.3, with no baseline
    Sampled actionProbabilitylogp$d\,\mathrm{logp}/dz$$G_0$$G_0\,d\,\mathrm{logp}/dz$
    Left0.7−0.357−0.31−0.3
    Right0.3−1.204+0.73+2.1

    Average the last column using the action probabilities:

    $$\mathbb{E}\!\left[G_0\frac{d\,\mathrm{logp}}{dz}\right]=0.7(-0.3)+0.3(2.1)=0.42=\frac{dJ}{dz}.$$

    One sample can point in the wrong direction; its probability-weighted mean matches the exact derivative. For the corresponding calculation with several actions, see the Coursera notes on deriving the softmax actor update.

    A positive reward on a left sample increases left’s probability, even though left is worse than right. Nothing is mathematically wrong: the better action wins in expectation. But this is a noisy way to learn. Subtracting the expected reward $V=1.6$ changes left’s weight to −0.6 and right’s weight to +1.4. Their gradients become +0.18 and +0.98 with respect to the right logit. The expectation stays 0.42.

    The baseline identity has one essential condition

    For a fixed state, an action-independent baseline $b(s)$ contributes

    $$\mathbb E_{a\sim\pi}[b(s)\nabla\log\pi(a\mid s)]=b(s)\sum_a\nabla\pi(a\mid s)=b(s)\nabla 1=0.$$

    This proves that subtraction does not change the expected score-function gradient. It does not prove that every baseline lowers variance. If you subtract 100 from rewards near 1, the sample gradients become enormous. Also, a baseline that depends on the sampled action cannot simply be pulled outside the action sum; it needs a separate correction or justification.

    For a scalar baseline and score vector $g=\nabla\log\pi$, minimizing the estimator’s second moment yields $b^*=\mathbb E[G\lVert g\rVert^2]/\mathbb E[\lVert g\rVert^2]$. The weights depend on score magnitude. In our bandit, this is $b^*=2.4$, whereas the value baseline is 1.6. At 2.4 the two possible sample gradients both equal 0.42, so variance is zero. Deterministic two-action rewards make this example unusually tidy; real trajectories retain substantial noise.

    Why a critic is still useful

    The value function $V^\pi(s)=\mathbb E[G_t\mid s_t=s]$ is a useful baseline because it removes predictable differences between states. Near the goal, a return of 0.9 may be ordinary; far away, the same return may be surprisingly good. The action value $Q^\pi(s,a)=\mathbb E[G_t\mid s_t=s,a_t=a]$ and advantage $A^\pi=Q^\pi-V^\pi$ express that comparison.

    A learned critic makes this baseline practical across many states. It also supports bootstrapping, where future return is partly replaced by a value prediction. Baseline subtraction alone preserves the expected gradient under its conditions; bootstrapping with an imperfect value function can introduce bias. The GAE chapter separates those two operations.

    Translate the identity into an optimizer loss

    Let a batch contain $B$ sampled transitions, indexed by $i$, with fixed advantage estimates $\hat A_i$. The practical mean-over-transitions convention discussed above uses this actor loss:

    $$L_{\mathrm{actor}}(\theta)=-\frac{1}{B}\sum_{i=1}^{B}\log\pi_\theta(a_i\mid s_i)\,\hat A_i.$$

    Holding the advantage weights fixed means differentiating this expression produces the negative of their weighted log-probability gradients. An optimizer minimizes the loss, so the minus sign makes it move the actor in the ascent direction. For a discrete-action policy, the code reads:

    dist = policy(obs)                 # Categorical distribution for each state
logp = dist.log_prob(action)        # log πθ(a_i | s_i), shape [B]
actor_loss = -(logp * advantage.detach()).mean()
# Optimizers minimize; the minus sign turns ascent into descent.

    logp contains one log probability per sampled action, and advantage must also have shape [B]. An advantage of shape [B, 1] would broadcast against [B] into a matrix and mix unrelated samples. If the log-probability notation is unfamiliar, revisit the Coursera log-likelihood explanation.

    The advantage is treated as a fixed weight for the actor derivative. If a shared actor-critic network produced the baseline, letting actor gradients flow through that weight introduces an extra term outside this derivation. The critic gets its own regression loss instead. The snippet is the basic policy-gradient loss; PPO later replaces it with a ratio-based surrogate for reuse of a recent rollout.

    Do not mistake an actor-loss decrease for a direct measurement of improved return. It is improvement on a sampled, weighted prediction problem. A new environment evaluation measures whether the changed action distribution actually produces better trajectories.

    Check your understanding: If every advantage is negative, must the policy gradient be zero?

    No. The weighted score vectors need not cancel. A constant action-independent baseline cancels only in expectation over actions; a finite sample is not that expectation. Advantage normalization is another finite-batch transformation, not a guarantee about individual action updates.
