    Policy Gradients: Why a Log Probability Times an Advantage Works

    September 2026 · 13 min read

    Understand the policy-gradient derivation, then turn returns into advantages and a simple actor loss.

    Read the explanationSee the baseline example

    Start with J: what are we trying to improve?

    A policy $\pi_\theta(a\mid s)$ gives the probability of choosing action $a$ in state $s$. Its parameters $\theta$ might be neural-network weights. Running this policy produces an episode, also called a trajectory, $\tau=(s_0,a_0,R_0,\ldots,s_T)$. Here $R_t$ is the reward received after action $a_t$, and $T$ is the number of actions before the episode ends.

    One episode supplies a return $G_0$: add its rewards, discounting later ones by powers of $\gamma$. The objective $J(\theta)$ is the average of that return over all the episodes this policy could generate:

    $$\begin{aligned} G_0(\tau) &= \sum_{t=0}^{T-1}\gamma^t R_t, \\ J(\theta) &= \mathbb{E}_{\tau\sim p_\theta}[G_0(\tau)] \\ &= \sum_\tau p_\theta(\tau)G_0(\tau). \end{aligned}$$

    In the last line, $p_\theta(\tau)$ is the probability of a complete trajectory. Each trajectory contributes its probability times its return. We write a sum for discrete trajectories; continuous variables require integrals. The previous chapter’s reward → return → objective figure separates these quantities and explains how to estimate $J$ from repeated episodes.

    $J(\theta)$ measures performance; $\nabla_\theta J(\theta)$ tells us how that performance changes with the parameters. The gradient contains one partial derivative per parameter. Gradient ascent takes a small step in that direction, with learning rate $\alpha>0$:

    $$\theta_{\mathrm{new}}=\theta+\alpha\nabla_\theta J(\theta).$$

    We rarely know the exact gradient, so we estimate it from sampled experience. The question this chapter answers is why a sampled return multiplied by a log-probability gradient provides such an estimate.

    What does logp actually mean?

    logp is a code variable for the natural logarithm of the probability the policy assigns to the action we actually took:

    $$\mathrm{logp}_t=\log\pi_\theta(a_t\mid s_t).$$

    For example, if the policy assigns probability 0.3 to right and we sample right, then logp is $\log(0.3)\approx-1.204$. If we sample left instead, it is $\log(0.7)\approx-0.357$. These negative numbers are ordinary log probabilities for discrete actions; they do not indicate negative rewards. Making right more likely raises its log probability towards zero.

    The Coursera RL notes in this blog derive this in Course 3, Week 5: the log-likelihood form. Those notes write $\ln\pi$ and call its gradient the eligibility vector or score function. Here $\log$ also means natural log. The course uses a continuing average-reward objective, whereas this chapter uses episodic return; the log-derivative identity is the same.

    Why the logarithm appears in the gradient of J

    Assume the starting-state distribution and the environment’s reward and transition rules do not depend on $\theta$. For a fixed trajectory, its return is then fixed. Changing the policy changes how likely that trajectory is. So differentiate the probability in the objective:

    $$\nabla_\theta J(\theta)=\sum_\tau G_0(\tau)\nabla_\theta p_\theta(\tau).$$

    For positive differentiable probabilities, the chain rule gives $\nabla_\theta\log p_\theta=(\nabla_\theta p_\theta)/p_\theta$. Multiply both sides by $p_\theta$ and substitute:

    $$\begin{aligned} \nabla_\theta J(\theta) &= \sum_\tau p_\theta(\tau)G_0(\tau)\nabla_\theta\log p_\theta(\tau) \\ &= \mathbb{E}_{\tau\sim p_\theta}\!\left[ G_0(\tau)\nabla_\theta\log p_\theta(\tau) \right]. \end{aligned}$$

    The sum now has probability weights, so it is an expectation we can estimate by sampling trajectories. This step assumes the usual regularity conditions for interchanging differentiation and expectation; finite tasks with smooth, strictly positive action probabilities are a simple setting where it applies.

    Why does a trajectory’s log gradient become a sum over actions?

    Write $d_0$ for the fixed starting-state distribution and $P(s_{t+1},R_t\mid s_t,a_t)$ for the environment’s joint distribution of the next state and reward. The trajectory probability factors as

    $$p_\theta(\tau)=d_0(s_0)\prod_{t=0}^{T-1}\pi_\theta(a_t\mid s_t)P(s_{t+1},R_t\mid s_t,a_t).$$

    Taking the logarithm turns the product into a sum. Differentiating removes the $d_0$ and $P$ terms because they have no $\theta$ dependence for a fixed trajectory. Only the policy log probabilities remain. No derivative through the environment is required.

    $$\nabla_\theta\log p_\theta(\tau)=\sum_{t=0}^{T-1}\nabla_\theta\log\pi_\theta(a_t\mid s_t).$$

    Substituting gives the trajectory form of the policy gradient:

    $$\nabla_\theta J(\theta)=\mathbb{E}_{\tau\sim p_\theta}\!\left[ G_0(\tau)\sum_{t=0}^{T-1}\nabla_\theta\log\pi_\theta(a_t\mid s_t) \right].$$

    Each observed episode supplies a return and a sum of log-probability gradients. Averaging their product over fresh episodes estimates the gradient of $J$. The return supplies the weight; the log-probability gradient supplies the direction. See Spinning Up’s policy-gradient derivation for a complementary presentation.

    From the whole episode to each action’s remaining rewards

    The objective above discounts every reward relative to the episode’s start. For a three-action episode, that means

    $$J(\theta)=\mathbb E_{\tau\sim p_\theta}[R_0+\gamma R_1+\gamma^2R_2].$$

    Now consider the action at time 1. It can influence $R_1$ and $R_2$, but $R_0$ has already happened. We can remove that earlier reward from this action’s learning signal while preserving the expected gradient. The remaining rewards must keep the discounts they had in $J$. This is where the outer factor comes from.

    First remove rewards that happened before the action

    To keep the algebra short, write $g_t$ for the log-probability gradient of the sampled action. It is a vector, with one entry per policy parameter:

    $$g_t=\nabla_\theta\log\pi_\theta(a_t\mid s_t).$$

    The trajectory formula we just derived is $\nabla_\theta J=\mathbb E[\sum_t g_tG_0]$. For each action, split its weight $G_0$ into the rewards before that action and the rewards from that action onward:

    $$\begin{aligned} G_0 &=\underbrace{\sum_{k=0}^{t-1}\gamma^kR_k}_{C_t:\ \text{past rewards}} +\underbrace{\sum_{k=t}^{T-1}\gamma^kR_k}_{\text{remaining rewards}}. \end{aligned}$$

    We want to show that the past-reward contribution $C_tg_t$ averages to zero. Imagine pausing an episode just before choosing action $a_t$. Call everything observed so far $H_t$: the states, earlier actions, and rewards, ending at the current state $s_t$. At this pause, $C_t$ is already known. The next action is still random.

    Why does the log-probability gradient average to zero?

    The reason is that action probabilities always add to 1. Hold the history fixed and average over the actions the policy could choose next. This is what the conditional expectation $\mathbb E[g_t\mid H_t]$ means. It is a probability-weighted average, not an equally weighted average of actions.

    To make the proof easier to read, abbreviate $\pi_\theta(a\mid s_t)$ as $p(a)$. The dependence on $\theta$ is still there; only the notation is shorter. For a finite action set with positive, differentiable probabilities, such as a softmax policy, the log-derivative rule gives

    $$\nabla_\theta\log p(a)=\frac{\nabla_\theta p(a)}{p(a)}.$$

    Substitute that rule into the action average:

    $$\begin{aligned} \mathbb E[g_t\mid H_t] &=\sum_a p(a)\nabla_\theta\log p(a)\\ &=\sum_a p(a)\frac{\nabla_\theta p(a)}{p(a)}\\ &=\sum_a\nabla_\theta p(a)\\ &=\nabla_\theta\!\left(\sum_a p(a)\right)\\ &=\nabla_\theta 1=0. \end{aligned}$$

    The probability in the expectation cancels the denominator from the log derivative. We can then move the gradient outside the finite sum. That sum is 1 for every parameter setting, and the derivative of a constant is zero. Increasing some action probabilities must be balanced by decreases elsewhere.

    Now multiply by the rewards already received

    At the paused history, choosing the next action cannot change $C_t$. It multiplies every possible next action’s gradient by the same number, so it can come outside the conditional expectation:

    $$\begin{aligned} \mathbb E[C_tg_t\mid H_t] &=C_t\,\mathbb E[g_t\mid H_t]\\ &=C_t\times0=0. \end{aligned}$$

    Another history may have a different past total and different action probabilities. The same argument holds separately at that history.

    Finally, average over all the histories the policy could reach. Averaging a conditional mean of zero at every history still gives zero. This is the law of total expectation:

    $$\begin{aligned} \mathbb E[C_tg_t] &=\mathbb E_{H_t}\!\left[\mathbb E[C_tg_t\mid H_t]\right]\\ &=\mathbb E_{H_t}[0]=0. \end{aligned}$$

    We did not assume that past rewards and current actions are independent across episodes: the current state can connect them. We first fixed the history, which made the past rewards constant, and only then averaged over actions. At time 0 there are no earlier rewards, so $C_0=0$ immediately.

    Why does this not make the whole policy gradient zero? Future returns can depend on which action we choose. They can weight the action gradients differently, breaking the cancellation. In the one-step example below, right pays 3 and left pays 1, so the two actions receive different return weights. That dependence supplies the learning signal. Past rewards contribute zero in expectation, so removing them changes individual sample gradients while preserving their mean. The same reasoning will justify an action-independent baseline.

    Put the return on the episode’s discount scale

    The usual return $G_t$ measures delays from the current action. Its first reward has weight 1, the next has weight $\gamma$, and so on:

    $$G_t=\sum_{k=t}^{T-1}\gamma^{k-t}R_k.$$

    But the remaining-reward sum in $G_0$ measures delays from time 0. Factoring out $\gamma^t$ connects the two:

    $$\begin{aligned} \sum_{k=t}^{T-1}\gamma^kR_k &=\gamma^t\sum_{k=t}^{T-1}\gamma^{k-t}R_k\\ &=\gamma^tG_t. \end{aligned}$$

    The two factors do not discount a reward twice. They cover two parts of the same delay: from the episode’s start to action $t$, then from action $t$ to reward $k$:

    $$\underbrace{\gamma^t}_{0\ \text{to}\ t}\;\times\; \underbrace{\gamma^{k-t}}_{t\ \text{to}\ k} =\underbrace{\gamma^k}_{0\ \text{to}\ k}.$$

    Substitute $G_0=C_t+\gamma^tG_t$ into the trajectory gradient and use the zero-expectation result for $C_tg_t$:

    $$\begin{aligned} \nabla_\theta J(\theta) &=\mathbb E_{\tau\sim p_\theta}\!\left[\sum_{t=0}^{T-1}g_t(C_t+\gamma^tG_t)\right]\\ &=\mathbb E_{\tau\sim p_\theta}\!\left[\sum_{t=0}^{T-1}\gamma^t g_tG_t\right]. \end{aligned}$$

    Writing out $g_t$ again gives the exact finite-episode identity:

    $$\nabla_\theta J(\theta)=\mathbb E_{\tau\sim p_\theta}\!\left[\sum_{t=0}^{T-1}\gamma^t\nabla_\theta\log\pi_\theta(a_t\mid s_t)G_t\right].$$

    Compare the return with a baseline

    The critic predicts the return from a state, $V(s_t)$. Use this prediction as a baseline. The advantage estimate measures how much better or worse the observed return was:

    $$\hat A_t=G_t-V(s_t).$$

    A baseline that does not depend on the chosen action preserves the expected gradient and can reduce noise. This uses the cancellation we already proved.

    Use the advantage to update the policy

    The actor is the policy. A positive advantage says “encourage this action”; a negative one says “discourage it.”

    Minimize −logp × advantage, keeping the advantage fixed while differentiating. Weight each step by $\gamma^t$ to match the objective derived above.

    Optional: what does “absorb the weighting into the state distribution” mean?

    A state distribution describes how often states appear in an average. Some formulas count state visits with their discounts already attached, so $\gamma^t$ need not appear separately.

    Suppose every episode visits A at time 0 and B at time 1. With $\gamma=0.5$, A gets weight 1 and B gets weight 0.5: A counts twice as much as B. Grouping these discounted visits by state is what “absorb the weighting” means.

    These weights need not sum to 1. If converted into probabilities, their total stays as a multiplier outside the average. Formal definition: Sutton et al.

    In the PPO lab, transitions are weighted equally, with discounting inside the advantage estimates. This is a practical training convention; it does not exactly match the objective derived above.
