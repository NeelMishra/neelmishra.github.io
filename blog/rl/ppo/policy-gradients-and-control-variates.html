    Policy Gradients: Why a Log Probability Times an Advantage Works

    September 2026 · 5 min read + experiments

    Derive the actor gradient, work through a two-action example, and see why an unbiased baseline need not reduce variance.

    See the ideaRead the explanationTry the calculation

    Differentiate the distribution, not the environment

    Let $\tau=(s_0,a_0,R_0,\ldots)$ be a trajectory. Its probability factors into the initial-state distribution, policy probabilities, and environment transition probabilities. Assume only the policy depends on $\theta$. Even when the environment is a simulator with no usable derivative, the policy factors are differentiable.

    $$\nabla_\theta J=\nabla_\theta\sum_\tau p_\theta(\tau)G_0(\tau)=\mathbb E_\tau\!\left[G_0\nabla_\theta\log p_\theta(\tau)\right]$$
    $$\nabla_\theta\log p_\theta(\tau)=\sum_t\nabla_\theta\log\pi_\theta(a_t\mid s_t)$$

    The identity $\nabla p=p\nabla\log p$ converts a sum over all trajectories into an expectation we can estimate with sampled trajectories. The return is a weight on a log-probability gradient. We do not ask the network to predict the action’s reward as its action probability. The critic will solve a regression problem; the actor solves a different problem.

    Rewards earned before action $a_t$ can be removed from its weight: conditioned on the history, they are constants, and the expected policy score at that history is zero. With the discounted start-state objective from chapter 1, this gives the exact finite-episode identity

    $$\nabla J=\mathbb E\!\left[\sum_t\gamma^t\nabla\log\pi_\theta(a_t\mid s_t)G_t\right].$$

    The outer $\gamma^t$ belongs here because $G_t$ starts discounting at time $t$, whereas $J$ discounts from time 0. Another presentation absorbs this weighting into a discounted state-occupancy distribution. Many practical PPO implementations uniformly average rollout transitions and use discounting only inside their return estimator, including our lab. That is a practical surrogate convention; it is not an algebraic proof that the outer factor disappears. The GAE paper carefully distinguishes the performance objective and discounted estimators. Schulman et al., GAE.

    Work through both possible samples

    Use the one-step bandit: left pays 1, right pays 3, and $p=P(\mathrm{right})=0.3$. For the right-action logit $z$, the score is $1-p=0.7$ on a right sample and $-p=-0.3$ on a left sample. Without a baseline, the sampled gradients are 2.1 and −0.3. Their expectation is $0.3(2.1)+0.7(-0.3)=0.42$, exactly $2p(1-p)$.

    A positive reward on a left sample increases left’s probability, even though left is worse than right. Nothing is mathematically wrong: the better action wins in expectation. But this is a noisy way to learn. Subtracting the expected reward $V=1.6$ changes left’s weight to −0.6 and right’s weight to +1.4. Their gradients become +0.18 and +0.98 with respect to the right logit. The expectation stays 0.42.

    The baseline identity has one essential condition

    For a fixed state, an action-independent baseline $b(s)$ contributes

    $$\mathbb E_{a\sim\pi}[b(s)\nabla\log\pi(a\mid s)]=b(s)\sum_a\nabla\pi(a\mid s)=b(s)\nabla 1=0.$$

    This proves that subtraction does not change the expected score-function gradient. It does not prove that every baseline lowers variance. If you subtract 100 from rewards near 1, the sample gradients become enormous. Also, a baseline that depends on the sampled action cannot simply be pulled outside the action sum; it needs a separate correction or justification.

    For a scalar baseline and score vector $g=\nabla\log\pi$, minimizing the estimator’s second moment yields $b^*=\mathbb E[G\lVert g\rVert^2]/\mathbb E[\lVert g\rVert^2]$. The weights depend on score magnitude. In our bandit, this is $b^*=2.4$, whereas the value baseline is 1.6. At 2.4 the two possible sample gradients both equal 0.42, so variance is zero. Deterministic two-action rewards make this example unusually tidy; real trajectories retain substantial noise.

    Why a critic is still useful

    The value function $V^\pi(s)=\mathbb E[G_t\mid s_t=s]$ is a useful baseline because it removes predictable differences between states. Near the goal, a return of 0.9 may be ordinary; far away, the same return may be surprisingly good. The action value $Q^\pi(s,a)=\mathbb E[G_t\mid s_t=s,a_t=a]$ and advantage $A^\pi=Q^\pi-V^\pi$ express that comparison.

    A learned critic makes this baseline practical across many states. It also supports bootstrapping, where future return is partly replaced by a value prediction. Baseline subtraction alone preserves the expected gradient under its conditions; bootstrapping with an imperfect value function can introduce bias. The GAE chapter separates those two operations.

    Translate the identity into an optimizer loss

    dist = policy(obs)
logp = dist.log_prob(action)
actor_loss = -(logp * advantage.detach()).mean()
# Optimizers minimize; the minus sign turns ascent into descent.

    The advantage is treated as a fixed weight for the actor derivative. If a shared actor-critic network produced the baseline, letting actor gradients flow through that weight introduces an extra term outside this derivation. The critic gets its own regression loss instead. The snippet is the basic policy-gradient loss; PPO later replaces it with a ratio-based surrogate for reuse of a recent rollout.

    Do not mistake an actor-loss decrease for a direct measurement of improved return. It is improvement on a sampled, weighted prediction problem. A new environment evaluation measures whether the changed action distribution actually produces better trajectories.

    Check your understanding: If every advantage is negative, must the policy gradient be zero?

    No. The weighted score vectors need not cancel. A constant action-independent baseline cancels only in expectation over actions; a finite sample is not that expectation. Advantage normalization is another finite-batch transformation, not a guarantee about individual action updates.
