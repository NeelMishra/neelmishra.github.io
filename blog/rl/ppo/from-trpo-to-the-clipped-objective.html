    PPO Clipping: The Four Cases, the Geometry, and the Limits

    September 2026 · 5 min read + experiments

    Derive the likelihood-ratio surrogate, connect it to TRPO, and inspect exactly when clipping removes a sample’s gradient.

    See the ideaRead the explanationTry the calculation

    Why introduce a ratio at all?

    At a fixed state, suppose actions were sampled from $\pi_{\mathrm{old}}$ but we want an expectation under $\pi_\theta$. If the new distribution’s support is covered by the old one, multiplying each old sample by $\rho=\pi_\theta(a\mid s)/\pi_{\mathrm{old}}(a\mid s)$ reweights that action expectation. PPO builds a local policy-improvement surrogate from this identity:

    $$L^{\mathrm{sur}}(\theta)=\mathbb E_{s,a\sim\mathrm{old}}[\rho_t(\theta)\hat A_t],\qquad \rho_t=\exp(\log\pi_\theta(a_t\mid s_t)-\log\pi_{\mathrm{old}}(a_t\mid s_t)).$$

    At $\theta=\theta_{\mathrm{old}}$, every ratio is one and $\nabla\rho_t=\nabla\log\pi_\theta(a_t\mid s_t)$. The surrogate therefore has the sampled advantage-weighted policy gradient at the collection policy. This is the useful local connection to the previous chapters.

    It does not make an old rollout an unbiased estimate of an arbitrary new policy’s full return. The state distribution is still the old one, and the advantages were estimated for the old policy. Full trajectory importance sampling would involve products of ratios and can have severe variance. PPO chooses local reuse and frequent recollection instead of claiming a general replay correction.

    The trust-region motivation

    TRPO seeks surrogate improvement subject to a policy-distance constraint, using an average KL approximation in its practical algorithm. Its theoretical development relates performance to a surrogate and a stronger divergence control under assumptions; practical implementations use approximations. PPO-Clip replaces that constrained optimization procedure with a first-order clipped surrogate. The connection explains the motivation, but it does not transfer a hard trust-region guarantee to PPO. TRPO paper, PPO paper.

    $$L^{\mathrm{clip}}(\theta)=\mathbb E_t\!\left[\min\!\left(\rho_t\hat A_t,\operatorname{clip}(\rho_t,1-\epsilon,1+\epsilon)\hat A_t\right)\right].$$

    Read the formula in order: compute the actual ratio; compute a clamped copy; multiply both by the same frozen advantage; take the smaller contribution. The minimum is an elementwise pessimistic choice relative to the unclipped surrogate term. It is not a proven lower bound on real-world return.

    The sign of the advantage decides which side is flat

    AdvantageRatio regionContributionDerivative with respect to ρ
    A > 0ρ ≤ 1 + ερAA
    A > 0ρ > 1 + ε(1 + ε)A0
    A < 0ρ < 1 − ε(1 − ε)A0
    A < 0ρ ≥ 1 − ερAA

    At the exact boundary, there is a kink and the derivative needs a subgradient convention. Away from it, the table is the whole story. An advantageous action is protected against further increases once its ratio is high enough. A disadvantageous action is protected against further decreases once its ratio is low enough. Moves in the harmful direction retain their penalty, even far outside the clip interval.

    For $A=-2$ and $\rho=0.5$, the two candidate terms are −1 and −1.6, so PPO chooses −1.6. For the same negative advantage and $\rho=1.5$, it chooses −3 rather than −2.4. This asymmetry is why simply multiplying a clamped ratio by the advantage is the wrong implementation.

    Why a flat term is not a hard boundary

    A neural-network parameter influences many states and actions. Suppose one favorable sample is already at $\rho=1.4$ with $\epsilon=0.2$, so its contribution is flat. Another sample can still update shared parameters and raise the first ratio further. Entropy regularization can do the same. If actor and critic share a trunk, the value loss can move the policy too.

    Even the step that first crosses the clip boundary can overshoot it. The optimizer evaluates a gradient at the current parameters and then takes a finite step; the objective does not project the resulting distribution into a constrained set. This is why implementations often combine a modest learning rate, a limited number of epochs, gradient-norm clipping, and KL-based early stopping. They serve different purposes.

    The loss in five lines

    log_ratio = new_logp - old_logp       # old_logp is frozen
ratio = log_ratio.exp()
unclipped = ratio * advantage        # advantage is frozen
clipped = ratio.clamp(1-eps, 1+eps) * advantage
actor_loss = -torch.minimum(unclipped, clipped).mean()

    Compute log probabilities with the distribution’s stable log-probability routine. Subtract logs before exponentiating, rather than dividing tiny probabilities. Extremely large log ratios can still overflow; that is evidence of a failed update or mismatched likelihoods, not a reason to silently invent an arbitrary ratio cap. The lab checks all four gradient cases and the initial-ratio invariant before training.

    Check your understanding: For A = +2, ε = 0.2 and ρ = 0.5, is the sample clipped flat?

    No. Its contribution is 1 and its derivative with respect to the ratio is +2. Reducing a good action’s probability is the harmful direction, so the objective continues to push back.
