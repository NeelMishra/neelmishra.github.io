    PPO Clipping: The Four Cases, the Geometry, and the Limits

    September 2026 · 4 min read

    Understand the current/old probability ratio, work through four clipping cases, and see what clipping can and cannot control.

    Read the explanationSee the four cases

    How much more likely did this action become?

    Chapter 3 gave each sampled action an advantage: positive means better than expected, negative means worse. PPO now uses that batch for several optimizer steps. We need to track how far the updated policy has moved from the policy that collected it.

    Call the collector $\pi_{\mathrm{old}}$ and the trainable policy $\pi_\theta$. For the same stored state $s_t$ and action $a_t$, divide the new probability by the old probability. This is the probability ratio $\rho_t$:

    $$\rho_t(\theta)=\frac{\pi_\theta(a_t\mid s_t)}{\pi_{\mathrm{old}}(a_t\mid s_t)}.$$

    If an action’s probability changes from 0.25 to 0.30, its ratio is 1.2: it became 20% more likely. A ratio of 1 means unchanged; a ratio below 1 means less likely. The denominator stays fixed throughout this batch.

    Multiplying the ratio by the saved advantage gives a simple score, $\rho_t\hat A_t$, to maximize. For a positive advantage, increasing the ratio improves this score. For a negative advantage, decreasing the ratio improves it. Initially, its gradient is the sampled action’s log-probability gradient times its advantage. This uses the uniform transition weighting described in chapter 2.

    Stop rewarding changes that have gone far enough

    Repeatedly maximizing that score can push the policy too far on one batch. PPO chooses a small tolerance $\epsilon$, often illustrated as 0.2. It compares the actual ratio with a copy restricted to $[1-\epsilon,1+\epsilon]$, or $[0.8,1.2]$ in this example.

    Multiply both copies by the same advantage and keep the smaller score. For one sample, the clipped objective is:

    $$\ell_t^{\mathrm{clip}}=\min\!\left(\rho_t\hat A_t,\operatorname{clip}(\rho_t,1-\epsilon,1+\epsilon)\hat A_t\right).$$

    Here clip restricts its input to the specified interval. PPO averages these sample scores and maximizes the result. This rule is Equation 7 in Schulman et al., PPO. Its motivation comes from limiting policy changes, as in trust-region policy optimization (TRPO), but the clipping rule itself is simple to optimize with ordinary gradient methods.

    The advantage sign decides where the score stops improving

    Evaluating the minimum above gives these four cases, also explained in Spinning Up’s PPO guide. The saved advantage $\hat A_t$ stays fixed during the update. The slope, $\partial\ell_t^{\mathrm{clip}}/\partial\rho_t$, tells us how this sample’s score changes as its ratio increases.

    AdvantageRatioSelected score $\ell_t^{\mathrm{clip}}$Slope and effect
    Positive
    $\hat A_t>0$    		$\rho_t<1+\epsilon$$\rho_t\hat A_t$Slope $=\hat A_t>0$. Increasing the ratio makes the action more likely and raises the score.
    Positive
    $\hat A_t>0$    		$\rho_t>1+\epsilon$$(1+\epsilon)\hat A_t$Slope $=0$. The score is constant; increasing the ratio further earns no extra credit.
    Negative
    $\hat A_t<0$    		$\rho_t>1-\epsilon$$\rho_t\hat A_t$Slope $=\hat A_t<0$. Decreasing the ratio makes the action less likely and raises the score.
    Negative
    $\hat A_t<0$    		$\rho_t<1-\epsilon$$(1-\epsilon)\hat A_t$Slope $=0$. The score is constant; decreasing the ratio further earns no extra credit.

    Clipping is an incentive limit

    Clipping changes how an update is scored; it does not force the actor’s output probabilities to stay within a limit. The actual ratio is still $\rho_t=\pi_\theta(a_t\mid s_t)/\pi_{\mathrm{old}}(a_t\mid s_t)$. The clip operation makes a restricted copy for the objective, without changing either probability.

    For example, take a positive advantage and $\epsilon=0.2$. At a ratio of 1.19, this sample still encourages an increase. The optimizer computes that gradient and takes a finite step, which could move the ratio to 1.23. When we next evaluate the objective, the clipped copy is 1.2, while the actual ratio remains 1.23. This sample’s score is now flat: it stops encouraging an increase, but supplies no gradient to bring the ratio back to 1.2.

    Also, all samples update the same actor network. Updates encouraged by other samples can keep changing this action’s probability even when its own score is flat. PPO therefore needs sensible learning rates and limited batch reuse. Chapter 5 explains how to stop batch reuse when the policy moves too far.

    Translate the rule directly into code

    Let $p_{\mathrm{new}}$ and $p_{\mathrm{old}}$ be the new and old probabilities for the same stored action and state. new_logp and old_logp are their natural logs. Subtracting logs gives the log of a ratio, and .exp() undoes the log:

    $$\exp\!\left(\log p_{\mathrm{new}}-\log p_{\mathrm{old}}\right)=\exp\!\left(\log\frac{p_{\mathrm{new}}}{p_{\mathrm{old}}}\right)=\frac{p_{\mathrm{new}}}{p_{\mathrm{old}}}=\rho_t.$$

    This gives exactly the same ratio while avoiding direct division of two tiny probabilities. Keep old_logp and advantage fixed. The minus sign turns the maximization score into a loss for the optimizer:

    ratio = (new_logp - old_logp).exp()
unclipped = ratio * advantage
clipped = ratio.clamp(1-eps, 1+eps) * advantage
actor_loss = -torch.minimum(unclipped, clipped).mean()

    Each array has one entry per sampled action. The lab checks both advantage signs and both flat regions. Next, we put this loss into a complete PPO update.
