    Implementation Details & Debugging

    July 2026 · 22 min read

    Part 10 finished the objective. If the algorithm were the objective, you could implement PPO from that one line and be done. You cannot. The uncomfortable open secret of policy-gradient RL is that the published loss is maybe half of what determines whether a run succeeds; the other half is a pile of implementation choices that no paper puts in its abstract, that vary between codebases, and that turn a diverging run into a converging one. This post is about that second half: the details that actually move outcomes, the handful of knobs that matter, the dashboard that tells you what is happening, and the named ways it fails.

    Where We Are: The Objective Is Not the Algorithm

    The loss from Part 10 assumes clean advantages, well-scaled gradients, correctly masked sequences, and a critic that tracks returns. Every one of those is an assumption you have to actively engineer. Two careful groups implemented the same PPO equations and got materially different results; the discrepancy lived entirely in code-level choices, which is the thesis of Engstrom et al.’s “Implementation Matters” and the motivation for Huang et al.’s catalogue of thirty-seven PPO details. Treat what follows as the missing appendix to the objective.

    The Details That Actually Change Outcomes

    Advantage normalisation (whitening). After GAE, subtract the mean and divide by the standard deviation of the advantages so the batch has zero mean and unit variance. Without it, the scale of the policy gradient rides on the arbitrary scale of the reward, and the effective learning rate wanders as reward magnitude drifts. The subtlety is where you normalise: per-minibatch whitening uses statistics from a few dozen tokens and is noisier; per-batch (whole-rollout) whitening is more stable and is the usual choice. Mixing them up silently changes the effective step size.

    Value-loss clipping. Many codebases clip the critic’s prediction to a trust region around its old value, mirroring the policy clip. It is widely copied and, on the evidence, of dubious benefit — controlled studies find it rarely helps and sometimes hurts. Keep it if your reference implementation has it and you are matching results; do not treat it as load-bearing.

    Global gradient-norm clipping. Clip the total gradient norm across all parameters to a fixed ceiling (commonly $0.5$ or $1.0$) before the optimiser step. This is the cheapest insurance in the whole loop: one bad minibatch that produces an enormous gradient cannot blow up the weights. Nearly every stable run has it.

    Shared versus separate actor–critic trunks. A scalar value head bolted onto the policy trunk saves a full model’s worth of memory, but the two heads now fight over shared features, and a large value loss can corrupt the representation the policy depends on. Separate networks are cleaner and more stable but cost more memory. At LLM scale the shared trunk with a carefully scaled value loss is common; when the critic misbehaves, decoupling is the first structural fix to try.

    Learning-rate ratio, Adam epsilon, reward scaling. The critic often wants a higher learning rate than the policy — it is doing plain regression and can afford to move faster, while the policy must inch. Adam’s $\epsilon$ (the denominator floor, not the clip $\epsilon$) matters more than people expect at this scale; too small and updates explode on flat gradients. And reward whitening or scaling — standardising the reward-model outputs before they enter GAE — keeps advantages in a sane range across prompts with wildly different raw scores.

    Masking, and the truncation bug. Two masking mistakes are endemic. The first: letting prompt tokens or padding contribute to the loss. Prompt tokens were not generated by the policy; training on them teaches the model to predict its own input. Mask them to zero in every term. The second is subtler and a genuine source of silent bugs: a sequence cut off by the maximum-length limit is not a terminated episode. A real EOS means the value beyond it is zero; a truncation means there was more to come, so the last value should bootstrap off $V_\phi$, not be treated as terminal. Conflate the two and you systematically mis-estimate returns for every long response.

    A truncated sequence is not a terminal state. This one line causes more quiet damage than any other. If your GAE loop sets the bootstrap term to zero at the max-length cutoff, you are telling the critic that every long answer ended in a state worth exactly nothing — punishing length for a reason that has nothing to do with quality. Track a real done flag (EOS reached) separately from a truncated flag (length cap), and only zero the bootstrap on done.

    The Hyperparameters That Matter

    PPO exposes dozens of knobs; most can be left at their defaults forever. These are the ones worth understanding, with a typical range and the symptom of setting each one wrong.

    KnobTypicalSymptom when wrong
    Clip $\epsilon$0.1 – 0.2Too large: updates too aggressive, unstable. Too small: learning crawls, clipfrac saturates.
    Inner epochs $K$1 – 4Too many: policy drifts far off the behaviour policy, KL spikes. Too few: wasted rollouts, slow.
    Minibatch sizehardware-boundToo small: noisy whitening and gradients. Too large: fewer steps per rollout.
    Discount $\gamma$$\approx 1.0$Below ~0.99 for LLMs: near-terminal reward gets discounted away over long responses.
    GAE $\lambda$$\approx 0.95$Toward 1: high-variance advantages. Toward 0: biased, over-trusts a shaky critic.
    KL coefficient $\beta$problem-specificToo low: policy runs away, reward hacking. Too high: policy frozen near the reference, no learning.
    Policy learning rate1e-6 – 1e-5Too high: entropy collapse, KL explosion. Too low: no visible improvement.
    Entropy coefficient $c_2$0 – 0.01Too high: injects noise, garbled text. Too low/zero: risk of premature collapse.

    Be honest about the ranking: of these, $K$ and $\beta$ are the two that most often decide a run. $K$ governs how far you let the policy stray from the data it was trained on before refreshing; $\beta$ governs how hard the reference leash pulls back. Get those two into a sensible range and the rest usually forgive you.

    The Diagnostic Dashboard

    You do not debug PPO by reading the loss — the loss is a sum of three terms fighting each other and tells you almost nothing. You debug it by watching a small dashboard of curves, each of which has a known healthy shape and a known pathology. The essentials: mean reward (should rise, then plateau — not spike), KL to the reference (should grow slowly and stay bounded), clipfrac (fraction of tokens clipped, healthy around $0.1$–$0.3$), approx_kl between old and new policy per update (should stay small, a few hundredths), explained variance of the critic (should climb toward $1$), entropy (should ease down gently, never crash), gradient norm (steady, no spikes), and response length (watch for runaway inflation).

    Diagnostics in Code

    Three of these are worth computing exactly, and all three fit in a few lines. Note the KL estimator: the naive $\log\pi_{\text{old}} - \log\pi_{\text{new}}$ is unbiased but high-variance and can go negative; Schulman’s $k_3$ estimator $(r-1)-\log r$ is always non-negative and far steadier.

    def diagnostics(old_logp, new_logp, values, returns, eps, mask):
    with torch.no_grad():
        logratio = new_logp - old_logp
        ratio    = torch.exp(logratio)
        approx_kl = masked_mean((ratio - 1) - logratio, mask)      # k3, non-negative
        clipfrac  = masked_mean((ratio - 1).abs().gt(eps).float(), mask)
        resid_var = (returns - values).var()
        explained_var = 1.0 - resid_var / (returns.var() + 1e-8)
    return approx_kl.item(), clipfrac.item(), explained_var.item()

    Put numbers through it. Suppose over five tokens the log-ratios are $[0.01, -0.02, 0.25, -0.30, 0.05]$, so the ratios are about $[1.01, 0.98, 1.28, 0.74, 1.05]$. With $\epsilon=0.2$, two of the five exceed the band ($1.28$ and $0.74$), so clipfrac $=2/5=0.40$ — high, a sign the step was too big. The $k_3$ estimator gives approx_kl $\approx 0.015$, borderline. Now explained variance: take returns $[0.75, 0.80, -0.65, -0.60, 0.20]$ and critic values $[0.50, 0.60, -0.40, -0.55, 0.10]$. The residuals have variance $0.033$ and the returns have variance $0.395$, so

    $$\text{explained\_var} = 1 - \frac{0.033}{0.395} \approx 0.92.$$

    A critic that explains $92\%$ of the variance in returns is healthy. If that number sits near zero — or worse, goes negative — the critic is predicting no better than a constant, and every advantage you feed the policy is noise.

    Named Failure Modes

    Most PPO failures are one of a small cast of characters. Learn to recognise them by their signature curve.

    SymptomCauseFix
    Entropy crashes toward zero; outputs become one repeated templateEntropy collapse — the policy over-committed to a locally high-reward formAdd or raise the entropy bonus, lower the LR, tighten $\epsilon$
    KL to reference explodes; text scores well but reads as gibberishKL explosion — the policy ran away from the referenceRaise $\beta$ (or use an adaptive controller), lower the LR, reduce $K$
    Explained variance stuck near zero all runThe critic never learns — usually value LR or advantage normalisationRaise value LR, check whitening, consider a separate critic trunk
    Reward-model score climbs while real quality fallsReward hacking (see Part 9) — the policy exploits the proxyStrengthen the KL leash, audit outputs by hand, improve the reward model
    Response length inflates without quality gainLength is correlated with reward in the model; the policy games itLength-normalise or penalise length; re-check truncation handling
    Loss becomes NaN after a few hundred stepsRatio overflow from unstable log-prob differencesClamp the log-ratio before exp, add grad-norm clipping, lower LR
    Everything looks calm but nothing improvesReward has near-zero variance across the batch, so advantages vanishIncrease reward signal/variance, check the reward model actually discriminates

    That last row is the quietest and the most demoralising. The curves are flat and serene, the run does not crash, and the model simply never gets better — because if every completion in a batch earns nearly the same reward, the advantages are all near zero and there is no gradient signal to speak of. This exact failure is what motivates the group-based methods of Part 12, which need reward variance within a group to function at all.

    Testing an Implementation

    You do not have to trust PPO code; you can test it. Four checks catch the overwhelming majority of bugs before you waste a GPU-week:

    # 1) The ratio must be exactly 1 on the first inner epoch.
assert torch.allclose(ratio_epoch0, torch.ones_like(ratio_epoch0), atol=1e-5)

# 2) Whitened advantages are centred (mean approx 0) and scaled (std approx 1).
adv_w = whiten(adv, mask)
assert masked_mean(adv_w, mask).abs().item() < 1e-4

# 3) GAE with lambda=1 must equal the Monte-Carlo advantage on a tiny trajectory.
adv_gae, _ = gae(r, v, mask, gamma=1.0, lam=1.0)
mc = discounted_return(r, gamma=1.0) - v[:-1]
assert torch.allclose(adv_gae, mc, atol=1e-5)

# 4) Smoke test: overfit a single prompt; reward must reach the ceiling.
for _ in range(200):
    ppo_step(single_prompt)
assert reward(single_prompt) > 0.95 * max_reward

    Test 1 is the cheapest and catches an astonishing number of bugs — a wrong ratio on epoch zero means your old_logprob was recorded incorrectly or detached at the wrong moment. Test 3 validates the single most error-prone loop in the codebase against a brute-force computation you can check by hand. Test 4 is the RL equivalent of overfitting one batch in supervised learning: if PPO cannot memorise a single prompt, no hyperparameter will save it on a real dataset.

    With the details nailed down and the dashboard watched, PPO is a reliable tool. But it is a heavy one — four models, a critic to babysit, a reward model to keep from being hacked. That weight is exactly what the next generation of methods set out to shed. On to Part 12.
