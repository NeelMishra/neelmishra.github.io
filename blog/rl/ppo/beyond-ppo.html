    GRPO, DPO & Where PPO Stands Today

    July 2026 · 22 min read

    Eleven posts built PPO up. This one takes it apart. Every method that has challenged PPO for language models can be read as the same move: pick one component of PPO, delete it, and pay a specific price for the simplicity you gain. GRPO deletes the critic. DPO deletes the whole reinforcement-learning loop. RLOO deletes the clip and the critic. Verifiable-reward training deletes the reward model. Seen this way the zoo of acronyms stops being intimidating — each is PPO minus one box, and the interesting question is always what breaks when that box is gone.

    Where We Are: A Full, Heavy Algorithm

    We ended Part 11 with a working but demanding algorithm: four models resident (Part 10’s policy, critic, reward model, reference), a critic that needs babysitting, and a reward model that can be gamed. It is the most general RLHF tool we have, and also the most expensive. The successors below are best understood not as replacements but as trades — each gives up some of PPO’s generality for a smaller memory bill, a simpler loop, or a harder-to-hack reward. Keep the four-model diagram in mind; it is the thing they are all cutting into.

    GRPO: Delete the Critic

    The critic exists to answer one question: how good is this state, so we can measure how much better than average an action was? GRPO — Group Relative Policy Optimization, from the DeepSeekMath work — answers that question without a learned value function at all. Instead of training a network to estimate the baseline, it samples a group of $G$ completions for the same prompt and uses the group’s own reward statistics as the baseline. For completion $i$ with reward $r_i$:

    $$\hat{A}_i = \frac{r_i - \text{mean}(\mathbf{r})}{\text{std}(\mathbf{r})}, \qquad \mathbf{r}=(r_1,\dots,r_G).$$

    That single scalar $\hat{A}_i$ is assigned to every token in completion $i$. There is no per-token credit assignment and no bootstrapping — the whole GAE apparatus of Part 6 is gone, replaced by “did this completion beat its siblings?”

    def grpo_advantages(rewards):        # rewards: shape [G], one prompt's group
    mean = rewards.mean()
    std  = rewards.std() + 1e-8
    adv  = (rewards - mean) / std    # one scalar per completion
    return adv                        # broadcast to every token in completion i

    Worked example. Sample $G=4$ completions and score them $\mathbf{r}=(0.9,\,0.2,\,0.7,\,0.4)$. The mean is $0.55$; the deviations are $(0.35,-0.35,0.15,-0.15)$, whose squares average to $0.0725$, so the standard deviation is about $0.269$. Dividing through:

    Completion$r_i$$r_i-\text{mean}$$\hat{A}_i$Effect on its tokens
    10.90+0.35+1.30made more likely
    20.20-0.35-1.30made less likely
    30.70+0.15+0.56mildly reinforced
    40.40-0.15-0.56mildly suppressed

    What this buys: no critic means roughly a quarter less memory, no critic-training instability, and the “dead critic” failure mode of Part 11 simply cannot happen. What it costs: you now generate $G$ completions per prompt instead of one, so generation — already the bottleneck — gets several times more expensive. And crucially it needs reward variance within the group.

    GRPO dies when the group agrees. If all $G$ completions are correct, or all wrong, the reward std is zero and every advantage is $0/0$ — no signal at all. On an easy prompt the model already solves every time, or an impossible one it never solves, GRPO learns nothing from that prompt. This is the “flat reward, no learning” failure of Part 11 sharpened to a single prompt, and it is why GRPO pipelines curate prompts of intermediate difficulty. Note what GRPO keeps, though: the clipped ratio of Part 8 and usually the KL term of Part 9. Most of this series still applies to it unchanged — which is why GRPO is behind much recent reasoning-model training.

    DPO: Delete Reinforcement Learning

    DPO — Direct Preference Optimization — makes the boldest cut: it removes the RL loop entirely. Its starting point is a fact we could have derived back in Part 9: the policy that maximises expected reward under a KL leash to the reference has a closed form,

    $$\pi^\star(y\mid x) \;\propto\; \pi_{\text{ref}}(y\mid x)\,\exp\!\Big(\tfrac{1}{\beta}\,r(x,y)\Big).$$

    That relation can be inverted to express the reward in terms of the optimal policy and the reference. Substitute it into the standard preference model — the probability that a human prefers response $y_w$ over $y_l$ — and the reward model cancels out entirely. What remains is a plain classification-style loss over preference pairs:

    $$L_{\text{DPO}} = -\log\sigma\!\Big(\beta\log\frac{\pi_\theta(y_w\mid x)}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y_w\mid x)} - \beta\log\frac{\pi_\theta(y_l\mid x)}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y_l\mid x)}\Big).$$
    def dpo_loss(logp_w, logp_l, ref_w, ref_l, beta):
    # logp_* : policy log-prob of the winning / losing response
    # ref_*  : frozen reference log-prob of the same responses
    margin = beta * ((logp_w - ref_w) - (logp_l - ref_l))
    return -F.logsigmoid(margin).mean()          # no reward model, no sampling

    No reward model, no sampling, no critic — two models instead of four, and an ordinary supervised training loop. The honest trade-off is that DPO is offline. It learns only from a fixed dataset of preference pairs; it never generates its own responses, so it cannot discover and reinforce a good answer the model stumbles into on its own. It is also sensitive to the gap between the preference data’s distribution and the policy’s: as the policy moves away from the data it was trained on, the pairs stop being informative. PPO’s on-policy sampling is exactly what DPO gives up, and for problems where the model must improve on its own outputs that loss is real.

    RLOO: Delete the Clip and the Critic

    RLOO — REINFORCE Leave-One-Out, revived by Ahmadian et al. — makes a narrower argument: PPO’s clipping and its learned critic were engineered for long-horizon control problems with stochastic transitions and per-step rewards. LLM alignment has none of that. Transitions are deterministic (append a token), there is usually a single terminal reward, and the “episode” is one response. For that structure, RLOO says, plain REINFORCE with a good baseline is enough — drop the clip, drop the critic, and build the baseline from multiple samples. For $k$ samples of a prompt, the baseline for sample $i$ is the mean reward of the other $k-1$:

    def rloo_baseline(rewards):          # rewards: shape [k], k samples of one prompt
    k = rewards.shape[0]
    total = rewards.sum()
    baseline = (total - rewards) / (k - 1)   # leave-one-out mean, unbiased
    return rewards - baseline                 # advantage per sample

    Leave-one-out keeps the baseline unbiased (the sample never appears in its own baseline), which is the neat trick. Like GRPO it uses multiple samples and no critic; unlike GRPO it drops the clipped surrogate too, betting that with a low-variance baseline the trust-region machinery is unnecessary overhead for the single-reward case.

    RLVR: Delete the Reward Model

    The last cut targets the component most responsible for PPO’s worst behaviour. RLVR — reinforcement learning from verifiable rewards — replaces the learned reward model with a checker: run the generated code against unit tests, or compare the final answer of a maths solution to the known ground truth. The reward is $1$ if it passes and $0$ otherwise, computed by a program, not a network.

    This is arguably the biggest practical shift for reasoning models, for one reason: a checker cannot be hacked the way a reward model can. There is no learned proxy to exploit, so the classic reward-hacking failure of Part 9 — high score, low quality — largely disappears; a wrong answer that merely looks right still fails the check. The price is generality: RLVR only works where a cheap, reliable verifier exists. Maths and code qualify; “write a more empathetic reply” does not. RLVR pairs naturally with GRPO or RLOO, since a binary verifiable reward slots straight into a group baseline.

    Choosing a Method

    Which cut is right depends entirely on what you have and what you need. The short decision guide:

    Your situationReach forWhy
    Good reward model, need on-policy improvement, memory to sparePPOMost general; per-token credit assignment; the best-understood option.
    Have a reward (model or checker), want less memory, can afford more samplingGRPODrops the critic; group baseline; still on-policy and clipped.
    Only a fixed set of preference pairs; tight memory budgetDPONo reward model, no sampling; simple offline training.
    Verifiable answers (maths, code)RLVR (with GRPO/RLOO)Checker replaces the reward model; no classic reward hacking.
    Single terminal reward, want minimalism over PPORLOODrops clip and critic; leave-one-out baseline; light machinery.

    An Honest Verdict on PPO

    PPO is no longer the automatic default it was in 2022, and pretending otherwise does a disservice. If you have only preference pairs, DPO is simpler and cheaper. If you have verifiable answers, RLVR removes a whole failure class. If memory is the binding constraint, GRPO buys back a quarter of it. Its four-model footprint is a genuine operational cost, not a detail.

    And yet: PPO is still the most general and best-understood method in the set. It is still what you want when you have a good reward model and genuinely need on-policy improvement — when the model has to get better at things it can already sometimes do, by sampling, scoring, and reinforcing its own successes. Its per-token advantages give a finer credit signal than any group-level scalar. In practice it is the thing to reach for when the simpler methods plateau: teams routinely start with DPO or GRPO and move to PPO when they hit a ceiling those methods cannot pass. The successors did not kill PPO; they gave it competition, and narrowed it from “always” to “when you need its generality.”

    The Objective, One Last Time

    Twelve posts, one expression. It is worth seeing the whole construction collapse back into a single line, each term now something you derived rather than met:

    $$L(\theta,\phi)=\underbrace{-\hat{\mathbb{E}}_t\big[\min\big(r_t(\theta)\hat{A}_t,\ \text{clip}(r_t(\theta),1-\epsilon,1+\epsilon)\hat{A}_t\big)\big]}_{\text{policy}} + c_1\,\hat{\mathbb{E}}_t\big[(V_\phi(s_t)-G_t)^2\big] - c_2\,\hat{\mathbb{E}}_t\big[\mathcal{H}[\pi_\theta]\big]$$
    • The $-\log\pi_\theta\,G_t$ of REINFORCE (Part 3) gained a baseline (Part 4) to become $-\log\pi_\theta\,\hat{A}_t$.
    • The advantage $\hat{A}_t$ (Part 5) became the bias/variance-tuned GAE estimate (Part 6).
    • The log became a ratio $r_t(\theta)$ to reuse off-policy rollouts (Part 7), then got clipped for safety (Part 8).
    • A KL leash to the reference held reward hacking at bay (Part 9), and the value and entropy terms completed the loss (Part 10).

    Every symbol earned its place by fixing a concrete failure of the one before it. That is the whole of PPO — and now the map for reading everything built on top of it. Back to the series overview to see the full arc, or to revisit any single idea.
