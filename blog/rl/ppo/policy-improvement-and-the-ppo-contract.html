    PPO Begins with a Contract: Collect, Estimate, Improve

    September 2026 · 5 min read + experiments

    Build a concrete mental model of the actor, critic, rollout buffer, and two clocks before opening the PPO loss.

    See the ideaRead the explanationTry the calculation

    The problem PPO is trying to solve

    A policy is a rule for sampling an action from the current state. In a navigation task, it assigns probabilities to left and right. In a language model, it assigns probabilities to next tokens. We want to change those probabilities so that complete interactions earn more reward. The difficulty is that reward may arrive long after the action that helped produce it.

    Consider a six-position corridor. You start at position 0; reaching position 5 gives reward +1 and ends the episode. Every other transition gives −0.01. Left at position 0 leaves you there. A policy that always moves right reaches the goal in five steps and earns $1-4(0.01)=0.96$ undiscounted reward. An initially random policy often wanders. This small task will follow us into the executable lab.

    PPO learns from sampled attempts. It estimates whether an action was better or worse than expected, then uses that estimate to adjust the probability of the action. Since collecting attempts costs something, it makes several optimizer passes over one collection. Its clipped objective moderates the incentive to keep pushing an already-changed probability in the same beneficial direction. That last sentence is the part to remember: clipping is not a hard limit on the resulting policy change. The original paper introduces both clipped and KL-penalty variants; this series implements PPO-Clip. Schulman et al., PPO.

    We use $R$ for reward and $ ho$ for a likelihood ratio so the two never get confused. For an episode ending at time $T$, define $G_t=sum_{k=t}^{T-1}gamma^{k-t}R_k$ and $J( heta)=mathbb E[G_0]$. The expectation includes action sampling, initial states, and any environment randomness. A policy distribution matters even when the environment transitions are deterministic.

    First remove the trajectory

    In a one-step bandit, left pays 1 and right pays 3. If $p$ is the probability of right, then $J=1+2p$. If the policy parameter is a logit $z$ with $p=\operatorname{sigmoid}(z)$, then $dJ/dz=2p(1-p)$. Both actions pay a positive reward, but right deserves more probability. The next chapter derives how sampled actions can recover this gradient without differentiating the reward function.

    What stays fixed, and for how long?

    The actor samples actions and provides their log probabilities. The critic predicts return. Their neural networks may share features, but they have different outputs and losses. You do not need a reward model or reference language model for general PPO; the corridor supplies rewards directly.

    During collection, hold the actor fixed. Save each state, action, reward, old action log probability, old value prediction, and episode-boundary information. At the end of a rollout, compute advantages and value targets once. During the subsequent optimizer epochs, only current actor and critic predictions change. If you recompute the denominator using the current actor at every step, the ratio becomes one and you erase the comparison that PPO needs.

    A separate copy of the old actor is not always necessary. For the sampled actions, stored log probabilities supply the denominator. Similarly, stored old values let you build fixed targets. An additional model copy becomes useful if you need old-policy predictions at actions or states that were not recorded.

    Why the buffer eventually expires

    Changing a policy changes which states it reaches. If a policy learns to go right, it visits positions near the goal more often. Reweighting an old left/right choice does not manufacture the new states that this policy would have visited later. PPO therefore uses a short-lived buffer and regularly collects fresh data. “Several epochs” means several local passes over a recent rollout, not an unlimited replay archive.

    The probability ratio and clipping rule address this local optimization problem. The critic and GAE address the separate problem of noisy credit assignment. Keeping those purposes distinct makes it easier to debug: a broken bootstrap target is not fixed by narrowing the clip range, and a huge actor step is not fixed by making the critic deeper.

    A useful reading order

    Derive the sampled actor signal in policy gradients and baselines, turn trajectories into that signal with GAE, and only then study clipping. The later chapters put these pieces into code, extend the action distribution, and explain diagnostic plots. Language-model PPO is the final application, so general ideas get defined once.

    Check your understanding: Can PPO change the policy even when one sample’s clipped term is flat?

    Yes. Other samples, entropy regularization, and a shared critic trunk can still change the same parameters. A flat contribution from one sample is not a constraint on that action’s probability.
