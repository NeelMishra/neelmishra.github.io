    Policy Gradients from First Principles

    July 2026 · 22 min read

    We have a policy $\pi_\theta$, a reward, and the return $G_t$. What we do not have is a way to improve the policy. This post derives that way — the policy gradient — from the ground up, slowly, because it is the single derivation readers most want to see done without hand-waving. By the end you will know exactly why the training loss is a log-probability scaled by a number, why that number should be the reward-to-go, and why the most obvious choice of number makes the whole thing almost too noisy to use.

    Where we are. Part 1 set up the objective $J(\theta) = \mathbb{E}_{\tau \sim \pi_\theta}[R(\tau)]$; Part 2 defined the reward and the return $G_t$. Now we take $\nabla_\theta J$. The obstacle is that the thing we average over depends on $\theta$, so we cannot differentiate naively. The log-derivative trick clears it, and out falls the first term of the running objective: $L(\theta) = -\log\pi_\theta(a_t\mid s_t)\,G_t$.

    The Objective and Why It Resists Differentiation

    The goal is to push up the expected trajectory reward. Because trajectories are sequences of discrete tokens, the expectation is a sum over all possible trajectories, each weighted by how likely the policy is to produce it:

    $$ J(\theta) = \mathbb{E}_{\tau \sim \pi_\theta}\big[R(\tau)\big] = \sum_{\tau} p_\theta(\tau)\, R(\tau). $$

    We want $\nabla_\theta J$. Push the gradient inside the sum. The reward $R(\tau)$ is a property of a realised trajectory — the verifier's score for a specific string — and does not depend on $\theta$, so only the probability carries a gradient:

    $$ \nabla_\theta J = \sum_{\tau} \big(\nabla_\theta\, p_\theta(\tau)\big)\, R(\tau). $$

    Here is the wall. This is a sum over trajectories weighted by $\nabla_\theta p_\theta(\tau)$, not by $p_\theta(\tau)$. It is therefore not an expectation, and we have no way to estimate it by sampling rollouts — sampling gives us trajectories drawn according to $p_\theta$, but this quantity weights them by a gradient we cannot observe. We need to turn it back into an average over trajectories we can actually draw.

    The Log-Derivative Trick

    The device that rescues us is one line of calculus, the log-derivative (or score-function) identity. For any positive, differentiable $p_\theta$, the chain rule gives $\nabla_\theta \log p_\theta = \tfrac{1}{p_\theta}\nabla_\theta p_\theta$, which rearranges to

    $$ \nabla_\theta\, p_\theta(\tau) = p_\theta(\tau)\, \nabla_\theta \log p_\theta(\tau). $$

    Substitute it into the stuck expression. The lone $\nabla_\theta p_\theta$ becomes $p_\theta$ times a log-gradient, and the $p_\theta$ factor is exactly the weight that makes a sum over trajectories an expectation again:

    $$ \begin{aligned} \nabla_\theta J &= \sum_{\tau} p_\theta(\tau)\, \nabla_\theta \log p_\theta(\tau)\, R(\tau) \\[3pt] &= \mathbb{E}_{\tau \sim \pi_\theta}\big[\, R(\tau)\, \nabla_\theta \log p_\theta(\tau)\,\big]. \end{aligned} $$

    This is progress of the best kind: the gradient of the objective is now an expectation over trajectories drawn from the current policy, so we can estimate it by rolling out, computing $R(\tau)\,\nabla_\theta \log p_\theta(\tau)$ on each sample, and averaging. Note what the trick did not do: it never touched $R(\tau)$. The reward can be non-differentiable, discrete, a black-box verifier — it does not matter, because we only ever differentiate the log-probability, never the reward.

    Where the Environment Cancels

    The remaining piece, $\nabla_\theta \log p_\theta(\tau)$, still mentions the whole trajectory. Expand the trajectory probability into its factors — the initial state, then at each step the policy's choice and the environment's transition:

    $$ p_\theta(\tau) = \rho(s_0) \prod_{t=0}^{T-1} \pi_\theta(a_t \mid s_t)\, P(s_{t+1} \mid s_t, a_t). $$

    Taking logs turns the product into a sum, which is exactly why we took the log in the first place:

    $$ \log p_\theta(\tau) = \log \rho(s_0) + \sum_{t=0}^{T-1} \Big[\log \pi_\theta(a_t \mid s_t) + \log P(s_{t+1} \mid s_t, a_t)\Big]. $$

    Now differentiate with respect to $\theta$ and watch two-thirds of the terms disappear. The initial-state distribution $\rho(s_0)$ — the prompt — does not depend on the policy parameters. Neither do the transitions $P(s_{t+1}\mid s_t, a_t)$: the environment's dynamics are fixed, whatever $\theta$ is. Their gradients are zero, and all that survives is the policy term:

    $$ \nabla_\theta \log p_\theta(\tau) = \sum_{t=0}^{T-1} \nabla_\theta \log \pi_\theta(a_t \mid s_t). $$

    For language models this cancellation is not just true in expectation, it is exact and obvious. Recall from Part 1 that transitions are deterministic: $P(s_{t+1}\mid s_t, a_t)$ is $1$ when $s_{t+1} = s_t \Vert a_t$ and $0$ otherwise, regardless of $\theta$. A quantity that is identically $1$ has $\log 1 = 0$ and gradient $0$ — there is nothing to cancel because there was never a $\theta$ in it. Substituting back gives the policy gradient theorem in its episodic form:

    $$ \boxed{\; \nabla_\theta J = \mathbb{E}_{\tau \sim \pi_\theta}\Big[\, R(\tau) \sum_{t=0}^{T-1} \nabla_\theta \log \pi_\theta(a_t \mid s_t) \,\Big]. \;} $$

    The Loss: A Weighted Log-Probability, Not a Target

    Autograd frameworks descend a loss, and ascending $J$ means descending $-J$. Reading the theorem as “the gradient of a loss” gives the REINFORCE objective:

    $$ L(\theta) = -\sum_{t=0}^{T-1} \log \pi_\theta(a_t \mid s_t)\, w_t, \qquad w_t = R(\tau). $$

    Stare at this next to a supervised loss, because the difference is the entire conceptual leap of the post. Supervised fine-tuning has a target: a gold token $y_t^\star$, and its cross-entropy loss $-\log\pi_\theta(y_t^\star\mid s_t)$ drags the whole distribution toward the one-hot vector on that target. Policy gradient has no target. There is only the token the model happened to sample, and a scalar $w_t$ multiplying its log-probability. The scalar is the whole message:

    • Sign decides direction: $w_t \gt 0$ pushes the sampled token's probability up; $w_t \lt 0$ pushes it down.
    • Magnitude decides strength: a bigger $|w_t|$ means a harder shove.
    AspectSupervised (SFT)Policy gradient (REINFORCE)
    Target per positionA gold token $y_t^\star$None — only the sampled token
    What the loss doesPull distribution toward the one-hot targetScale the sampled token's log-prob by $w_t$
    Signal per tokenA full gradient toward a known answerOne number: a sign and a magnitude
    DataFixed (prompt, gold answer) pairsFresh rollouts from the current policy
    Ceiling / failureCannot exceed the demonstrationsUnbiased, but high variance
    The one-line takeaway. REINFORCE is literally the cross-entropy loss against the sampled token, multiplied by a per-token scalar. That is why it drops into any existing training stack with almost no plumbing: compute the same log-probabilities you already compute for SFT, then multiply by $w_t$ before summing. The novelty is not the machinery; it is that the “label” is a token you generated and the loss is weighted by how good the result turned out to be.

    Causality: Reward-to-Go

    The theorem multiplies every $\nabla_\theta \log \pi_\theta(a_t\mid s_t)$ by the whole-trajectory return $R(\tau)$ — including rewards collected before step $t$. That should feel wrong, and it is wasteful. An action taken at step $t$ cannot reach back in time and change a reward that was already handed out at some earlier step $t' \lt t$.

    Make it precise. For $t' \lt t$, the past reward $r_{t'}$ is fixed before the action $a_t$ is chosen, so it is statistically independent of that action given the state. The score function has zero mean, $\mathbb{E}_{a_t \sim \pi_\theta}[\nabla_\theta \log \pi_\theta(a_t\mid s_t)] = 0$, and therefore

    $$ \mathbb{E}\big[\, r_{t'}\, \nabla_\theta \log \pi_\theta(a_t\mid s_t)\,\big] = 0 \quad\text{for } t' \lt t. $$

    Those cross terms contribute nothing in expectation — dropping them leaves the estimator unbiased — but they are not zero on any individual sample, so they inject pure noise. Removing them therefore strictly reduces variance. Keeping only the rewards an action can actually influence replaces $R(\tau)$ with the reward-to-go, which is precisely the return $G_t$ from Part 2:

    $$ \nabla_\theta J = \mathbb{E}\Big[\, \sum_{t=0}^{T-1} G_t\, \nabla_\theta \log \pi_\theta(a_t\mid s_t) \,\Big], \qquad G_t = \sum_{k \ge 0} \gamma^{k} r_{t+k}. $$

    Why Raw Returns Are a Terrible Weight

    REINFORCE is now unbiased, but as an estimator it is dangerously noisy, and the reason is worth understanding concretely rather than as a slogan.

    The trouble is the sign structure of the weights. Suppose, as is completely normal, that every return in a batch is positive — rewards in $[0,1]$, say. Then every sampled token gets its probability pushed up. But a probability distribution must sum to one, so you cannot raise everything; raising all logits does almost nothing to reshape the distribution after renormalisation. What actually moves the policy is which tokens got pushed hardest, and with all-positive weights that is dominated by which tokens happened to be sampled — sampling noise — rather than which tokens were genuinely better. On top of that, the estimator's variance grows with the reward scale and with trajectory length, since it sums one noisy score-function term per step.

    A tiny example

    One prompt, three sampled responses, scoring $R = 8$, $9$, and $10$. Weight each response's log-probability by its raw score and all three are reinforced — the $10$ hardest, the $8$ least, but all upward. Yet is the response that scored $8$ actually good? Only the batch can say, and relative to its peers it is the worst of the three. The signal we genuinely want is each score measured against the average, $\bar R = 9$:

    ResponseRaw score $R$Raw weightCentered weight $R - \bar R$Effect
    A8$+8$ (up)$-1$pushed down
    B9$+9$ (up)$0$left alone
    C10$+10$ (up)$+1$pushed up

    The raw weights $(8, 9, 10)$ are almost all shared offset; only the deviations $(-1, 0, +1)$ carry information about which response was better. Subtracting the mean throws away the useless common component and keeps the useful relative one — and because the mean is a constant with respect to the action, subtracting it (like any baseline $b$) leaves the gradient unbiased, since $\mathbb{E}[b\,\nabla_\theta \log\pi_\theta] = 0$. That single idea — subtract a baseline — is the whole of Part 4, where the constant $\bar R$ becomes a learned, state-dependent baseline $V_\phi(s_t)$.

    Computing the Loss in Code

    The loss is exactly a masked, weighted cross-entropy. Per-token log-probabilities come from a log_softmax and a gather; the returns enter as a detached weight; and a response mask zeroes out the prompt tokens, which the policy never chose and must not be trained on.

    import torch
import torch.nn.functional as F

# logits:    (B, T, V)  policy logits; logits[b, t] is the distribution
#                       from which actions[b, t] was sampled (already aligned)
# actions:   (B, T)     the token id taken at each position
# returns:   (B, T)     per-token weight w_t = G_t  (a CONSTANT here)
# resp_mask: (B, T)     1.0 on response tokens, 0.0 on prompt tokens

logp_all = F.log_softmax(logits, dim=-1)                       # (B, T, V)
logp = logp_all.gather(-1, actions.unsqueeze(-1)).squeeze(-1)  # (B, T)

# returns come from the rollout: detach so no gradient flows through the weight
w = returns.detach()

# REINFORCE: -log pi(a_t | s_t) * w_t, averaged over response tokens only
per_token = -logp * w * resp_mask                              # (B, T)
loss = per_token.sum() / resp_mask.sum().clamp(min=1.0)        # scalar
loss.backward()

    Two details are load-bearing. The resp_mask must exclude prompt tokens: they were supplied, not sampled, so their log-probabilities carry no policy decision and including them would train the model to predict its own inputs. And returns must be detached — $G_t$ is a fixed number computed from the sampled rewards, a multiplier on the log-probability, never something to back-propagate through. Getting either wrong produces a loss that runs without error and silently learns the wrong thing.

    The update on a single position is easy to see in isolation, and it is exactly what the animation below does: the gradient of $\log\pi_\theta$ at a categorical is $\mathbf{e}_j - \mathbf{p}$, so a step nudges the sampled token $j$ up and everything else down, scaled by the weight.

    import numpy as np

def softmax(z):
    z = z - z.max()
    e = np.exp(z)
    return e / e.sum()

# One policy-gradient step on a single categorical over 6 tokens.
logits = np.array([0.2, 0.5, 0.1, -0.1, -0.3, -0.6])
p = softmax(logits)
j = 0            # the token that was actually sampled
w = 1.0          # positive weight pushes token j up; negative pushes it down
lr = 0.8

onehot = np.eye(len(logits))[j]
grad = -w * (onehot - p)        # gradient of the LOSS  -w * log pi_j
logits = logits - lr * grad     # descend the loss
p_new = softmax(logits)         # p[j] has risen; the others shrank to renormalise

    Watching an Update

    Six candidate next tokens after the prefix “3” in our running arithmetic example; the model sampled times. Each Step applies one policy-gradient update to that sampled token — first with a positive weight (its bar grows, the rest shrink to keep the distribution normalised), then, in the later steps, with a negative weight (its bar shrinks and mass flows back to the others). This is the whole mechanism of REINFORCE, one token at a time.

    Where This Leaves Us

    From the bare objective we derived the policy gradient, saw the environment cancel, and read off a loss that is a log-probability scaled by a weight. Causality trimmed the weight to the reward-to-go $G_t$. So the running objective, at the close of Module 1's derivation, stands at:

    $$ \boxed{\; L(\theta) = -\log \pi_\theta(a_t \mid s_t)\, G_t \;} \qquad \text{(REINFORCE: unbiased, high variance).} $$

    The last section showed the flaw that dooms this form in practice: weighting by the raw return conflates a large shared offset with the small relative signal that actually matters, and the variance is punishing. The fix is to subtract a baseline, turning the weight from “how much reward” into “how much more reward than expected”. Building that baseline — a learned critic $V_\phi(s_t)$ — and proving it costs no bias is the job of Part 4, which turns the weight into $\big(G_t - V_\phi(s_t)\big)$.

    • Williams, Simple Statistical Gradient-Following Algorithms for Connectionist RL paper the original REINFORCE; read it for the score-function estimator and the role of the baseline.
    • Sutton & Barto, Reinforcement Learning: An Introduction book Chapter 13 derives the policy gradient theorem and the baseline; work through 13.1–13.4.
    • Schulman et al., High-Dimensional Continuous Control Using GAE paper the opening sections restate the policy gradient and variance reduction cleanly; a bridge to Part 6.
    • by hand derive $\mathbb{E}_{a\sim\pi_\theta}[\nabla_\theta \log\pi_\theta(a\mid s)] = 0$ from $\sum_a \pi_\theta(a\mid s) = 1$, and use it to prove that subtracting any state-dependent baseline leaves the gradient unbiased.
    • implement the single-categorical update above, sample a token, apply ten positive then ten negative steps, and plot the sampled token's probability to see it rise and fall.
