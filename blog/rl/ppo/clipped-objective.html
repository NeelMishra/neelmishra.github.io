    The Clipped Surrogate Objective

    July 2026 · 24 min read

    This is the post the whole series has been building toward. Everything so far — returns, the critic, the advantage, GAE, the importance ratio — was apparatus for estimating how good each token was and reusing an expensive batch. The previous post left us with a surrogate objective, $L(\theta)=\hat{\mathbb{E}}_t[r_t(\theta)\hat{A}_t]$, and a warning stapled to it: the moment the policy drifts, the importance weights explode. PPO is the two-symbol fix for that warning. One clip, one min.

    The Problem We Inherited

    The surrogate has a failure mode that is worse than mere variance. Maximise $r_t(\theta)\hat{A}_t$ with no constraint and a plain optimiser will do the obvious thing: for any token with positive advantage it will push $r_t(\theta)$ as high as it can, and for negative advantage as low as it can, because the objective is linear in the ratio and linear functions have no interior maximum. A single aggressive update can move $\pi_\theta$ arbitrarily far from $\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}$.

    That is self-defeating for two independent reasons. First, the importance weights are only trustworthy near $r_t(\theta)=1$; far from it the estimate is dominated by its rarest samples. Second — and this is the subtler one — the advantages $\hat{A}_t$ were computed under $\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}$. They are estimates of how much better than average each token was for the old policy. Move far enough and “better than average” is measured against a distribution you no longer have, so the very quantity you are ascending becomes stale. The step invalidates both the weights and the signal that justified the step. We need to stay in a neighbourhood where both remain approximately valid.

    Trust Regions: What TRPO Did

    The principled answer predates PPO. Trust Region Policy Optimization (Schulman et al., 2015) makes the neighbourhood explicit. Maximise the surrogate, but subject to a hard cap on how far the new policy is allowed to move from the old one, measured by the average KL divergence between their token distributions:

    $$\max_{\theta}\; \hat{\mathbb{E}}_t\!\big[r_t(\theta)\,\hat{A}_t\big] \qquad\text{subject to}\qquad \hat{\mathbb{E}}_t\!\big[\mathrm{D}_{\mathrm{KL}}\!\big(\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}(\cdot\mid s_t)\,\Vert\,\pi_\theta(\cdot\mid s_t)\big)\big]\le\delta.$$

    This is exactly right in spirit and genuinely works: the constraint is the “trust region” — the ball around $\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}$ within which the surrogate is a trustworthy model of the true objective. The problem is that it is a constrained optimisation. TRPO solves it by linearising the objective and taking a quadratic approximation of the KL constraint, which turns the step into a natural-gradient direction requiring the Fisher information matrix. That matrix is far too large to form for a real network, so TRPO computes matrix–vector products with it via conjugate gradient, then does a line search to satisfy the constraint. It is a lot of moving parts.

    At the scale and shape of language models, the cost compounds. The conjugate-gradient inner loop and the Fisher–vector products are expensive per step. Parameter sharing between the policy and value heads muddies the Fisher estimate. Stochastic regularisers like dropout, and the general non-smoothness of large-batch language training, break the second-order assumptions the derivation leans on. None of this is fatal, but all of it is friction, and it is friction in the hottest part of the loop. PPO's entire pitch is to keep the trust-region intuition while throwing away the second-order machinery — to get most of the benefit from a first-order objective you can write in three lines and drop into any autodiff optimiser.

    Designing the Clip

    So set aside the constraint and ask the design question directly: what objective, differentiated by plain SGD, has no incentive to push $r_t(\theta)$ far outside a band around $1$? Take the good-action case, $\hat{A}_t\gt 0$, where the surrogate wants $r_t(\theta)$ large. The incentive is the slope of the objective in $r_t(\theta)$. To kill the incentive past $1+\epsilon$, make the objective flat there. The obvious instrument is to clip the ratio:

    $$\text{clip}\big(r_t(\theta),\,1-\epsilon,\,1+\epsilon\big)\,\hat{A}_t.$$

    Clip the ratio into $[1-\epsilon,1+\epsilon]$ before multiplying by the advantage, and once $r_t(\theta)$ leaves the band the term stops changing, so its gradient is zero. For a good action that is exactly what we want above $1+\epsilon$. But clipping alone is not enough, and seeing why is the crux of PPO. Consider a good action whose ratio is already below the band, at $r_t(\theta)\lt 1-\epsilon$ — a token a previous inner epoch over-suppressed. The clip pins the term at $(1-\epsilon)\hat{A}_t$, a constant, so there is no gradient to pull it back up. Worse, in the bad-action case a pure clip can actually reward an unboundedly large step: if $\hat{A}_t\lt 0$ and the ratio has run above $1+\epsilon$, the clipped term $(1+\epsilon)\hat{A}_t$ is a fixed, relatively mild penalty, so the optimiser is free to let the ratio run even further with no additional cost. A pure clip removes the incentive on one side but leaves loopholes on the other.

    PPO closes the loopholes by taking the elementwise minimum of the clipped and unclipped terms:

    $$L^{\text{CLIP}}(\theta)\;=\;\hat{\mathbb{E}}_t\Big[\min\big(\,r_t(\theta)\,\hat{A}_t,\;\; \text{clip}(r_t(\theta),\,1-\epsilon,\,1+\epsilon)\,\hat{A}_t\,\big)\Big].$$

    Because $\min(a,b)\le a$ and $\min(a,b)\le b$ for any $a,b$, the clipped objective is a pessimistic lower bound on the unclipped surrogate, everywhere and termwise. That single word, pessimistic, is the design. Whenever clipping would make the objective look better than the honest unclipped value, the min ignores the clip and keeps the honest value; whenever clipping makes it look worse, the min keeps the pessimistic clipped value. You only ever get the benefit of the doubt in the direction that makes you cautious. This is the eighth milestone of the running objective — the loss is now $-\min\big(r_t(\theta)\hat{A}_t,\ \text{clip}(r_t(\theta),1-\epsilon,1+\epsilon)\hat{A}_t\big)$.

    The Four Cases (Really Six)

    The min plus the sign of the advantage produce a small, exhaustive set of behaviours. There are two signs of $\hat{A}_t$ and three regions for $r_t(\theta)$ — below the band, inside it, above it — giving six rows. Work each one by asking which of the two terms is smaller, because that is the one the min selects, and whether that term still depends on $\theta$, because that is whether a gradient flows.

    $\hat{A}_t$region of $r_t(\theta)$min keepsgradientinterpretation

    $\gt 0$$1-\epsilon\le r\le1+\epsilon$either (equal)flowsInside the trust region the two terms coincide; this is an ordinary policy-gradient step.
    $\gt 0$$r\gt 1+\epsilon$clipped $(1{+}\epsilon)\hat{A}$zeroA good token already made much more likely; the incentive to push it further is removed on this batch.
    $\lt 0$$r\lt 1-\epsilon$clipped $(1{-}\epsilon)\hat{A}$zeroA bad token already made much less likely; stop driving it down on the strength of one sample.
    $\lt 0$$1-\epsilon\le r\le1+\epsilon$either (equal)flowsInside the trust region; an ordinary policy-gradient step lowering the token's probability.

    Read the “gradient” column and the asymmetry jumps out. The flat, zero-gradient region always sits on the rewarding side of the objective — the side where pushing the ratio further would raise $L^{\text{CLIP}}$. For a good action that rewarding side is $r\gt 1+\epsilon$ (making a good token yet more likely); for a bad action it is $r\lt 1-\epsilon$ (making a bad token yet less likely). On the costly side, the min always retains the unclipped term, so the penalty is never capped. Rows one and six are the ones people miss: PPO keeps pushing when the ratio is on the wrong side of the band, and only goes flat once you are already winning. It caps your greed, never your caution.

    The last row is the pessimism made arithmetic. A token with a negative advantage has drifted to $r=1.6$, well above the band. The clipped term would score it a gentle $-0.96$; the unclipped term scores it a harsh $-1.28$. The min takes the harsher $-1.28$ and lets the gradient flow, so a bad token that became more likely is dragged back down at full strength. Compare row three: a good token at the same $r=1.6$ is capped at $+0.96$ with zero gradient. Same ratio, opposite advantage, opposite treatment — that is the asymmetry.

    What Clipping Does Not Do

    Here is the point most summaries get wrong. Clipping does not bound how far $\theta$ can move. It bounds the gradient, not the parameters. In the flat regions the gradient contribution from those tokens is zero, so they stop encouraging further movement — but other tokens in the same minibatch still have live gradients, and nothing in $L^{\text{CLIP}}$ forbids the optimiser from taking a large step overall. Run many inner epochs, or pair the clip with a generous learning rate, and the policy can still wander a long way from $\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}$ over the course of the update. The clip removes the local incentive to leave the trust region; it does not build a wall.

    Clipping is a soft, per-token brake, not a hard constraint. TRPO enforced an actual KL ball; PPO only flattens the objective near its edges. In practice this is why robust implementations still measure the KL between $\pi_\theta$ and $\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}$ each epoch and early-stop the inner loop when it exceeds a threshold. The clip does most of the work; the KL check catches the cases where it was not enough. The next post adds a second, different KL — against the frozen reference model — for an entirely separate reason.

    Choosing ε, and the clipfrac Diagnostic

    The clip width $\epsilon$ is the one hyperparameter this objective introduces, and it is remarkably forgiving. Values in $0.1$–$0.2$ are standard; $0.2$ is the paper's default and a fine starting point. Smaller $\epsilon$ makes a tighter trust region — more conservative, slower, more stable — while larger $\epsilon$ lets each update move further and risks the very drift the clip exists to contain. Its effect is entangled with two other knobs: the number of inner epochs and the minibatch size. More inner epochs over the same batch means more opportunities to drift, so a tighter $\epsilon$ or an earlier KL stop pairs naturally with aggressive reuse; smaller minibatches mean noisier ratios, which argues for a little more caution.

    The diagnostic that tells you whether $\epsilon$ is doing anything is the clip fraction: the share of tokens whose ratio left the band and were therefore clipped on this update. A clipfrac that is a small but non-trivial slice — very roughly the low tens of percent at most — means the clip is engaging on the tail without gagging the whole batch. A clipfrac near zero means the updates are so timid the clip never binds and you could likely afford a larger step; a clipfrac that climbs toward a large fraction of tokens means the policy is trying to leap out of the trust region on nearly every token, and something upstream — learning rate, epoch count, advantage scale — is too hot. Watch it next to the measured KL; together they are the two-number dashboard for whether the trust region is holding.

    The Clipped Loss in Code

    The whole objective is a handful of lines. Every tensor is shaped $(B,T)$ over the response positions, and old_logprobs and advantages arrive detached from the rollout and GAE stages.

    def ppo_clip_loss(new_logprobs, old_logprobs, advantages, mask, eps=0.2):
    # ratio r_t(theta), formed in log-space for numerical stability
    ratio = torch.exp(new_logprobs - old_logprobs)                 # (B, T)

    surr1 = ratio * advantages                                     # unclipped  r * A
    clipped = torch.clamp(ratio, 1.0 - eps, 1.0 + eps)             # ratio pinned to band
    surr2 = clipped * advantages                                   # clipped   clip(r) * A

    # take the WORSE of the two per token: the pessimistic lower bound.
    # negate because optimisers minimise and we want to MAXimise L_CLIP.
    per_token = -torch.min(surr1, surr2)                           # (B, T)
    loss = (per_token * mask).sum() / mask.sum().clamp(min=1.0)    # masked mean

    # diagnostic only (no grad): fraction of real tokens that got clipped
    with torch.no_grad():
        left_band = (torch.abs(ratio - 1.0) > eps).float()
        clipfrac = (left_band * mask).sum() / mask.sum().clamp(min=1.0)

    return loss, clipfrac

    Three details earn their place. The min is elementwise and comes before the mean, so the pessimism is applied per token, not to the batch as a whole. The negation turns the maximisation of $L^{\text{CLIP}}$ into a minimisation for the optimiser. And the clipfrac is computed under no_grad because it is a monitoring number, not part of the objective — feeding it into the loss would be meaningless, since the clip's whole point is that its gradient is zero where it binds.

    That loss is called several times over the same frozen batch — the off-policy reuse from Part 7 — and because the clip bounds the gradient but not the step, a robust loop also measures the drift each epoch and bails out when it grows too large:

    def ppo_update(policy, batch, opt, eps=0.2, inner_epochs=4, target_kl=0.02):
    # batch is the FROZEN rollout: tokens, old_logprobs, advantages, mask
    for epoch in range(inner_epochs):
        new_logprobs = policy.logprobs(batch.tokens)          # (B, T), needs grad
        loss, clipfrac = ppo_clip_loss(
            new_logprobs, batch.old_logprobs, batch.advantages, batch.mask, eps)

        opt.zero_grad()
        loss.backward()
        torch.nn.utils.clip_grad_norm_(policy.parameters(), 1.0)
        opt.step()

        # clipping bounds the GRADIENT, not the step -- so watch the actual drift.
        # approx KL between old and new policy over this batch (samples from old):
        with torch.no_grad():
            approx_kl = ((batch.old_logprobs - new_logprobs) * batch.mask).sum() \
                        / batch.mask.sum().clamp(min=1.0)
        if approx_kl.abs() > 1.5 * target_kl:
            break            # this batch has drifted far enough; stop reusing it
    return loss, clipfrac

    The early-stop is the pragmatic admission that the clip is a soft brake: most of the time all four inner epochs run, but when a batch turns out to move the policy unusually fast, the loop stops reusing it rather than trusting the clip to hold the line by itself.

    Where This Leaves Us

    The objective is now recognisably PPO. Written as a loss to minimise, per token:

    $$-\,L^{\text{CLIP}}(\theta)\;=\;-\min\!\big(r_t(\theta)\,\hat{A}_t,\;\; \text{clip}(r_t(\theta),\,1-\epsilon,\,1+\epsilon)\,\hat{A}_t\big),\qquad r_t(\theta)=\frac{\pi_\theta(a_t\mid s_t)}{\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}(a_t\mid s_t)}.$$

    It reuses expensive rollouts safely, resists the importance-weight explosion, and needs nothing more than first-order autodiff. But look at what it still does not control. The clip constrains movement relative to $\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}$ — last iteration's policy — and says nothing about drift away from the original model you started with; run enough iterations of small, legal steps and you can walk arbitrarily far from the checkpoint that made the model coherent in the first place. And nothing here stops the policy from exploiting a flawed reward model, quietly maximising a proxy that stops correlating with quality. Both gaps are the subject of the next post, KL Penalties, Reference Models & Reward Hacking.
