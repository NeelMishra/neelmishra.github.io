    Advantage: How Much Better Than Average?

    July 2026 · 19 min read

    Part 4 left a bracket sitting in the objective, $G_t - V_\phi(s_t)$, doing all the interesting work and lacking a name. This post gives it one, connects it to a second value function you have not met yet, and then confronts the real question: that bracket is only one way to estimate the quantity we actually care about, and it is the noisiest way. There is a second route that is far calmer but no longer tells the truth. Choosing between them is the whole content of Part 6, so we spend this post making the choice sharp.

    Naming the Bracket

    Recall the state-value function $V^{\pi}(s)$, the return you expect from state $s$ under the current policy. Alongside it lives the action-value function, which fixes not just the state but the first action before letting the policy take over:

    $$Q^{\pi}(s, a) \;=\; \mathbb{E}\big[\,G_t \mid s_t = s,\; a_t = a\,\big].$$

    The difference between them is the advantage:

    $$A^{\pi}(s, a) \;=\; Q^{\pi}(s, a) - V^{\pi}(s).$$

    Read it slowly, because the reading is what makes it click. The value $V^{\pi}(s)$ is, by definition, the average of $Q^{\pi}(s, a)$ over the actions the policy would take: $V^{\pi}(s) = \mathbb{E}_{a \sim \pi}[Q^{\pi}(s, a)]$. So the advantage measures how much better this particular action was than the policy's own average action in that state. Not better than nothing, not better than optimal — better than what the model itself would typically have done from here. A positive advantage means "this token beat the field of tokens I would usually have picked"; a negative one means "I have picked better than this on average."

    One consequence falls straight out and is worth stating on its own, because it governs how a batch of updates behaves:

    $$\mathbb{E}_{a \sim \pi}\big[A^{\pi}(s, a)\big] \;=\; \mathbb{E}_{a \sim \pi}\big[Q^{\pi}(s, a)\big] - V^{\pi}(s) \;=\; V^{\pi}(s) - V^{\pi}(s) \;=\; 0.$$

    The advantage is zero-mean by construction. In a batch, the actions the policy already favours sit near zero, the genuinely-better-than-usual actions are positive, and the worse-than-usual ones are negative, and the whole thing balances. The update does not inflate probabilities across the board; it reshuffles mass from below-average actions to above-average ones, leaving the total untouched. That is exactly the "re-centred, signed weights" behaviour the baseline bought us in Part 4, now given a precise identity.

    Two Ways to Estimate It

    The definitions above are expectations, and we cannot evaluate expectations — we have samples. So $A^{\pi}(s,a)$ has to be estimated from the rollouts, and here the road genuinely forks. There are two honest ways to turn one trajectory into an advantage number, they disagree, and the disagreement is not a bug: it is the bias–variance trade-off showing up in the flesh. Everything in Part 6 is an interpolation between these two poles, so meet them properly.

    Route One: Monte Carlo

    The first route is the bracket we already have. Use the return that the episode actually produced and subtract the critic’s forecast:

    $$\hat{A}_t^{\text{MC}} \;=\; G_t - V_\phi(s_t), \qquad G_t = \sum_{k \ge 0} \gamma^k r_{t+k}.$$

    This uses only real rewards — nothing but the numbers the environment actually handed back — plus the critic as a baseline. If the critic is correct, this estimator is unbiased: on average over episodes it equals the true advantage, because $\mathbb{E}[G_t \mid s_t] = V^{\pi}(s_t)$ and $\mathbb{E}[G_t \mid s_t, a_t] = Q^{\pi}(s_t, a_t)$, so the difference recovers $A^{\pi}$ exactly. No approximation has been smuggled in past the baseline.

    The catch is that $G_t$ is a single sample from a distribution over all the ways the episode could have unfolded. Sampling had its say at every step after $t$ — every token drawn, every branch taken — and all of that randomness is packed into the one scalar $G_t$. The variance is large, and crucially it grows the further $t$ is from the end: the return at the second-to-last step gathers noise from one remaining action, while the return at the first step gathers noise from the entire rest of the generation. Early tokens, which are often the ones you most want to credit correctly, get the noisiest estimates. That is the flaw Monte Carlo cannot fix from the inside.

    Route Two: Bootstrapping

    The second route refuses to wait for the whole future. Instead of summing the real rewards to the end, take one real reward and then let the critic stand in for everything after it. Start from the definition $Q^{\pi}(s_t, a_t) = \mathbb{E}[r_t + \gamma V^{\pi}(s_{t+1})]$ — one step of reward, plus the discounted value of wherever you land — and substitute the learned critic for the unknown $V^{\pi}$:

    $$\hat{A}_t^{\text{TD}} \;=\; r_t + \gamma V_\phi(s_{t+1}) - V_\phi(s_t) \;\equiv\; \delta_t.$$

    That quantity has a name we will lean on for the entire next post: the temporal-difference residual $\delta_t$. The word for what we just did is bootstrapping — estimating a value using your own current estimate of a later value, pulling yourself up by your own bootstraps. The random tail of the trajectory, all those unsampled continuations, has been replaced by a single deterministic network output $V_\phi(s_{t+1})$.

    That replacement is a spectacular variance reduction: a whole random future collapses into one fixed number, so $\delta_t$ barely flickers between rollouts. But it is not free. You have injected the critic’s error into the estimate. If $V_\phi$ is wrong — and early in training it is very wrong — every $\delta_t$ inherits that wrongness as bias. Monte Carlo trusted only real rewards and paid in variance; bootstrapping trusts the critic and pays in bias. There is no third option that pays neither, which is precisely why Part 6 has to negotiate.

    The TD Residual, and What Its Sign Means

    Because the whole of GAE is built out of it, pin the residual down on its own:

    $$\boxed{\;\delta_t \;=\; r_t + \gamma\, V_\phi(s_{t+1}) - V_\phi(s_t)\;}$$

    Read $\delta_t$ as surprise. The term $r_t + \gamma V_\phi(s_{t+1})$ is a fresh, one-step-better estimate of where you stand: the reward you just received plus the value of where you actually ended up. The term $V_\phi(s_t)$ is where you thought you stood a moment ago. Their difference is the update to your belief — how much the last step changed your mind.

    • $\delta_t \gt 0$: things went better than expected. You collected more reward, or landed in a more promising state, than the critic had priced in. The action looks good.
    • $\delta_t \lt 0$: things went worse than expected. Even if you have not failed yet, this step moved you toward a state the critic values less than the one you came from.
    • $\delta_t \approx 0$: no news. The step unfolded exactly as the critic anticipated, so there is nothing to learn from it.

    This "surprise" reading is why $\delta_t$ is such a natural credit-assignment signal: it localises the moment a trajectory’s fortunes changed, rather than smearing the final outcome across every token the way raw $G_t$ does.

    The Same Trajectory, Both Estimators

    Reuse the episode from Part 4 so you can watch the estimates evolve across the series: five steps, terminal reward only, $\gamma = 1$, so $G_t = 1$ everywhere. The critic estimates are $V_\phi = (0.50, 0.70, 0.60, 0.80, 0.90)$, and at the terminal step $V_\phi(s_5) = 0$. The Monte Carlo advantage is $G_t - V_\phi(s_t)$; the TD residual is $\delta_t = r_t + \gamma V_\phi(s_{t+1}) - V_\phi(s_t)$.

    Look at step 1, where the two estimators openly disagree in sign. Monte Carlo says $+0.30$: the episode ended in success, so in hindsight this step was on a winning path. The TD residual says $-0.10$: locally, the critic valued $s_1$ at $0.70$ but the next state $s_2$ at only $0.60$, so the one-step move looked like a small step backward. Neither is lying. Monte Carlo has the benefit of hindsight and the burden of all the noise between step 1 and the end; TD has only the critic’s one-step opinion, which is calm but only as trustworthy as the critic. That single row is the entire bias–variance tension in miniature.

    Notice also the internal consistency: the residuals sum to the Monte Carlo advantage, $\sum_t \delta_t = 0.20 - 0.10 + 0.20 + 0.10 + 0.10 = 0.50 = G_0 - V_\phi(s_0)$. That telescoping is not a coincidence of these numbers; it is an identity, and Part 6 turns it into the engine of GAE. The Monte Carlo route needs the full return, computed backward from the terminal reward:

    import torch

gamma   = 1.0
rewards = torch.tensor([0., 0., 0., 0., 1.])
values  = torch.tensor([0.50, 0.70, 0.60, 0.80, 0.90])
T       = len(rewards)

# Monte Carlo advantage: real returns minus the critic baseline.
returns = torch.zeros(T)
running = 0.0
for t in reversed(range(T)):
    running    = rewards[t] + gamma * running
    returns[t] = running
adv_mc = returns - values                 # [0.50, 0.30, 0.40, 0.20, 0.10]

    The bootstrapped route never touches the future beyond one step; it reads off the critic at $s_{t+1}$ instead, taking that value to be $0$ past the terminal step:

    # One-step TD residual: bootstrap off the next value.
# next_value is 0 at the terminal step (nothing comes after the episode).
next_values = torch.cat([values[1:], torch.zeros(1)])
delta = rewards + gamma * next_values - values   # [0.20, -0.10, 0.20, 0.10, 0.10]

assert torch.isclose(delta.sum(), adv_mc[0])     # residuals telescope to the MC advantage

    Best whenEpisodes are short, or the critic is untrustworthy.The critic is decent and episodes are long.
    A common misread: "bootstrapping" is not resampling. In statistics the word means drawing samples with replacement to estimate a sampling distribution. In reinforcement learning it means the opposite of collecting more samples — it means refusing to sample the future and substituting your own current estimate for it instead. Same word, unrelated idea. Here it buys variance reduction at the price of trusting a critic that is itself still learning.

    The Objective So Far

    With the bracket named, the running objective reads in terms of the advantage estimate:

    $$L(\theta) \;=\; -\,\log \pi_\theta(a_t \mid s_t)\,\hat{A}_t.$$

    Every symbol here is settled except one: which $\hat{A}_t$ do we plug in? The unbiased-but-noisy Monte Carlo estimate, or the calm-but-biased one-step residual? Committing to either extreme is leaving value on the table — one is too jittery to train on stably, the other too dependent on a critic that has barely warmed up. Part 6 stops choosing and builds a dial that slides continuously between them, using the very telescoping identity we just noticed.
