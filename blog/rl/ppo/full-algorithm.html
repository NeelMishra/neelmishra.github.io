    The Complete PPO Training Loop

    July 2026 · 23 min read

    Nine posts of construction have produced one line of policy objective. Part 9 left it looking like this: a clipped surrogate that raises the probability of better-than-average tokens, held on a leash to a frozen reference by a KL penalty. That line tells the policy what to do. It says nothing about who computes the advantages, which networks are loaded, in what order the tensors are produced, or how the critic that supplies those advantages is itself trained. This post is the assembly step. We put every part in memory, run one iteration end to end, and finish with the entire objective — policy, value, and entropy — on a single line.

    Where We Are, And What Is Still Missing

    The surrogate from Part 8, shaped by the KL term from Part 9, improves the actor. But the advantage $\hat{A}_t$ inside it is not free. It is produced by the GAE recursion of Part 6, which needs a value estimate $V_\phi(s_t)$ at every token, which is produced by a critic that has to be trained alongside the policy. So the real objective has always had a second half we have been quietly ignoring: a regression loss that keeps the critic honest. Add a small entropy term to stop the policy collapsing too fast, and you have the full PPO loss. Everything below exists to compute those three terms correctly and in the right order.

    The Four Models in Memory

    The first shock when you move PPO from a diagram to a GPU is how many networks it holds at once. A supervised fine-tuning job keeps one model and its optimiser state. PPO keeps four networks, two of them with optimiser state:

    NetworkTrainable?Typical sizeShared / offloaded?
    Policy $\pi_\theta$Yes — the thing we are optimisingFull model (e.g. 7B)No. Needs gradients and optimiser state; the memory heavyweight.
    Critic $V_\phi$Yes — trained by regressionFull trunk + a scalar headOften shares the policy trunk with a separate value head, halving its cost.
    Reward model $r_\psi$No — inference onlyFull modelFrozen. Runs once per completion; can be served on a separate device.
    Reference $\pi_{\text{ref}}$No — inference onlyFull modelFrozen. Quantisable and offloadable; supplies only per-token log-probs.

    Even with the critic sharing a trunk, that is roughly a fourfold jump in resident parameters over plain fine-tuning, and it is the single loudest operational complaint about PPO. Every successor method in Part 12 is, in part, an attempt to delete one of these four boxes. Keep this table in mind — when GRPO drops the critic and DPO drops both the critic and the reward model, the memory column is exactly what they are buying back.

    The reference and the reward model do different jobs. A common early confusion is to merge them. The reward model $r_\psi$ scores quality — it emits one scalar for a whole completion. The reference $\pi_{\text{ref}}$ scores familiarity — it emits a per-token log-probability the KL penalty compares against. One says “is this a good answer,” the other says “is this still recognisably your old self.” You need both.

    One Iteration, Three Phases

    A single PPO iteration is not one pass over data. It is three distinct phases, and the reason the algorithm confuses people is that they run on different models, produce different tensors, and — crucially — only one of them computes gradients.

    Phase 1 — Rollout (generation, no gradients)

    Sample a batch of prompts. For each, generate a completion by autoregressive sampling from $\pi_\theta$. As you go, record two things per generated token: the log-probability the policy assigned to the token it actually sampled — call it old_logprob, and detach it — and the critic's value estimate $V_\phi(s_t)$ at that position. No loss is computed here. This phase is pure autoregressive decoding, and it is the wall-clock bottleneck of the entire algorithm: generating a few hundred tokens for thousands of prompts dwarfs the cost of a gradient step. That expense is the whole reason the batch is later reused for several optimisation epochs — the subject of Part 7.

    Phase 2 — Scoring (evaluation, no gradients)

    Now turn the raw completions into learning targets. Run the reward model once per full completion to get its terminal score. Run the frozen reference over every sequence to get its per-token log-probs. Combine them with the KL shaping from Part 9: a per-token penalty proportional to $\log\pi_\theta(a_t\mid s_t) - \log\pi_{\text{ref}}(a_t\mid s_t)$, with the reward model's scalar dropped onto the terminal token. That gives a shaped per-token reward $r_t$. Feed it and the stored values into the GAE recursion — computed backwards from the last token — to get $\hat{A}_t$, and set the value targets to $G_t = \hat{A}_t + V_\phi(s_t)$. Optionally, whiten the advantages across the batch to zero mean and unit variance. Still no gradients: every model here is frozen or detached.

    Phase 3 — Optimisation (the only phase with gradients)

    For $K$ inner epochs, shuffle the batch into minibatches and take gradient steps on the combined loss. This is where and only where backward() is called. The decisive fact: old_logprob, the advantages, and the returns were all computed once, in phases 1 and 2, and are frozen for the entire duration of phase 3. The new policy's log-probs are recomputed every minibatch (they must be, to form the ratio), but everything they are compared against is a constant. This is what makes the reuse valid — each inner epoch is an off-policy correction against the same fixed snapshot.

    ▶ One Iteration, Phase by Phase

    Step through the cycle. Each phase lights up the models doing work and marks which tensors are being computed versus held frozen. Notice that generation and scoring never touch a gradient, and the optimisation phase repeats K times on a batch that is never regenerated.

    The Combined Objective, Term by Term

    Here is the whole thing on one line — the destination the series has been walking toward since Part 3. It is a sum of three pieces, each with its own job and its own scaling constant.

    $$L(\theta,\phi)=\underbrace{-\hat{\mathbb{E}}_t\big[\min\big(r_t(\theta)\hat{A}_t,\ \text{clip}(r_t(\theta),1-\epsilon,1+\epsilon)\hat{A}_t\big)\big]}_{\text{policy (actor)}} \;+\; c_1\underbrace{\hat{\mathbb{E}}_t\big[(V_\phi(s_t)-G_t)^2\big]}_{\text{value (critic)}} \;-\; c_2\underbrace{\hat{\mathbb{E}}_t\big[\mathcal{H}[\pi_\theta(\cdot\mid s_t)]\big]}_{\text{entropy}}$$

    The policy term is exactly the clipped surrogate of Part 8. Minimising its negative pushes the probability of positive-advantage tokens up and negative-advantage tokens down, but the clip flattens the gradient once the ratio $r_t(\theta)$ leaves the trust region, so no single sample can move the policy too far. The value term is a plain squared-error regression: it drags each $V_\phi(s_t)$ toward the return $G_t$ it was supposed to predict. A better critic gives lower-variance advantages, which gives a cleaner policy gradient — the two halves feed each other. $c_1$ is usually around $0.5$ to $1.0$.

    The entropy term rewards keeping the output distribution spread out. $\mathcal{H}[\pi_\theta(\cdot\mid s_t)]$ is the Shannon entropy of the next-token distribution; subtracting $c_2$ times it (so the loss decreases as entropy rises) discourages the policy from collapsing prematurely onto one high-reward template and refusing to explore anything else. In classic control PPO this term matters a lot. In LLM RLHF it is often tiny or set to zero, because the KL-to-reference penalty already resists collapse from a different direction — and a large entropy bonus on a $10^5$-way vocabulary can inject noise faster than it buys exploration. A typical $c_2$ is $0$ to $0.01$.

    The Loop in Full

    Everything above, as runnable-in-spirit code. The GAE helper first — note it walks the sequence backwards and never sees a gradient:

    def gae(rewards, values, mask, gamma, lam):
    # rewards, values, mask: [T]  (values has V(s_T)=0 appended)
    # returns per-token advantages and value targets, all detached
    adv = torch.zeros_like(rewards)
    last = 0.0
    for t in reversed(range(len(rewards))):
        nonterminal = mask[t]                       # 0 past the real EOS
        delta = rewards[t] + gamma * values[t + 1] * nonterminal - values[t]
        last  = delta + gamma * lam * nonterminal * last
        adv[t] = last
    returns = adv + values[:-1]                      # G_t = A_t + V(s_t)
    return adv.detach(), returns.detach()

    And the driver, one full iteration across the three phases:

    for iteration in range(num_iterations):
    # ---- Phase 1: ROLLOUT (no grad) ----------------------------------
    prompts = sample_prompts(batch_size)
    with torch.no_grad():
        seq, logp = policy.generate(prompts)         # autoregressive decode
        old_logprob = logp.detach()                  # FROZEN from here on
        old_values  = critic(seq).detach()           # V(s_t) at gen time
    mask = completion_mask(seq)                       # 1 on generated tokens only

    # ---- Phase 2: SCORING (no grad) ----------------------------------
    with torch.no_grad():
        ref_logprob = reference(seq)                 # frozen model, per token
        score = reward_model(seq)                    # one scalar per sequence
        reward = -beta * (old_logprob - ref_logprob) # per-token KL shaping
        reward = add_terminal(reward, score, mask)   # RM score on last token
        adv, returns = gae(reward, old_values, mask, gamma, lam)
        adv = whiten(adv, mask)                       # optional: mean 0, var 1

    # ---- Phase 3: OPTIMISATION (K inner epochs, the only grads) -------
    for epoch in range(K):
        for mb in minibatches(shuffle(batch_indices)):
            logp = policy.log_prob(seq[mb])           # NEW policy, differentiable
            ratio = torch.exp(logp - old_logprob[mb]) # r_t(theta); == 1 on epoch 0
            s1 = ratio * adv[mb]
            s2 = torch.clamp(ratio, 1 - eps, 1 + eps) * adv[mb]
            policy_loss = -masked_mean(torch.min(s1, s2), mask[mb])

            v = critic(seq[mb])
            value_loss = masked_mean((v - returns[mb]) ** 2, mask[mb])

            ent = masked_mean(policy.entropy(seq[mb]), mask[mb])

            loss = policy_loss + c1 * value_loss - c2 * ent
            opt.zero_grad(); loss.backward()
            clip_grad_norm_(params, max_norm)         # see Part 11
            opt.step()

    Read the detach() calls carefully — they are the load-bearing lines. old_logprob and old_values are detached in phase 1; the advantages and returns come back detached from gae. In phase 3 the only tensors that carry a gradient back to a parameter are logp, v, and ent — the fresh forward passes. And note masked_mean: prompt tokens and padding must never contribute to any term, or you train the model to explain its own prompt.

    A Trace of One Iteration

    Abstractions dissolve under actual numbers. Take a microscopic batch: two prompts, five generated tokens each, $\gamma=1$ and $\lambda=1$ so the arithmetic stays legible. (With $\lambda=1$, GAE reduces to the Monte-Carlo advantage $G_t-V_\phi(s_t)$ derived in Part 6; the backward recursion still produces it, and we use it here only to keep the sums checkable by eye.) Prompt A drew a good completion, Prompt B a poor one. The shaped rewards $r_t$ already fold in a small per-token KL penalty of $-0.05$, with the reward model's terminal score added to the last token.

    Scoring, Prompt A (terminal reward $+1.0$). Read $\delta_t = r_t + V_{t+1} - V_t$, then $\hat{A}_t = \delta_t + \hat{A}_{t+1}$ backwards from the end, and $G_t = \hat{A}_t + V_t$:

    4+0.950.25+0.700.700.95

    0-0.050.200.00-0.95-0.75

    Ten advantages in all. Their mean is $-0.165$ and standard deviation about $0.739$ — note it is not centred at zero, because Prompt B dragged the batch down. That is precisely why whitening is optional-but-common: it would re-centre these to mean $0$.

    Optimisation, first inner epoch. Before any update the new policy still equals the snapshot, so every ratio $r_t(\theta)=1$ exactly and $\min(r_t\hat{A}_t,\,\text{clip}(\cdot)\hat{A}_t)=\hat{A}_t$. Using the raw (un-whitened) advantages, the policy term is just the negative mean advantage:

    $$L_{\text{policy}} = -\tfrac{1}{10}\textstyle\sum_t \hat{A}_t = -(-0.165) = 0.165.$$

    The value term is the mean squared gap between each $V_\phi(s_t)$ and its return $G_t$. Those gaps are large — the critic is badly calibrated this early — and the squared errors sum to $5.727$ over the ten tokens, a mean of $0.573$. With an entropy of about $2.0$ nats per token:

    $$L = \underbrace{0.165}_{L_{\text{policy}}} + \underbrace{0.5 \times 0.573}_{c_1 L_{\text{value}}} - \underbrace{0.01 \times 2.0}_{c_2 \mathcal{H}} = 0.165 + 0.287 - 0.020 = 0.432.$$

    Two lessons fall out of that one number. First, at the very first step the loss is dominated by the value term: the critic is wrong, and most of the early gradient goes into fixing it, not into moving the policy. Second, had we whitened the advantages, $L_{\text{policy}}$ would have been exactly $0$ on this first epoch — the policy signal is entirely in the per-token differences, not in the batch mean. The policy only starts to move once a later epoch shifts the ratios off $1$. Say token A4 (advantage $0.70$) sees its probability rise until $r_t(\theta)=1.30$; with $\epsilon=0.2$ the clip caps its contribution at $1.2\times 0.70 = 0.84$ instead of $1.30\times 0.70 = 0.91$, and its gradient goes flat — exactly the brake from Part 8.

    The Ordering Trap

    Advantages are computed once, before the inner epochs — never inside them. It is tempting to recompute $\hat{A}_t$ with the current policy on each epoch “to keep it fresh.” Do that and you have silently changed the algorithm: the importance-sampling correction of Part 7 assumes a fixed behaviour policy $\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}$ for the whole batch, and the ratio $r_t(\theta)$ is meaningless if its denominator drifts. Freeze the advantages, the returns, and old_logprob at the end of scoring, and treat them as constants until the next rollout. The moment they move inside the epoch loop, the ratio stops being an importance weight and PPO stops being PPO.

    With the loop assembled, two questions remain: which of the forty knobs actually matter, and how do you tell when it is going wrong? That is Part 11. And the four-model memory bill we opened with is the pressure that produced GRPO, DPO and the rest — Part 12.
