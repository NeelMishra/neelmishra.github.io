    Continuous-Action PPO: Densities, Squashing, and Vectorized Rollouts

    September 2026 · 5 min read + experiments

    Build a Gaussian actor, sum the right log probabilities, account for action transforms, and preserve trajectory boundaries in parallel environments.

    Read the explanationTry the calculation

    Replace left-or-right with a sampled number

    A robot may choose a steering angle or several motor commands. PPO’s update stays the same; the policy now describes a distribution over continuous values. A common choice is a Normal distribution, also called a Gaussian.

    The Gaussian actor in this example takes an observation and produces two outputs: mean and log_std. mean is $\mu_\theta(s)$, the center of the possible action values at state $s$. log_std is $\log\sigma_\theta(s)$; exponentiating it gives the positive standard deviation that controls their spread. For a vector with $d$ action components, the actor produces one mean and one log standard deviation per component.

    Normal(mean, log_std.exp()) packages these outputs into a Gaussian distribution. Calling sample() then draws an action from that distribution. The mean describes an action value, such as a steering angle; the critic’s new_value estimates future return.

    One environment action is the whole vector. If its components are independent, their densities multiply, so their log densities add. Write $u_j$ for component $j$ of the sampled vector $u$:

    $$\log p_\theta(u\mid s)=\sum_{j=1}^{d}\log p_\theta(u_j\mid s).$$

    For example, component log densities of −0.4 and −0.7 give a joint log density of −1.1. PPO needs that single number to compare the old and new policies. For a batch of $B$ vectors, PyTorch’s Independent distribution performs this final-dimension sum:

    mean, log_std = actor(obs)           # [B, action_dim] each
base = torch.distributions.Normal(mean, log_std.exp())
action_dist = torch.distributions.Independent(base, 1)
u = action_dist.sample()             # [B, action_dim]
old_logp = action_dist.log_prob(u)    # [B]
# Store u; do not replace it with a newly sampled action during training.

    Why can a density be greater than one?

    Probability is area; density is height, measured as probability per unit of action width. The limit of 1 applies to probability.

    If an action is chosen uniformly between 0 and 0.2, all its probability must fit into that width. Its flat density is $1/0.2=5$, while its total probability is height times width: $5\times0.2=1$. A density of 5 therefore does not mean a 500% chance.

    A narrow Gaussian can also have a height above 1, so its log_prob(u) can be positive: it returns a log density. PPO compares new and old densities at the same stored action; these describe how much probability each policy places nearby.

    Fit the sample inside the action bounds

    A Normal sample can be any real number. If the environment requires an action between −1 and 1, one option is to transform it with $a=\tanh(u)$. Call $u$ the latent sample and $a$ the executed action. For example, $u=1$ becomes approximately $a=0.762$.

    The transformation compresses space near the bounds, so the action’s density changes too. In one dimension, the corrected log density is:

    $$\log\pi_\theta(a\mid s)=\log p_\theta(u\mid s)-\log(1-a^2).$$

    The first term scores the original sample. The second corrects for the compression. For a vector, apply the correction to each component and sum. Use a stable implementation such as PyTorch’s TanhTransform rather than directly taking a logarithm near $a=\pm1$.

    Save the latent sample as well as the executed action. This avoids trying to undo a transform after floating-point rounding. A fixed invertible transform has the same correction for the old and new policies at a stored action, so it cancels in their log-density difference. Keep the transformation consistent during collection and training.

    Hard clipping needs different bookkeeping

    Hard clipping sends every sample above 1 to exactly 1. That is different from the smooth, invertible tanh map. Evaluating a Normal density at the clipped value does not describe how the executed action was sampled.

    A consistent approach is to store and score the original latent sample, treating clipping as part of the environment’s action transformation. The important rule is to compare old and new likelihoods for the same stored quantity. The authors’ PPO implementation study traces these continuous-control choices to concrete code.

    Keep each environment’s timeline intact

    Running $N$ environments for $T$ steps produces a buffer with time and environment axes, [T, N, ...]. Calculate GAE separately down each environment’s timeline before flattening for minibatches. One environment ending must not cut off another’s advantage calculation.

    The boundary rules from chapter 3 still apply. If you normalize observations, also keep that transformation consistent between collection and re-scoring. Saving a neural network without its normalization settings may not reproduce the policy that acted.
