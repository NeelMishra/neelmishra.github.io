    Bias, Variance & Generalized Advantage Estimation

    July 2026 · 23 min read

    Part 5 ended at a fork: the Monte Carlo advantage is unbiased and impossibly noisy, the one-step residual is calm and biased, and neither extreme is a good place to train from. This post builds the bridge between them. It is the most machinery-heavy stop in the series, but every piece is forced by the last, and the famous final formula — a discounted sum of TD residuals with a single tunable decay — will look inevitable rather than invented by the time we reach it.

    Bias and Variance, RL-Style

    The bias–variance trade-off you met in supervised learning does not quite transfer, so reset your intuition. There, variance came from a finite training set and could be beaten down with more labelled data. Here the expectation we are estimating is taken over the space of entire trajectories — every sequence of tokens the policy might emit — and that space is astronomically large: a vocabulary of $10^5$ raised to the power of the response length. We estimate an average over that space from a handful of sampled rollouts per update. Variance is therefore not an occasional nuisance; it is the default condition, and the estimator is fighting it from behind at every step.

    That reframing explains why the whole algorithm leans, again and again, toward variance reduction: bootstrapping off a critic, clipping updates, truncating horizons, normalising advantages. And it explains the recurring tax. Nearly every device that lowers variance does so by injecting a model-based assumption — usually the critic’s current estimate — in place of a random quantity, and if that assumption is even slightly wrong it shows up as bias. Variance is what you have too much of; bias is what you pay to remove it. GAE is simply the most principled way anyone has found to set that price.

    The $n$-Step Family

    Between "one real reward then bootstrap" and "all real rewards to the end" lies a whole ladder. The $n$-step advantage uses $n$ real rewards and then bootstraps off the critic for the tail:

    $$\begin{aligned} \hat{A}^{(1)}_t &= r_t + \gamma V(s_{t+1}) - V(s_t) \\[2pt] \hat{A}^{(2)}_t &= r_t + \gamma r_{t+1} + \gamma^2 V(s_{t+2}) - V(s_t) \\[2pt] \hat{A}^{(3)}_t &= r_t + \gamma r_{t+1} + \gamma^2 r_{t+2} + \gamma^3 V(s_{t+3}) - V(s_t) \\[2pt] &\;\;\vdots \\[2pt] \hat{A}^{(n)}_t &= \Big(\textstyle\sum_{k=0}^{n-1} \gamma^k r_{t+k}\Big) + \gamma^n V(s_{t+n}) - V(s_t). \end{aligned}$$

    Read the ladder top to bottom and one thing happens at every rung: you trade a bootstrapped estimate for one more real reward. The $n=1$ rung leans entirely on the critic after a single step — low variance, high bias. Each additional step swaps a piece of the critic’s guess for a piece of ground truth, lowering bias and raising variance, until the bottom rung, $n = T - t$, reaches the terminal step, bootstraps off nothing (the final $V$ is zero), and becomes exactly the Monte Carlo estimate $G_t - V(s_t)$. The $n$-step family is the continuum between the two poles of Part 5 — but it is a discrete ladder, and choosing a single $n$ is a blunt instrument. GAE will smooth it into a dial.

    The Identity That Makes It Tractable

    The $n$-step estimates look like they involve different-length sums of rewards, which would be awkward to combine. The trick that dissolves the awkwardness is an identity: every $n$-step advantage is the discounted sum of the first $n$ TD residuals.

    $$\hat{A}^{(n)}_t \;=\; \sum_{k=0}^{n-1} \gamma^k\, \delta_{t+k}, \qquad \delta_t = r_t + \gamma V(s_{t+1}) - V(s_t).$$

    Derive it for $n = 2$, where the mechanism is fully visible. Start from the two-step estimate and add and subtract $\gamma V(s_{t+1})$ — a term worth zero:

    $$\begin{aligned} \hat{A}^{(2)}_t &= r_t + \gamma r_{t+1} + \gamma^2 V(s_{t+2}) - V(s_t) \\[2pt] &= \big[\, r_t + \gamma V(s_{t+1}) - V(s_t)\,\big] + \big[\, \gamma r_{t+1} + \gamma^2 V(s_{t+2}) - \gamma V(s_{t+1})\,\big] \\[2pt] &= \delta_t \;+\; \gamma\big[\, r_{t+1} + \gamma V(s_{t+2}) - V(s_{t+1})\,\big] \;=\; \delta_t + \gamma\,\delta_{t+1}. \end{aligned}$$

    The inserted $\gamma V(s_{t+1})$ landed in both brackets with opposite signs, so the intermediate value telescopes away and the whole thing reassembles into two clean residuals. Repeat the trick $n-1$ times — each inserted $\gamma^k V(s_{t+k})$ cancels between adjacent brackets — and the general identity drops out. This is the step most explanations skip, and skipping it is why the GAE formula tends to look arbitrary. It is not: once every $n$-step estimate is a sum of residuals, mixing them is just mixing coefficients on a shared set of $\delta$’s.

    Generalized Advantage Estimation

    Rather than commit to one rung of the ladder, GAE takes an exponentially weighted average of all of them, with a decay parameter $\lambda \in [0, 1]$ that down-weights the longer, noisier estimates:

    $$\hat{A}^{\text{GAE}(\gamma,\lambda)}_t \;=\; (1-\lambda) \sum_{n=1}^{\infty} \lambda^{\,n-1}\, \hat{A}^{(n)}_t.$$

    The $(1-\lambda)$ out front normalises the geometric weights $\lambda^{n-1}$ so they sum to one. Now substitute the residual identity and collect terms by residual. The coefficient on $\delta_t$ is $(1-\lambda)(1 + \lambda + \lambda^2 + \cdots) = 1$; the coefficient on $\gamma\,\delta_{t+1}$ is $(1-\lambda)(\lambda + \lambda^2 + \cdots) = \lambda$; on $\gamma^2\,\delta_{t+2}$ it is $\lambda^2$; and in general the residual $\gamma^k \delta_{t+k}$ carries weight $\lambda^k$. Everything collapses to a single geometric sum:

    $$\hat{A}^{\text{GAE}(\gamma,\lambda)}_t \;=\; \sum_{k=0}^{\infty} (\gamma\lambda)^k\, \delta_{t+k}.$$

    One decay, one sum of residuals. The two poles of Part 5 are its endpoints:

    • $\lambda = 0$: only the $k=0$ term survives, so $\hat{A}_t = \delta_t$. This is the one-step TD estimate — lowest variance, highest bias.
    • $\lambda = 1$: the weights become $\gamma^k$, and the residuals telescope back into $G_t - V(s_t)$, the Monte Carlo advantage — unbiased, highest variance.

    So $\lambda$ is a continuous knob that slides from full bootstrapping to full Monte Carlo without ever forcing you to pick an integer $n$. In practice $\lambda \approx 0.95$ is the standard setting: close enough to $1$ to keep bias small, far enough below it to tame the worst of the variance. Note that $\gamma$ and $\lambda$ play different roles even though they multiply together here — $\gamma$ discounts real future reward and is a property of the problem, while $\lambda$ discounts the influence of the critic’s bootstrap and is purely a variance-reduction dial.

    Computing It: the Backward Recursion

    Summing $\sum_k (\gamma\lambda)^k \delta_{t+k}$ separately for every $t$ would be quadratic in the sequence length. But the sum has an obvious recursive shape — the advantage at $t$ is $\delta_t$ plus a discounted copy of the advantage at $t+1$:

    $$\hat{A}_t \;=\; \delta_t + \gamma\lambda\,\hat{A}_{t+1}.$$

    So a single backward pass computes every advantage in linear time. This loop is the most-copied artefact of any GAE explanation, and it is easy to get subtly wrong at the episode boundaries, so here it is with the boundary handling made explicit:

    import torch

def compute_gae(rewards, values, dones, next_value, gamma=0.99, lam=0.95):
    """
    rewards, values, dones : (T,) tensors for one rollout segment.
        dones[t] == 1.0 marks the last step of an episode.
    next_value : V(s_T), the bootstrap value of the state AFTER the segment.
        Pass 0.0 if the segment ends because the episode terminated.
    Returns advantages (T,) and value targets returns (T,).
    """
    T = rewards.shape[0]
    advantages = torch.zeros(T)
    last_gae = 0.0
    for t in reversed(range(T)):
        mask = 1.0 - dones[t]                       # 0 at an episode boundary
        v_next = next_value if t == T - 1 else values[t + 1]
        delta = rewards[t] + gamma * v_next * mask - values[t]
        last_gae = delta + gamma * lam * mask * last_gae
        advantages[t] = last_gae
    returns = advantages + values                   # critic regression targets
    return advantages, returns

    Two boundary details carry the whole correctness of this function. First, next_value = 0 when the episode actually terminated: there is no state after the end, so there is nothing to bootstrap from, and the final residual reduces to $r_{T-1} - V(s_{T-1})$ plus the real terminal reward. Second, the mask = 1 - dones[t] factor zeroes both the bootstrap and the carried-over last_gae at every episode boundary, so advantages never leak across the seam between two episodes packed into the same batch. Drop the mask and a failure at the end of episode A will silently bleed credit into the start of episode B.

    Run it on the Part 5 trajectory to reproduce the $\lambda = 0.95$ column exactly, then standardise the batch the way every implementation does just before it multiplies the policy term:

    # One episode: only the final step ends it, and we bootstrap off nothing.
rewards = torch.tensor([0., 0., 0., 0., 1.])
values  = torch.tensor([0.50, 0.70, 0.60, 0.80, 0.90])
dones   = torch.tensor([0., 0., 0., 0., 1.])

adv, returns = compute_gae(rewards, values, dones,
                           next_value=0.0, gamma=1.0, lam=0.95)
# adv     ~ [0.45, 0.27, 0.39, 0.20, 0.10]   the lambda = 0.95 column
# returns ~ adv + values  =  critic regression targets

# Standardise advantages to zero mean, unit variance before the update.
adv = (adv - adv.mean()) / (adv.std() + 1e-8)

    The $\lambda = 0$ column is exactly the residuals; the $\lambda = 1$ column is exactly the Monte Carlo advantages from Part 5, closing the loop. Watch how the estimates spread as $\lambda$ climbs. Step 1 is the dramatic one: at $\lambda = 0$ it reads $-0.10$ (the critic thought this step went backward), but as $\lambda$ rises it absorbs the good news from the successful tail and swings all the way to $+0.30$. Step 0 grows from $+0.20$ to $+0.50$ for the same reason — more of the downstream residuals are folded in. The last step barely moves at any $\lambda$, because there are no residuals after it to average. The dial does not just rescale the advantages; it redistributes credit along the trajectory.

    A Detail That Confuses Everyone: the Returns

    The critic still needs regression targets, and this is where readers of real implementations trip. You might expect the value targets to be computed from the raw rewards with their own discounted sum. Almost universally they are not. Instead the targets are recovered from the advantages you just computed, by adding the baseline back:

    $$G_t \;=\; \hat{A}_t + V_\phi(s_t).$$

    That is the returns = advantages + values line in the code, and it looks like circular reasoning until you see why it is deliberate. Rearranged, it just restates the definition of the advantage, $\hat{A}_t = G_t - V_\phi(s_t)$. Using the GAE advantage to define the return makes the critic’s targets consistent with the same $\lambda$-weighted, bootstrapped estimate the actor is trained on, rather than the much noisier raw Monte Carlo return. The critic and actor then agree on what "the return from here" means, which stabilises both. When you read a PPO codebase and find the value targets built from the advantages, this is why — it is not a bug, it is the point.

    Advantages get normalised before the update. One more step you will see in every implementation: the batch of $\hat{A}_t$ is standardised to zero mean and unit variance just before it multiplies the policy term. This keeps the effective step size stable as reward scales drift over training and is the reason the raw magnitude of your advantages rarely matters — only their signs and relative sizes do. We return to it in the implementation post; for now, do not be surprised when the numbers above come out rescaled in real code.

    The Objective So Far

    The advantage slot in the running objective is now filled by the GAE estimate, and with it Module 2 is complete:

    $$L(\theta) \;=\; -\,\log \pi_\theta(a_t \mid s_t)\,\hat{A}^{\text{GAE}(\gamma,\lambda)}_t, \qquad \hat{A}^{\text{GAE}(\gamma,\lambda)}_t = \sum_{k=0}^{\infty} (\gamma\lambda)^k\, \delta_{t+k}.$$

    We now have a genuinely good estimate of how much better than average each token was, at a bias–variance point we control. But the objective still weights the log-probability, and it still assumes the data was generated by exactly the policy we are updating. The moment we try to reuse a batch of expensive rollouts for more than one gradient step — and we will, because generation is the costly part — that assumption breaks. Part 7 replaces the log with a probability ratio to repair it, and sets up the instability that PPO’s clipping exists to contain.
