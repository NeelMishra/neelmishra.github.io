    KL Penalties, Reference Models & Reward Hacking

    July 2026 · 21 min read

    The clipped objective keeps each update close to the policy that collected the batch. That is enough to make the optimisation stable, and it is not enough to make the result good. Two things it never looked at: how far the policy has crept from the model you started with after hundreds of small legal steps, and whether the reward it is diligently maximising still means anything. This post is about the mechanism that addresses both — a KL penalty against a frozen reference model — and about the failure it exists to prevent, which is the most characteristic pathology in all of RLHF.

    Two Gaps the Clip Left Open

    Recall precisely what the clip constrains: the ratio $r_t(\theta)=\pi_\theta/\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}$, where $\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}$ is last iteration's policy. Every iteration, $\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}$ is refreshed to wherever the policy currently sits. So the clip is a constraint that moves with you. Take two hundred iterations of small, individually-legal steps and the policy can end up somewhere the original model would not recognise — each step was within the trust region of the step before it, but the composition of two hundred such steps is unbounded. Nothing in $L^{\text{CLIP}}$ anchors the policy to a fixed point.

    The second gap is deeper. The reward signal in RLHF is not the truth; it is a model of the truth. Optimise a model of the truth hard enough and you stop optimising the truth. Both gaps have the same fix, and it hangs on a distinction worth stating carefully before anything else.

    The Distinction That Organises Everything

    Clipping and the KL penalty constrain two different things against two different anchors. The clip constrains the step relative to $\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}$ — last iteration's policy, refreshed every iteration — and its job is optimisation stability. The KL penalty constrains the policy relative to $\pi_{\text{ref}}$ — the frozen SFT checkpoint, never updated for the entire run — and its job is not destroying the model. One is a moving guardrail on the size of each step; the other is a fixed tether to the coherent model you began with. They are not redundant, and confusing them is the single most common mistake people make reading a PPO implementation.

    Say it once more in the concrete, because it is that important. $\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}$ is a photograph taken at the start of each iteration and thrown away at the end. $\pi_{\text{ref}}$ is a photograph taken once, before training began, and kept in a drawer for reference for the whole run. Clipping asks “did I move too far since the last photo?” The KL penalty asks “have I wandered too far from who I originally was?”

    Reward Hacking and Goodhart's Law

    Why tether the policy to its old self at all? Because the reward model is a learned proxy. It was trained on a finite dataset of human preference comparisons, and it is accurate in the region of response-space where those comparisons lived — near the SFT model's own outputs — and increasingly unreliable the further you travel from that region. This is Goodhart's law in its cleanest form: a measure that is a good proxy for quality under normal conditions stops being one the moment it becomes the target of hard optimisation. The optimiser's entire job is to search response-space for the highest reward, which means its entire job is to find the places where the proxy is wrong and reward is high.

    Concretely, and in ways you can watch happen, an unconstrained policy tends to discover things like these:

    • Degenerate repetition. A phrasing or sentence the reward model happens to score highly, repeated with slight variation, because the model found a local pocket the RM over-rewards.
    • Length inflation. If longer answers were mildly preferred by annotators — and they usually were — the policy learns that padding raises reward, and responses swell with qualifications and restated premises that add no content.
    • Flattery and hedging. Deferential openers, excessive agreement, and safe non-answers that the RM associates with “helpful and harmless” without checking whether the question was actually answered.
    • Boilerplate scaffolding. Formulaic introductions and summaries — “Great question! Here are the key points” — that pattern-match to high-scoring training responses regardless of the prompt.
    • Template collapse. In the limit, the policy converges to one high-scoring response shape and emits variations of it no matter what was asked, having found a single point that games the proxy.

    The diagnostic signature is unmistakable once you know to look for it: the reward-model score climbs smoothly and convincingly while human evaluation and held-out quality flatten and then fall. The gap between the two is the reward hacking, quantified. The animation makes the shape concrete — and shows what the KL penalty does to it.

    The KL Penalty, Two Placements

    The fix is to penalise divergence from the reference. The penalty is the KL divergence between the current policy and the frozen reference, per token:

    $$\mathrm{D}_{\mathrm{KL}}\big(\pi_\theta \,\Vert\, \pi_{\text{ref}}\big)(s_t)\;=\;\mathbb{E}_{a\sim\pi_\theta}\!\left[\log\frac{\pi_\theta(a\mid s_t)}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(a\mid s_t)}\right].$$

    Where you insert it changes what it interacts with, and the two common placements are not equivalent.

    Placement 1 — reward shaping. This is the InstructGPT recipe (Ouyang et al., 2022). Fold the penalty into the per-token reward before any advantage is computed, using the single-sample estimate of the KL:

    $$\tilde{r}_t \;=\; r_t \;-\; \beta\big(\log\pi_\theta(a_t\mid s_t) - \log\pi_{\text{ref}}(a_t\mid s_t)\big).$$

    Because the penalty is now part of the reward, it flows through the entire machinery of the previous posts — the return $G_t$, the GAE advantage $\hat{A}_t$, and the critic's value targets all see it, exactly as they would see any environment reward. It is credit-assigned across the sequence like everything else. And it quietly fixes a problem we have carried since the start: outcome-only rewards are terminal-sparse, a single scalar at the end of a 400-token response. The per-token KL is dense — every token gets a signal — so shaping the reward this way densifies the learning signal as an underrated side effect. This is the ninth milestone of the running objective: the reward the whole pipeline optimises is now $\tilde{r}_t$, not $r_t$.

    Placement 2 — a direct loss term. Alternatively, leave the reward alone and subtract $\beta\,\mathrm{D}_{\mathrm{KL}}$ from the objective directly, alongside $L^{\text{CLIP}}$. This is simpler and more transparent, but the penalty no longer passes through GAE or the critic; it acts only as an extra gradient on the policy, penalising average divergence rather than being attributed token by token.

    Estimating the KL Without Blowing Up

    You never have the full KL; you have samples. The obvious estimator drops the expectation and uses the single realised token:

    $$\widehat{\mathrm{KL}}_{\text{naive}} \;=\; \log\pi_\theta(a_t\mid s_t) - \log\pi_{\text{ref}}(a_t\mid s_t).$$

    It is unbiased — its expectation under $\pi_\theta$ is the true KL — but it is high-variance, and crucially it can be negative for an individual token, whenever the policy happened to sample a token it rates lower than the reference does. A per-step penalty that flickers negative is a noisy signal to hand the critic, and a KL measurement that can come out below zero is a confusing thing to threshold a controller on. John Schulman's widely-used low-variance alternative — the “k3” estimator — fixes both. With $\rho = \pi_{\text{ref}}/\pi_\theta$,

    $$\widehat{\mathrm{KL}}_{k3} \;=\; \rho - 1 - \log\rho, \qquad \rho=\frac{\pi_{\text{ref}}(a_t\mid s_t)}{\pi_\theta(a_t\mid s_t)}.$$

    The function $x-1-\log x$ is zero at $x=1$ and strictly positive everywhere else, so this estimate is always non-negative, matching the fact that a true KL cannot be negative. It is still unbiased — a short calculation shows $\mathbb{E}_{\pi_\theta}[\rho-1-\log\rho]=\mathrm{D}_{\mathrm{KL}}(\pi_\theta\Vert\pi_{\text{ref}})$, because $\mathbb{E}_{\pi_\theta}[\rho]=1$ and $\mathbb{E}_{\pi_\theta}[-\log\rho]$ is exactly the KL — and it has markedly lower variance. Implementations reach for it whenever they need a stable KL number to log, to threshold, or to feed an adaptive controller.

    def kl_penalty_k3(logp_policy, logp_ref):
    # Schulman's low-variance, non-negative KL estimate.
    # rho = pi_ref / pi_theta = exp(logp_ref - logp_policy)
    log_rho = logp_ref - logp_policy
    rho = torch.exp(log_rho)
    return rho - 1.0 - log_rho          # always >= 0, unbiased for KL(pi_theta || pi_ref)

    And here is the reward-shaping placement in code, using the signed single-sample penalty per token and dropping the terminal reward-model score onto the last real token of each sequence:

    def shape_rewards(logp_policy, logp_ref, rm_score, mask, beta=0.2):
    # logp_policy, logp_ref : (B, T) log-probs of the SAMPLED tokens
    # rm_score              : (B,)   scalar reward-model score per sequence
    # dense, signed per-token KL penalty (log pi_theta - log pi_ref)
    kl = logp_policy - logp_ref                                # (B, T)
    shaped = -beta * kl                                        # penalty on every token
    # add the terminal RM score at each row's last real token
    last = mask.long().sum(dim=1) - 1                          # (B,) final-token index
    rows = torch.arange(shaped.size(0))
    shaped[rows, last] = shaped[rows, last] + rm_score         # sparse reward lands here
    return shaped * mask                                       # zero the padding

    Adaptive Controllers and Early Stopping

    How large should $\beta$ be? Too small and the tether is slack and the policy hacks the reward anyway; too large and the penalty dominates, the policy refuses to leave the reference, and nothing is learned. A fixed $\beta$ is a guess that is wrong for most of training, because the natural scale of the KL changes as the policy moves. The adaptive controller from the InstructGPT work turns $\beta$ into a feedback loop: pick a target KL, measure the actual KL each iteration, and nudge $\beta$ up when the measured KL overshoots the target and down when it undershoots.

    class AdaptiveKL:
    def __init__(self, beta, target):
        self.beta, self.target = beta, target
    def update(self, measured_kl):
        # proportional control: raise beta if we drifted too far, lower it if too close
        err = measured_kl / self.target - 1.0
        err = max(-0.2, min(0.2, err))     # clamp the proportional error for stability
        self.beta = self.beta * (1.0 + err)
        return self.beta

    The trade-off against a fixed coefficient is the usual one: the controller keeps the KL where you asked it to sit, at the cost of another moving part with its own target and gain to tune. Many modern stacks take a blunter and very effective route instead — keep $\beta$ fixed (or drop the reward-shaping penalty entirely) and early-stop the inner epochs the moment the measured policy-to-old KL crosses a threshold. That is the same measured-KL check the clipping post gestured at, now doing double duty: it caps per-update drift and, iteration after iteration, keeps the policy from running away from the reference.

    The Cost: A Fourth Model in Memory

    The reference model is not free. Up to this point the training loop has held three networks: the policy $\pi_\theta$ being trained, the critic $V_\phi$ being trained, and the reward model scoring completions. The KL penalty adds a fourth — $\pi_{\text{ref}}$ — and it is the same size as the policy. Four large models resident at once is the defining memory burden of PPO-based RLHF, and it is the setup for two later threads: the full training loop has to orchestrate all four, and the critique in the finale is largely about this cost being what motivates critic-free successors.

    There is one consolation. The reference model is frozen — pure inference, no gradients, no optimiser state — and so is the reward model. Only the policy and critic carry the heavy optimiser and activation memory of training. Because the reference never updates, it can be quantised, kept in a lower precision, or offloaded to CPU or a separate device and queried in batches, trading a little latency for a lot of memory. It has to be present; it does not have to be expensive.

    A Worked Numeric Example

    Take $\beta=0.2$ and four tokens of one response.     Three things to read off it. Token 2 is the policy being much more confident than the reference — a strong divergence — and it draws the largest penalty, exactly the drift we want to discourage. Token 3 shows the naive estimator's pathology: the policy sampled a token it rates lower than the reference, so the single-sample KL is negative and the “penalty” briefly becomes a small reward — a variance artefact the k3 estimator would remove. And every intermediate token now carries a nonzero shaped reward, so the once-terminal-only signal is dense all along the sequence, with the reward model's $+1.20$ still landing on the final token where it belongs.

    Where This Leaves Us

    The objective now has two guardrails serving two purposes. The clip holds each step near last iteration's policy for stability; the KL penalty holds the policy near the frozen reference so it neither wanders into incoherence nor hacks the reward model. Folded into the reward, the quantity the pipeline maximises is

    $$\tilde{r}_t \;=\; r_t \;-\; \beta\big(\log\pi_\theta(a_t\mid s_t) - \log\pi_{\text{ref}}(a_t\mid s_t)\big), \qquad \text{ascended via } L^{\text{CLIP}}(\theta).$$

    What is still missing is the plumbing: a value-function loss to train the critic that produced $\hat{A}_t$, an entropy bonus to keep the policy from collapsing, and the orchestration of all four models across a rollout–score–optimise cycle. Assembling those into the single objective PPO actually minimises is the job of the next post, The Complete PPO Training Loop.
