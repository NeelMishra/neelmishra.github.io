    The RL Framing of Language Models

    July 2026 · 18 min read

    Before any of PPO's machinery makes sense, one translation has to click: a language model generating text is an agent making a sequence of decisions, and training it with reinforcement learning is nothing more exotic than nudging those decisions toward higher-scoring outcomes. This post builds that translation carefully, because every later idea in the series — returns, baselines, advantages, clipping — is a patch on some consequence of it. Get the framing wrong and the rest reads like arbitrary algebra. Get it right and the algebra becomes inevitable.

    Where we are. This is Part 1, so there is no leftover problem from a previous post — only the goal of the whole series: to build the PPO objective one term at a time until nothing in it is memorised. We start at the foundation, by asking what it even means to treat next-token prediction as a control problem, and we end with the single expected-reward objective that the next eleven posts learn to optimise.

    Why Reach for RL When Fine-Tuning Exists?

    Supervised fine-tuning (SFT) already produces capable assistants: collect a pile of prompt-and-ideal-answer pairs, and train the model to reproduce each answer token by token. It is stable, cheap, and it works. So why bolt on the whole apparatus of reinforcement learning?

    The honest answer is a single limitation. SFT can only imitate demonstrations that someone actually wrote down. Its loss compares the model's next-token distribution against a specific gold token at every position, so the ceiling on quality is the quality of the human-authored continuations in your dataset. If you want the model to be better than your annotators, SFT has no vocabulary for that request: there is no gold token to imitate for an answer nobody has written.

    Reinforcement learning removes exactly that constraint. Instead of demanding a correct answer to copy, it asks only for a score on outputs the model produces itself. You do not have to author the better answer; you only have to recognise, after the fact, that one sampled answer is better than another. That is a dramatically weaker requirement. Judging “this response is more helpful than that one” is easy; writing the ideal response from scratch is hard. RL is the machinery that turns cheap comparative judgement into a training signal — the only mechanism we have for expressing “this is better than that” without ever having to compose the better one.

    That difference — imitating a fixed target versus optimising a score on your own samples — is the entire reason this series exists. Everything else is the question of how to do that optimisation without the process exploding.

    Language Generation as a Markov Decision Process

    Reinforcement learning lives inside a formal object called a Markov Decision Process: an agent observes a state, chooses an action, receives a reward, and moves to a new state, repeating until an episode ends. To use RL on text we have to say what each of those words means for a model emitting tokens. The mapping that actually holds up is this.

    MDP conceptIn autoregressive text generation
    Policy $\pi_\theta$The language model itself. Given a context it returns a distribution over the vocabulary. The model does not have a policy — it is the policy.
    Action $a_t$A single token, sampled from $\pi_\theta(\cdot \mid s_t)$.
    State $s_t$The prompt concatenated with every token generated so far. The state grows by exactly one token each step.
    TransitionDeterministic: $s_{t+1} = s_t \Vert a_t$, appending the chosen token to the context.
    Reward $r_t$Usually $0$ at every step except the last, where a scorer grades the finished response.
    EpisodeOne complete response, from the prompt to the end-of-sequence (EOS) token.
    Trajectory $\tau$The full record $(s_0, a_0, s_1, a_1, \dots, s_T)$ of one episode.

    The most important row is the first. It is tempting to imagine the model as an agent that consults a policy, the way a chess engine consults a lookup table. That picture is wrong and it will confuse everything downstream. The network's forward pass, from context to a vector of token probabilities, is the policy. When we later write $\nabla_\theta \log \pi_\theta(a_t \mid s_t)$, the $\theta$ we differentiate is the model's own weights, and pushing the policy up or down means changing those weights. There is no separate controller.

    The state deserves a second look too. Because $s_t$ is the entire prefix — prompt plus all emitted tokens — the Markov property holds trivially: the next-token distribution depends only on $s_t$, and $s_t$ already contains the complete history, so nothing about the past is hidden from it. That is a comfortable property to have. It comes at a steep price, discussed next.

    Three Ways This MDP Is Unusual

    The mapping above is standard, but three features of the language MDP are genuinely unlike a robotics or control problem, and each one drives a design decision later in the series.

    1. Transitions are deterministic. In a robot, choosing a motor torque leads to a next state governed by physics, friction, and noise — the environment is stochastic. In text, “take action $a_t$” means “append token $a_t$”, and appending is not random. So $s_{t+1}$ is a deterministic function of $s_t$ and $a_t$. Every bit of randomness in a rollout comes from one place: sampling the policy. This is a real simplification, and in Part 3 it makes a term in the policy-gradient derivation vanish cleanly instead of merely in expectation.

    2. The action space is the whole vocabulary. A control agent might pick among a handful of actions; a language model picks among every token it knows, on the order of $10^5$ discrete choices at every step. That enormous branching factor is why we can never enumerate futures and why the probability of any specific action is tiny, which will matter when we start taking ratios of probabilities.

    3. The state space is astronomically large. A state is a unique token prefix. The number of distinct length-$n$ prefixes is roughly $|V|^n$ — for a vocabulary of $10^5$ and a modest 200-token answer, more states than atoms in the observable universe, by an absurd margin. Two consequences follow. First, within a single episode, states never repeat: each step appends a token, so every $s_t$ is strictly longer than the last and no state is ever revisited. Second, and decisively, any function of the state — how good is this position, what reward can I still expect — cannot be stored in a table. The value function $V$ and action-value function $Q$, which classical RL sometimes tabulates cell by cell, here must be learned function approximators: neural networks that generalise across prefixes they have never seen. Parts 4 and 5 are, at bottom, about building and trusting one such approximator.

    A trap worth naming. Determinism of the transition is not determinism of the action. The policy still samples tokens stochastically; that is where exploration lives. What is deterministic is only the bookkeeping step of gluing the sampled token onto the context. Confusing the two leads people to think RL on text has no randomness to reason about — when in fact all of it is concentrated in the one place we control.

    The SFT Loss vs. the RL Objective

    Putting the two training signals side by side sharpens the distinction, because they are computed from different things and answer different questions.

    SFT minimises the cross-entropy between the model's predicted distribution and a known target token at each position. Crucially, the target distribution is given: for each context there is one gold next token, and the loss is small exactly when the model concentrates probability there. The optimisation problem is fully specified before training starts.

    $$ \mathcal{L}_{\text{SFT}}(\theta) = -\sum_{t} \log \pi_\theta\big(y_t^{\star} \mid s_t\big) \qquad \text{where } y_t^{\star} \text{ is the human-written gold token.} $$

    The RL objective asks something the SFT loss cannot phrase. It is the expected score of continuations the model samples for itself:

    $$ J(\theta) = \mathbb{E}_{\tau \sim \pi_\theta}\big[R(\tau)\big], \qquad R(\tau) = \text{total reward of trajectory } \tau. $$

    Here there is no target distribution at all. Nobody tells the model which token was correct; it emits a whole response, a scorer returns one number, and the number is all we have. The target that SFT hands you for free is, in RL, the very thing that is unknown — and recovering a usable per-token learning signal from a single end-of-episode scalar is precisely the problem the next several posts solve. Concretely the two look like this:

    # Supervised fine-tuning: imitate a reference answer token-by-token.
# target[t] is a token someone WROTE DOWN; the loss pulls the model toward it.
sft_loss = cross_entropy(logits, target)        # needs a gold continuation

# RL: there is no gold continuation. We sample our OWN continuation,
# score the whole thing, and push its probability up or down by that score.
tokens, log_probs = rollout(policy, prompt)     # the model's own output
reward            = score(prompt, tokens)        # one scalar for the response
rl_loss           = -(sum(log_probs) * reward)   # REINFORCE, in one line

    That last line is a preview of Part 3. Notice it contains no target token — only the log-probabilities of the tokens the model happened to sample, scaled by how good the result was. Understanding why that one line is a sensible gradient, and why in its naive form it is nearly unusable, is the arc of the next two posts.

    A Worked Rollout

    Abstract mappings blur; a concrete episode fixes them. Here is a small arithmetic prompt we will reuse throughout the series. The prompt asks: “A shelf holds 3 boxes; each box has 4 mugs. Total mugs? A:” The model samples a six-token response, 3 · times · 4 · is · 12 · EOS, and a verifier checks the final number against the known answer, $12$, awarding $+1$.

    Trace the MDP quantities step by step. Each row shows the step index $t$, the state $s_t$ that the policy conditions on (abbreviated to its tail), the action $a_t$ it samples, and the reward $r_t$ that arrives:

    $t$State $s_t$ (tail shown)Action $a_t$Reward $r_t$
    0… Total mugs? A:30
    1… A: 3times0
    2… 3 times40
    3… 3 times 4is0
    4… 3 times 4 is120
    5… 3 times 4 is 12EOS+1

    Three things are worth reading off the table directly. The state at each step is strictly longer than at the one before, confirming that states never repeat. The reward column is zero everywhere but the final row — the signal is radically sparse, a single scalar for six decisions. And the sampling loop that produced it is short enough to write in full:

    # One rollout: sample a response token-by-token to build a trajectory.
# policy(state) returns a probability vector over the whole vocabulary.
state   = list(prompt_token_ids)     # s_0 is just the prompt
tokens, log_probs = [], []

for t in range(MAX_NEW_TOKENS):
    probs = policy(state)            # distribution over |V| ~ 1e5 tokens
    a_t   = sample(probs)            # one action = one token id
    log_probs.append(log(probs[a_t]))
    tokens.append(a_t)
    state = state + [a_t]            # deterministic: s_{t+1} = s_t + a_t
    if a_t == EOS_ID:
        break

reward = score(prompt_token_ids, tokens)   # a single scalar, at the end

    What Is the “Environment” Here?

    In classical RL the environment is a rich, autonomous world — a physics engine, a game, a market. For a language model it is far thinner, and pinning down what it consists of clears up a lot of confusion. Four pieces do all the work:

    • The sampler that draws the next token from $\pi_\theta$ and appends it. This is the transition, and as we saw it is deterministic given the sampled token.
    • The scorer — a reward model, a programmatic verifier, or a unit-test harness — that grades the finished response. Part 2 is entirely about where this number comes from.
    • Tool calls, in agentic setups: a calculator, a search API, a code sandbox. These do inject genuine external stochasticity and non-determinism, and they are the one case where the tidy “deterministic transition” story needs an asterisk.
    • The human, upstream, who wrote the prompt and whose preferences trained the scorer — present in the loop only indirectly.

    For a pure text task with a fixed scorer, the environment is almost trivial: no world to model, no dynamics to predict, no perception to solve. That is why RL on language is, in one sense, easier than robotics — the transition function is a string concatenation. It is also, in another sense, harder: the action space is vast, the reward is a single distant scalar, and the thing being optimised is a hundred-billion-parameter network whose outputs you must keep coherent while you push them around. The rest of the series is about that second sense.

    Where This Leaves Us

    We now have the objects PPO operates on. The policy $\pi_\theta$ is the model; an action is a token; a state is the prompt-plus-prefix; an episode is a response; and a trajectory $\tau$ is one full sampled rollout. Transitions are deterministic, the action space is the vocabulary, and any value we want to attach to a state must be learned rather than tabulated. The goal, stated once and pursued for the rest of the series, is to maximise the expected score of the model's own outputs:

    $$ \boxed{\; J(\theta) = \mathbb{E}_{\tau \sim \pi_\theta}\big[R(\tau)\big] \;} $$

    Two questions stand between us and optimising it, and they define the next two posts. First: where does $R(\tau)$ actually come from — what produces that scalar, and why is it so sparse? That is Part 2. Second: how do you take the gradient of $J$ with respect to $\theta$ when the distribution you are averaging over itself depends on $\theta$? You cannot simply differentiate the reward. That is Part 3, the mathematical heart of the module.
