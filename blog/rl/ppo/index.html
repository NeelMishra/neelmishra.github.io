    PPO for LLMs: The Complete Guide

    July 2026 · 12 posts

    Proximal Policy Optimization is the algorithm that turned reinforcement learning from a robotics curiosity into the thing that makes chat models helpful. It is the RL in RLHF, and it is the backbone of the first generation of reasoning models trained to think before they answer. If you have used a modern assistant, you have used a model that PPO — or one of its direct descendants — shaped.

    It is also, by reputation, hard to learn. Not because any single idea in it is difficult, but because the final objective is a stack of about eight independent ideas, each invented to patch a specific failure of the one before it. Read the 2017 paper cold and you meet all eight at once, compressed into one line of notation. Read a production implementation and you meet forty hyperparameters with no indication which three actually matter.

    This series unstacks it. Each post introduces one idea, shows the concrete failure that motivated it, and adds it to a running objective that grows term by term until it is exactly the PPO loss. Nothing is asserted without a reason, and no notation appears before the quantity it names has been derived.

    Where this fits. PPO is a policy gradient method — it improves a policy by directly differentiating expected reward, rather than learning values and acting greedily. If terms like state, return, and value function are new, read the Fundamentals of RL notes first. This series re-derives what it needs, but assumes you have met the vocabulary once.

    PPO in One Sentence

    Strip away the machinery and PPO says this: make good actions more likely and bad actions less likely, in proportion to how much better than average they were — but never change the policy so much in one step that your estimate of "better than average" stops being valid.

    The first clause is the policy gradient, and it is about sixty years old. The second clause is PPO's actual contribution, and it is one min and one clip. Everything between those two clauses is the apparatus needed to estimate "better than average" from a handful of noisy samples.

    $$L^{\text{CLIP}}(\theta) \;=\; \hat{\mathbb{E}}_t\Big[\min\big(r_t(\theta)\,\hat{A}_t,\;\; \text{clip}\big(r_t(\theta),\,1-\epsilon,\,1+\epsilon\big)\,\hat{A}_t\big)\Big], \qquad r_t(\theta)=\frac{\pi_\theta(a_t\mid s_t)}{\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}(a_t\mid s_t)}$$

    By the end of this series every symbol above will be something you derived rather than memorised: why there is a ratio instead of a log, why the ratio is clipped, why the clip is wrapped in a min, and where $\hat{A}_t$ comes from.

    Why Clipping Works

    The single most important picture in PPO is what the objective does as the policy drifts away from the one that collected the data. The ratio $r_t$ is exactly that drift: it equals $1$ when nothing has changed, rises above $1$ when the new policy makes the sampled token more likely, and falls below $1$ when it makes it less likely.

    Plot the objective against $r_t$ and the design becomes obvious. When the action was good ($\hat{A}_t \gt 0$) the objective rewards raising its probability — but only up to $1+\epsilon$, after which the line goes flat and there is no further gradient. When the action was bad ($\hat{A}_t \lt 0$) it rewards lowering the probability, flattening at $1-\epsilon$. The flat regions are the whole point: they remove the incentive to take a huge step off a single noisy sample.

    The Part That Trips People Up: Language as an MDP

    Classical RL has an obvious agent moving through an obvious world. Language generation has neither, and forcing the mapping is where most explanations lose people. The translation that actually works:

    RL conceptIn text generation
    AgentThe language model being trained.
    Policy $\pi_\theta$The model itself — it maps a context to a distribution over the vocabulary.
    Action $a_t$One generated token, sampled from that distribution.
    State $s_t$The prompt plus every token generated so far. States grow by one token per step.
    EpisodeOne complete response, from the prompt to the end-of-sequence token.
    Reward $r_t$Usually $0$ everywhere except the final step, where a reward model or a correctness check scores the whole response.
    TransitionDeterministic — appending a token to a context is not stochastic. This is a genuine simplification over robotics.

    Two consequences fall straight out of that table and drive most of the algorithm design. First, the action space is the vocabulary — on the order of $10^5$ discrete choices at every step, which is enormous compared to the handful of joint torques in a control problem. Second, and much worse, the reward is radically sparse: a 500-token response earns exactly one scalar at the very end, and the algorithm has to work out which of those 500 token choices deserve credit. That credit assignment problem is what value functions and advantage estimation exist to solve, and it is why they occupy four of the twelve posts here.

    The Roadmap

    Read top to bottom. Each post ends with the objective as it stands at that point, so you can always see how far the construction has got.

    Module 1: The setup

    1. The RL Framing of Language Models: agent, environment, policy, state, action, trajectory; why next-token prediction is a sequential decision problem; deterministic transitions; the size of the action space; and what supervised fine-tuning cannot express that RL can.
    2. Rewards, Sparse Signals & Discounted Returns: outcome rewards versus process rewards, reward models trained from preference pairs, verifiable rewards for maths and code, the discount factor $\gamma$, the return $G_t$, and why $\gamma \approx 1$ is normal for language.
    3. Policy Gradients from First Principles: the objective $J(\theta)=\mathbb{E}[R]$, the log-derivative trick, the REINFORCE estimator, why the loss is $-\log \pi_\theta(a_t\mid s_t)\hat{A}_t$ and not a distance to a target, and why a plain reward weighting has crippling variance.
    4. The Value Function & the Critic: $V^\pi(s)$ as expected future return, why a baseline reduces variance without introducing bias, the critic head, its regression loss against the observed returns, and the actor–critic architecture.

    Module 2: Estimating the advantage

    1. Advantage: How Much Better Than Average?: $A(s,a)=Q(s,a)-V(s)$, the Monte Carlo estimate from a completed episode, the one-step temporal-difference estimate via bootstrapping, and what each one is really measuring.
    2. Bias, Variance & Generalized Advantage Estimation: why Monte Carlo is unbiased but wildly noisy and TD is stable but biased, the $n$-step family that interpolates between them, the TD residual $\delta_t$, and the exponentially-weighted sum that gives $\hat{A}_t^{\text{GAE}}$ with its $\lambda$ dial.
    3. Off-Policy Reuse & Importance Sampling: why generating rollouts is the expensive step, why reusing them for several epochs breaks the on-policy assumption, the importance weight that corrects the mismatch, and how the ratio $r_t(\theta)$ replaces the log-probability in the objective.

    Module 3: PPO itself

    1. The Clipped Surrogate Objective: what TRPO's trust region did and why it was impractical, clipping as a cheap first-order substitute, why the min makes the bound pessimistic, the exact gradient behaviour in each of the four cases, and how to choose $\epsilon$.
    2. KL Penalties, Reference Models & Reward Hacking: the frozen reference model, per-token KL as a reward penalty versus as a loss term, reward hacking and how it actually looks in practice, adaptive KL controllers, and why clipping alone is not enough for language.
    3. The Complete PPO Training Loop: the four models held in memory, the rollout/scoring/optimisation phases, the combined actor–critic–entropy loss, full annotated pseudocode, and a trace of one iteration end to end.

    Module 4: Practice and the state of the art

    1. Implementation Details & Debugging: advantage normalisation, value-loss clipping, gradient clipping, the handful of hyperparameters that matter, the diagnostic curves to watch, and the failure modes behind entropy collapse, KL explosions, and a critic that never learns.
    2. GRPO, DPO & Where PPO Stands Today: dropping the critic in favour of group-relative baselines, skipping RL entirely with a preference loss, RLOO and REINFORCE-style revivals, verifiable-reward training for reasoning, and an honest account of when PPO is still the right choice.

    Notation Used Throughout

    $\pi_\theta$The policy — the language model with parameters $\theta$.
    $\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}$The snapshot of the policy that generated the current batch of rollouts.
    $\pi_{\text{ref}}$The frozen reference model, normally the SFT checkpoint, used for the KL penalty.
    $s_t$State at step $t$: the prompt plus tokens $a_0 \dots a_{t-1}$.
    $a_t$Action at step $t$: the token emitted.
    $r_t$Reward at step $t$. Zero except at the terminal step in outcome-only setups.
    $\gamma$Discount factor, $0 \lt \gamma \le 1$.
    $\lambda$GAE trace decay — the bias/variance dial.
    $G_t$Discounted return from step $t$ to the end of the episode.
    $V_\phi$The critic: a learned estimate of the state value, parameters $\phi$.
    $\delta_t$TD residual, $r_t + \gamma V_\phi(s_{t+1}) - V_\phi(s_t)$.
    $\hat{A}_t$Estimated advantage at step $t$.
    $r_t(\theta)$Probability ratio $\pi_\theta(a_t\mid s_t)/\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}(a_t\mid s_t)$. Note the clash with reward $r_t$ — the argument $(\theta)$ disambiguates, and this series always writes it explicitly.
    $\epsilon$Clipping range, typically $0.1$–$0.2$.
    On the $r$ collision. The literature genuinely overloads $r$ for both reward and probability ratio, and it is a real source of confusion when reading the paper. Throughout this series, a bare $r_t$ is always a reward and $r_t(\theta)$ with its argument is always the ratio.

    Prerequisites

    • Comfort with expectations, gradients, and the chain rule. No measure theory.
    • Knowing what a transformer language model outputs at each position — a distribution over the vocabulary. That is genuinely all the architecture knowledge required.
    • Basic RL vocabulary. The MDP notes cover it in one sitting.
