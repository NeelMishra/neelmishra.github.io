    Off-Policy Reuse & Importance Sampling

    July 2026 · 20 min read

    The previous post handed us a good advantage estimator. GAE gave a per-token $\hat{A}_t$ with a $\lambda$ dial that trades bias against variance, and the running objective stood at $-\log\pi_\theta(a_t\mid s_t)\,\hat{A}_t$ with $\hat{A}_t=\hat{A}_t^{\text{GAE}(\gamma,\lambda)}$. Every derivation up to here quietly assumed one thing: that the tokens we are averaging over were sampled from the policy we are currently optimising. This post is about what happens when that assumption becomes too expensive to keep — and the correction that lets us break it on purpose.

    Where We Are, and the Wall We Hit

    Here is the loop implied by everything so far. Sample a batch of responses from the current policy, score them, compute advantages, take one gradient step. Then, because the gradient step moved $\theta$, the batch is now off-policy, so throw it away and sample a fresh batch. One batch, one update. It is correct, and it is ruinously slow. To see why, you have to look at where the wall-clock time actually goes.

    The Economics of a Rollout

    The two halves of the loop are not remotely the same price. Consider what it takes to produce one training example versus what it takes to learn from it.

    Generating a response is autoregressive. To sample a 400-token answer, the model runs a forward pass to produce the distribution over the first token, samples one token, appends it, runs another forward pass for the second token, and so on — four hundred forward passes, each depending on the output of the last. There is no teacher forcing here, because there is no ground-truth continuation to feed in; the model is writing the sequence it will later be graded on. And there is no parallelism across time, because token $t$ cannot be computed until token $t-1$ exists. This sequential decode is the single most expensive phase of RLHF, and it is why serving and rollout infrastructure is where the engineering effort concentrates.

    A gradient step is one forward–backward pass. Once the 400 tokens exist, computing $\log\pi_\theta$ for all of them is a single batched forward pass over the full sequence — every position at once, exactly the parallelism that decoding could not use — plus one backward pass. Compared to 400 sequential decode steps, the update is close to free.

    The asymmetry is the whole motivation. You pay a large, sequential, hard-to-parallelise cost to generate a batch, and a small, fully-parallel cost to learn from it. Using that batch for exactly one update, then discarding it, wastes the expensive half of the loop. PPO's first move — before any clipping — is to squeeze several updates out of each batch. Everything in this post exists to make that safe.

    The Stale-Batch Problem

    Reusing a batch sounds harmless until you re-read the policy gradient. In Part 3 the estimator was an expectation over trajectories drawn from the policy being differentiated:

    $$\nabla_\theta J(\theta) \;=\; \mathbb{E}_{\tau\sim\pi_\theta}\!\big[\nabla_\theta\log\pi_\theta(a_t\mid s_t)\,\hat{A}_t\big].$$

    The subscript $\tau\sim\pi_\theta$ is load-bearing. The Monte Carlo estimate — average the bracketed quantity over your sampled tokens — is only valid because those tokens came from $\pi_\theta$. Take one update, and the parameters are now $\theta'$, but the stored tokens still came from $\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}$. Average over them again as if nothing happened and you are estimating an expectation under the old distribution while pretending it is the new one. That is not a small numerical slip; it is silently optimising a different objective, and it gets worse with every extra epoch you squeeze out of the stale data.

    So we want two contradictory things: to reuse $\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}$'s samples for several updates of $\pi_\theta$, and to keep computing an honest expectation under $\pi_\theta$. Importance sampling is exactly the tool that reconciles them.

    Importance Sampling, Derived

    The identity is one line and worth deriving rather than quoting, because the derivation tells you precisely where it can go wrong. Take any function $f$ and any two distributions $p$ and $q$ with the single requirement that $q(x)\gt 0$ wherever $p(x)f(x)\neq 0$. Then multiply and divide the summand by $q(x)$:

    $$\mathbb{E}_{x\sim p}[f(x)] \;=\; \sum_x p(x)\,f(x) \;=\; \sum_x q(x)\,\frac{p(x)}{q(x)}\,f(x) \;=\; \mathbb{E}_{x\sim q}\!\left[\frac{p(x)}{q(x)}\,f(x)\right].$$

    That is the entire trick. The middle step is algebra — inserting $q(x)/q(x)=1$ — and the outer steps are just the definition of an expectation read left-to-right and then right-to-left. The factor $p(x)/q(x)$ is the importance weight: it re-weights each sample drawn from $q$ so that the average comes out as if the samples had been drawn from $p$. A sample that is twice as likely under $p$ as under $q$ counts double; one that is half as likely counts half. Nothing is approximated, so the identity is exact. The catch, which we return to, is hiding in that divide-by-$q$.

    The Surrogate Objective

    Now specialise. We want an expectation under $\pi_\theta$ but only have samples from $\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}$, so set $p=\pi_\theta$ and $q=\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}$. For the per-token objective, with the sampled token $a_t$ playing the role of $x$:

    $$\mathbb{E}_{a_t\sim\pi_\theta}\!\big[\hat{A}_t\big] \;=\; \mathbb{E}_{a_t\sim\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}}\!\left[\frac{\pi_\theta(a_t\mid s_t)}{\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}(a_t\mid s_t)}\,\hat{A}_t\right].$$

    The importance weight here has a name and a symbol — it is the probability ratio $r_t(\theta)$, always written with its argument to keep it distinct from the reward $r_t$:

    $$r_t(\theta) \;=\; \frac{\pi_\theta(a_t\mid s_t)}{\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}(a_t\mid s_t)}, \qquad L(\theta) \;=\; \hat{\mathbb{E}}_t\big[\,r_t(\theta)\,\hat{A}_t\,\big].$$

    This is the surrogate objective, and it is the sixth milestone in the running construction. Where Part 6 had $-\log\pi_\theta(a_t\mid s_t)\,\hat{A}_t$, the log-probability is gone and a plain ratio has taken its place:

    $$\underbrace{-\log\pi_\theta(a_t\mid s_t)\,\hat{A}_t}_{\text{Part 6, on-policy}} \;\longrightarrow\; \underbrace{-\,r_t(\theta)\,\hat{A}_t}_{\text{Part 7, off-policy}}.$$

    The word surrogate is doing honest work: $L(\theta)$ is a stand-in objective we maximise over several inner epochs while holding the sampled data and the advantages fixed, precisely because it stays defined as $\theta$ moves away from $\theta_{\text{old}}$. But swapping a log for a ratio should make you suspicious — the policy gradient we spent Part 3 deriving had a gradient of a log in it. Where did that go?

    Where the Log Went

    This is the step readers get stuck on, so treat it carefully. The two forms look different — one carries $\nabla_\theta\log\pi_\theta$, the other a bare ratio — yet they must agree at the point where the data was collected, because at that point nothing has been reused yet. Evaluate the gradient of the ratio at $\theta=\theta_{\text{old}}$. The denominator $\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}$ is a constant with respect to $\theta$ (it is a fixed snapshot), so

    $$\nabla_\theta\, r_t(\theta)\Big|_{\theta_{\text{old}}} \;=\; \frac{\nabla_\theta\,\pi_\theta(a_t\mid s_t)}{\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}(a_t\mid s_t)}\bigg|_{\theta_{\text{old}}} \;=\; \frac{\nabla_\theta\,\pi_\theta(a_t\mid s_t)}{\pi_\theta(a_t\mid s_t)}\bigg|_{\theta_{\text{old}}} \;=\; \nabla_\theta\log\pi_\theta(a_t\mid s_t)\Big|_{\theta_{\text{old}}}.$$

    The middle equality holds because at $\theta=\theta_{\text{old}}$ the numerator's policy and the denominator's snapshot are the same distribution, so $\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}=\pi_\theta$ there. The last equality is just the definition of the log-derivative, $\nabla\log u=\nabla u/u$. So the surrogate has exactly the same gradient as the policy gradient at the collection point, and differs from it only as $\theta$ drifts away. That is the property you want and could not have designed better by hand: near $\theta_{\text{old}}$ the surrogate is the policy gradient, and further out it is a smooth extrapolation that stays well-defined instead of becoming undefined. The log did not disappear; it is the ratio's behaviour at the one point where the two must coincide.

    Computing the Ratio in Practice

    Three implementation facts fall out of this and are worth pinning down, because getting any of them wrong produces a loss that looks plausible and trains to nonsense.

    • Compute the ratio in log-space. Never form $\pi_\theta/\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}$ as a quotient of probabilities — token probabilities routinely underflow, and dividing two tiny floats is exactly where precision dies. Store and subtract log-probabilities, then exponentiate once: $r_t(\theta)=\exp\!\big(\log\pi_\theta-\log\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}\big)$.
    • The old log-probs are data, not parameters. $\log\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}$ is computed during rollout and stored alongside the tokens. It must be detach()ed — gradients flow only through the numerator $\pi_\theta$. If you let gradient leak into the denominator you are differentiating your own baseline.
    • At the first inner epoch the ratio is identically one. Before any update, $\pi_\theta=\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}$ token-for-token, so every $r_t(\theta)=1$. This is the single best assertion to put in a test: if the first-epoch ratio is not all ones, your old and new log-probs are misaligned (wrong tokens gathered, an off-by-one on the prompt mask, or a sampling temperature you forgot to apply on one side).

    The old log-probs are collected during the rollout itself, inside a no-grad context, which is what makes them detached by construction — there is no graph to detach from. You record the log-probability of each token at the moment you sample it:

    @torch.no_grad()
def rollout(policy, prompts, max_new_tokens):
    # generate responses AND record the log-prob of every sampled token
    # under the CURRENT policy -- this snapshot is pi_theta_old
    tokens, old_logprobs = [], []
    state = prompts
    for _ in range(max_new_tokens):
        logits = policy(state)[:, -1, :]            # (B, V) next-token scores
        dist = torch.distributions.Categorical(logits=logits)
        a = dist.sample()                           # (B,) one token per row
        old_logprobs.append(dist.log_prob(a))       # (B,) logprob of the pick
        tokens.append(a)
        state = torch.cat([state, a.unsqueeze(1)], dim=1)
    # no grad was tracked, so these come out already detached
    return torch.stack(tokens, 1), torch.stack(old_logprobs, 1)   # (B, T), (B, T)

    Later, during the update, the same tokens are re-scored by the live policy — this time with gradients — and the two log-prob tensors are subtracted to form the ratio:

    import torch
import torch.nn.functional as F

def token_logprobs(logits, actions):
    # logits:  (B, T, V) scores from a policy over the response positions
    # actions: (B, T)    the tokens actually emitted during the rollout
    logp = F.log_softmax(logits, dim=-1)                     # (B, T, V)
    return logp.gather(-1, actions.unsqueeze(-1)).squeeze(-1)  # (B, T)

def surrogate_loss(new_logits, old_logprobs, actions, mask, advantages):
    # old_logprobs : (B, T) stored at rollout time, ALREADY detached
    # advantages   : (B, T) from GAE, detached (critic is trained separately)
    # mask         : (B, T) 1.0 on real response tokens, 0.0 on prompt / pad
    new_logprobs = token_logprobs(new_logits, actions)      # (B, T), needs grad

    # r_t(theta) = pi_theta / pi_theta_old, formed in log-space for stability
    ratio = torch.exp(new_logprobs - old_logprobs)          # (B, T)

    # unclipped surrogate  r_t * A_t   (Part 8 will wrap this in a clip)
    per_token = ratio * advantages                          # (B, T)

    # average only over real tokens; a masked mean, not a plain mean
    loss = -(per_token * mask).sum() / mask.sum().clamp(min=1.0)
    return loss, ratio

# cheap, decisive test for the whole pipeline:
#   at inner epoch 0 the policy has not moved, so every ratio must be 1
first_epoch_ratio = torch.exp(old_logprobs - old_logprobs)  # exactly ones
assert torch.allclose(first_epoch_ratio, torch.ones_like(first_epoch_ratio))

    Every shape is annotated because the bugs live in the shapes: a ratio computed against the wrong axis, or a mask that includes the prompt, will not raise an error — it will just quietly learn the wrong thing.

    The Danger: When Importance Weights Explode

    Importance sampling is unbiased in expectation. Its variance is another story, and the divide-by-$q$ we flagged earlier is the culprit. When $p$ and $q$ are close the weights sit near one and everything is calm. When they diverge, the weights can become enormous, and a single sample with a huge weight can dominate the entire batch average — an estimator that is correct on average but useless on any particular batch.

    Concretely: suppose a token had probability $0.001$ under $\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}$ — something the old policy almost never said — and after a couple of updates the new policy assigns it $0.05$. The ratio is

    $$r_t(\theta) \;=\; \frac{0.05}{0.001} \;=\; 50.$$

    One token, weight fifty. If its advantage is positive, that single token pushes on the gradient as hard as fifty ordinary tokens, off the strength of a probability estimate that was barely resolved to begin with. The rare-token direction is exactly where the old policy gave you the least data and the new policy is most likely to have swung, so this is not a corner case — it is the generic failure of unconstrained off-policy reuse.

    A Worked Batch of Five Tokens

    Numbers make the failure concrete. Below is a five-token batch with stored old log-probs, freshly computed new log-probs, the resulting ratio $r_t(\theta)=\exp(\log\pi_\theta-\log\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}})$, the GAE advantage, and the per-token surrogate contribution $r_t(\theta)\,\hat{A}_t$. Four tokens are ordinary; token 5 is the rare one that the new policy suddenly likes.

    token$\log\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}$$\log\pi_\theta$$r_t(\theta)$$\hat{A}_t$$r_t(\theta)\,\hat{A}_t$

    2−1.20−1.101.11−0.30−0.33
    3−0.92−0.851.07+0.20+0.21
    4−2.30−2.201.11+0.40+0.44
    5−6.91−3.0049.9+0.60+29.9

    Token 5 had probability $e^{-6.91}\approx0.001$ under the old policy and $e^{-3.00}\approx0.050$ under the new one — the exact $50\times$ ratio from above. The mean surrogate over the batch is $(0.45-0.33+0.21+0.44+29.9)/5\approx6.13$, of which token 5 alone supplies $29.9$. A single sample is contributing about $97\%$ of the batch signal, and it is the sample the old policy sampled least reliably. Your gradient is now a rumour about one rare token wearing the disguise of a batch average.

    This is the exact problem PPO's clipping solves. Do not try to fix the variance with a bigger batch or a smaller learning rate — those only postpone it. The structural fix is to refuse to trust the ratio once it leaves a neighbourhood of one, which is precisely what the clipped surrogate objective does. We have earned the ratio; the next post earns the clip.

    Where This Leaves Us

    The objective now reuses each expensive batch for several cheap updates by re-weighting stale samples with $r_t(\theta)$, and the reuse is exact in expectation with the same gradient as the policy gradient at $\theta_{\text{old}}$:

    $$L(\theta) \;=\; \hat{\mathbb{E}}_t\big[\,r_t(\theta)\,\hat{A}_t\,\big], \qquad r_t(\theta)=\frac{\pi_\theta(a_t\mid s_t)}{\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}(a_t\mid s_t)}.$$

    But it is a loaded weapon: the moment $\pi_\theta$ wanders far from $\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}$, the importance weights explode and the estimate is dominated by its rarest samples. The next post, The Clipped Surrogate Objective, turns that observation into the single design decision that defines PPO — capping the incentive to let the ratio run away.

    Practice

    • Derive the importance-sampling identity yourself for a continuous variable, replacing the sum with an integral, and write down the precise condition on $q$ that the derivation needs. derivation the support condition is the whole safety story.
    • Implement surrogate_loss above and add the first-epoch assertion. Then deliberately shift one token's new log-prob and confirm its ratio, and its share of the batch mean, both blow up. exercise reproduce the five-token table.
    • Show algebraically that $\nabla_\theta r_t(\theta)\big|_{\theta_{\text{old}}}=\nabla_\theta\log\pi_\theta(a_t\mid s_t)\big|_{\theta_{\text{old}}}$ and explain in one sentence why the surrogate and the policy gradient must agree there. derivation the collection point is special.
    • Estimate, for a model you know, the ratio of decode FLOPs to one optimisation-step FLOPs for a 400-token response, and use it to argue how many inner epochs are worth taking. estimate the economics justify the reuse.
