    Deriving DPO: Solve for the Policy, Then Eliminate the Reward

    September 2026 · 5 min read + experiments

    Work through the KL-regularized optimum, the partition function, and the reward-to-policy substitution with a three-response example.

    See the ideaRead the explanationTry the calculation

    State the optimization problem precisely

    Fix a prompt $x$ and temporarily write $q(y)=\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}(y\mid x)$. Assume a finite response space with $q(y)>0$, a finite reward $r(y)$, and $\beta>0$. We want a normalized distribution $\pi$ maximizing

    $$F(\pi)=\sum_y\pi(y)r(y)-\beta\sum_y\pi(y)\log\frac{\pi(y)}{q(y)}.$$

    The KL direction is policy to reference: the expectation is under $\pi$. The coefficient $\beta$ trades reward against departure from $q$. This is a penalized objective, not a hard bound that forbids a specified KL value. For an infinite response space, the exponential normalization below must also be finite. The finite case lets us inspect every assumption without that additional complication.

    Use normalization to solve for the optimum

    Introduce a multiplier $\eta$ for $\sum_y\pi(y)=1$. Differentiating the objective plus $\eta(\sum_y\pi(y)-1)$ with respect to an interior probability gives $r(y)-\beta[\log(\pi(y)/q(y))+1]+\eta=0$. Rearranging says that the probability is proportional to $q(y)e^{r(y)/\beta}$. Normalizing gives

    $$\pi^*(y\mid x)=\frac{\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}(y\mid x)e^{r(x,y)/\beta}}{Z(x)},\qquad Z(x)=\sum_y\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}(y\mid x)e^{r(x,y)/\beta}.$$

    We can verify optimality without relying only on a stationary point. Substitute $\log\pi^*=\log q+r/\beta-\log Z$ into $D_{\mathrm{KL}}(\pi\Vert\pi^*)$ and rearrange:

    $$F(\pi)=\beta\log Z-\beta D_{\mathrm{KL}}(\pi\Vert\pi^*).$$

    The first term is constant with respect to $\pi$, and KL is nonnegative. Therefore $\pi^*$ maximizes $F$. This identity will become a numerical check in the lab. It also shows that a policy can have higher raw reward yet a worse regularized objective if its KL cost is too large.

    Calculate the distribution for three possible responses

    Let reference probabilities be $(0.5,0.3,0.2)$ for A, B, C and utilities $(0,1,-0.5)$. At $\beta=1$, unnormalized weights are $(0.5,0.3e,0.2e^{-0.5})$, which sum to $Z\approx1.4368$. The normalized optimum is approximately $(0.3480,0.5676,0.0844)$. The best response becomes more likely, while the reference still affects how probability is distributed.

    For this fixed reward, larger $\beta$ pulls the optimum toward the reference; as $\beta\to\infty$, $\pi^*\to q$. As $\beta\to0^+$, probability concentrates on reward maximizers within the reference support, with ties weighted by the reference. This is a statement about the exact fixed-reward optimum. It is not a promise that increasing $\beta$ makes every finite neural-network optimizer step smaller.

    Why the partition function disappears

    Take logs of the optimum and solve for reward:

    $$r(x,y)=\beta\log\frac{\pi^*(y\mid x)}{\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}(y\mid x)}+\beta\log Z(x).$$

    For two responses to the same prompt, subtracting rewards removes $\beta\log Z(x)$. Inserting the remaining difference into the Bradley–Terry model yields a preference probability written entirely in terms of policy and reference likelihoods. Now replace the unknown optimal policy with a trainable $\pi_\theta$ and maximize the likelihood of observed comparisons. This substitution is the defining construction in the DPO paper. DPO, Sections 3–5.

    $$\mathcal L_{\mathrm{DPO}}=-\mathbb E_{(x,y_w,y_l)}\log\sigma\!\left(\beta\left[\log\frac{\pi_\theta(y_w\mid x)}{\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}(y_w\mid x)}-\log\frac{\pi_\theta(y_l\mid x)}{\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}(y_l\mid x)}\right]\right).$$

    What “the policy is a reward model” means

    Define the implicit score $\hat r_\theta(x,y)=\beta\log[\pi_\theta(y\mid x)/\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}(y\mid x)]$. It can model reward differences in preference comparisons. It is not the raw sequence log probability, and it is not a calibrated absolute measure of truthfulness or usefulness. Adding any prompt-only constant to reward leaves pairwise preferences and the optimal policy unchanged.

    For a fixed reference and positive normalized policy, the implicit reward has a convenient normalization: $\sum_y q(y)e^{\hat r_\theta(y)/\beta}=\sum_y\pi_\theta(y)=1$. It chooses one representative from the class of rewards differing by prompt-only offsets. Comparisons cannot recover those offsets, so reporting them as independently meaningful rewards would be misleading.

    The algebra is exact; the learned solution has additional conditions

    The derivation does not establish that a finite dataset and arbitrary neural policy recover a human utility function. That would additionally depend on the preference model, coverage, realizability, and successful optimization. When the data only compares A and B, it may say nothing about C. The coverage experiment makes that ambiguity visible.

    There is also no need to estimate $Z(x)$ in a DPO minibatch. You compute four sequence log probabilities, form two reference-relative gains, and take their difference. The next chapter explains the signs and gradients of that computation.

    Check your understanding: If every reward for a prompt increases by 10, does the optimal policy change?

    No. Every unnormalized weight is multiplied by $e^{10/\beta}$, and the same factor appears in Z. It cancels on normalization. The lab checks this invariance numerically.
