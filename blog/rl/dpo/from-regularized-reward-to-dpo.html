    Deriving DPO: Solve for the Policy, Then Eliminate the Reward

    September 2026 · 6 min read + experiments

    Work through the KL-regularized optimum, the partition function, and the reward-to-policy substitution with a three-response example.

    Read the explanationTry the calculation

    Prefer better answers while staying near a reference

    Chapter 1 explained how a difference in answer quality predicts a preference. We now connect that difference to a language model’s probabilities. Work with one fixed prompt and a small set of possible responses, so the normalization is visible.

    Write $q(y)$ for the fixed reference probability of response $y$, $\pi(y)$ for a candidate policy probability, and $r(y)$ for a hypothetical reward. Assume reference probabilities are positive. We want high expected reward, but also charge for moving away from the reference:

    $$F(\pi)=\underbrace{\sum_y\pi(y)r(y)}_{\text{expected reward}}-\beta\underbrace{D_{\mathrm{KL}}(\pi\Vert q)}_{\text{departure from reference}}.$$

    $F$ is the score to maximize. The positive coefficient $\beta$ sets the price of departure. Here KL means the policy-weighted average log ratio, $\sum_y\pi(y)\log[\pi(y)/q(y)]$. It is zero when the distributions match and nonnegative otherwise.

    The optimal policy reweights the reference

    For this objective, the optimal policy multiplies each reference probability by $e^{r(y)/\beta}$. Higher reward gives a larger multiplier. Call the sum of these weights $Z$; dividing by it makes a valid probability distribution:

    $$\begin{aligned}Z&=\sum_y q(y)e^{r(y)/\beta},\\\pi^*(y)&=\frac{q(y)e^{r(y)/\beta}}{Z}.\end{aligned}$$

    The star denotes the optimum for the assumed rewards. Why is it optimal? Substituting this expression into the objective gives the following identity:

    $$F(\pi)=\beta\log Z-\beta D_{\mathrm{KL}}(\pi\Vert\pi^*).$$

    The first term is fixed once the rewards and reference are fixed. The second can only subtract a nonnegative amount, and becomes zero at $\pi=\pi^*$. That proves the optimum. The DPO paper, Section 4 and Appendix A.1, gives the corresponding derivation.

    Watch reward and reference compete

    The example below has three answers, A, B, and C. Their reference probabilities are 0.5, 0.3, and 0.2. Their rewards start at 0, 1, and −0.5. At $\beta=1$, reweighting makes B the most likely answer even though A was the reference favorite.

    Increase $\beta$ while holding the rewards fixed: leaving the reference costs more, so the optimum moves toward it. This statement concerns the exact objective above. The behavior of a finite training run also depends on its optimizer and data.

    Comparing two answers removes the unknown normalizer

    Restore the prompt $x$ in the notation: each prompt has its own normalizer $Z(x)$. Take the logarithm of the optimal policy and rearrange to express the reward:

    $$r(x,y)=\beta\log\frac{\pi^*(y\mid x)}{\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}(y\mid x)}+\beta\log Z(x).$$

    For two answers to the same prompt, the last term is identical. Subtracting the rejected answer’s reward from the chosen answer’s reward removes it. We therefore need only two policy-to-reference log ratios.

    DPO uses a trainable policy $\pi_\theta$ in place of the unknown optimum. Let $y_w$ be the chosen answer and $y_l$ the rejected answer. Their preference logit is:

    $$m_\theta=\beta\left[\log\frac{\pi_\theta(y_w\mid x)}{\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}(y_w\mid x)}-\log\frac{\pi_\theta(y_l\mid x)}{\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}(y_l\mid x)}\right].$$

    We already know how to train a chosen-first comparison: minimize $-\log\sigma(m_\theta)$ and average over the dataset. This is the DPO loss. There is no need to calculate $Z(x)$ or sum over every possible generated answer during training.

    The policy supplies a comparison score

    The expression $\beta\log[\pi_\theta(y\mid x)/\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}(y\mid x)]$ acts as an implicit reward score. It measures a response relative to the reference. It is not an absolute score for truth or usefulness; only differences between same-prompt answers enter this loss.

    What the derivation gives us

    The algebra gives a trainable preference objective. It does not guarantee that limited or misleading labels recover the behavior we want. The data chapter examines that limitation. First, chapter 3 turns the two log ratios into a concrete loss calculation.
