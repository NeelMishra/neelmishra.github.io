    DPO: From Preference Pairs to a Policy You Can Explain

    September 2026 · 3 min read

    An original visual guide to Direct Preference Optimization: derivation, gradients, token-level implementation, data diagnostics, a runnable PyTorch lab, and the connection to PPO.

    Read the explanation

    Learn from “this answer was better”

    Direct Preference Optimization (DPO) trains a language model from chosen/rejected answer pairs. This series starts with one such comparison, explains the role of a fixed reference model, and then shows how to implement and evaluate the loss.

    If you have read the policy-gradient chapter, you already know how log probabilities connect to an update. DPO uses a different training signal. You can follow its first three chapters with conditional probabilities, logarithms, and basic derivatives; PyTorch shapes enter in chapter 4.

    Seven chapters with a clear stopping point

    Read chapters 1–3 to understand the method. Continue with 4–6 when you want to implement it. Chapter 7 compares the complete DPO and PPO workflows.

    Choose the depth you need today

    For a first pass, stop when you can explain the four scores in the margin example. For implementation, keep the token-scoring chapter open beside the code. For a disappointing training run, begin with data and diagnostics.

    Introduce symbols only when they are needed

    The first chapters use $x$ for a prompt and $y$ for a response. A policy probability $\pi(y\mid x)$ means the chance of that response given the prompt. Token positions and batch shapes appear only when we reach implementation.

    Use one calculation to check each new idea

    The figures let you change a preference score, reference penalty, or token-scoring choice and observe the result. The lab adds recorded training runs in a small response space with a known optimum. Its results illustrate the method; they are not language-model benchmark scores.

    Start with standard fixed-reference DPO

    Here DPO means the sigmoid preference loss, a fixed reference, and an existing pair dataset. Token-length normalization, changing the reference during training, and alternative preference losses are separate choices. The chapters identify the choices that affect their examples.

    The primary sources are Rafailov et al.’s DPO paper and the authors’ implementation. For trainer features and configuration, consult TRL’s documentation. References also appear beside the relevant explanations.
