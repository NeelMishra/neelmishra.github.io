    DPO Starts with a Comparison, Not a Reward Number

    September 2026 · 10 min read + experiments

    Understand preference pairs, the Bradley–Terry model, and the offline learning problem before assembling the DPO equation.

    Read the explanationTry the calculation

    What problem remains after supervised fine-tuning?

    A supervised fine-tuning example says, “For this prompt, imitate this answer.” It need not explain why another plausible answer is worse. Preference data adds that comparison. For example, two explanations can both be fluent, but one names the actual cause and the other repeats a misconception. Training only on the chosen text and training on the preference pair are different objectives.

    Direct Preference Optimization, or DPO, trains a policy using such pairs and a reference policy. The standard version studied here uses an offline dataset: the responses and labels already exist when an optimizer step begins. Its loss is a binary classification loss whose score is built from policy-to-reference log-likelihood ratios. The connection to KL-regularized reward optimization explains why this particular score appears. Rafailov et al., Direct Preference Optimization.

    If you have read the PPO series, keep the notion of a policy and a fixed reference model. Set aside rollout advantages, GAE, and the old-policy clipping ratio for now. They are not ingredients of the canonical DPO loss. We will compare the complete workflows in the final chapter rather than repeatedly re-deriving PPO.

    The first distinctionPPO asks, “What happened on this rollout?”DPO asks, “Which of these two answers should win?”

    That change in evidence determines the rest of the reading path: pairs first, policy-to-reference scores next, tokens after that.

    A dataset row has three essential fields

    {
  "prompt": "Explain why seasons change.",
  "chosen": "Earth's tilted axis changes sunlight angle and day length.",
  "rejected": "Summer happens because Earth is much closer to the Sun."
}

    Both responses must answer the same serialized prompt. In chat data, that means the same earlier messages, roles, and relevant context. “Chosen” means preferred under the labelling process. It does not necessarily mean factually correct, safe in every setting, or the best possible response. A judge can prefer the less bad of two poor answers.

    Store the rubric, label source, generation settings, and any repeated judgments when available. These help distinguish an optimization failure from a dataset that rewards the wrong behavior. A text-only chosen/rejected schema is enough to compute the loss; it is not enough to explain the provenance of the preference.

    Turn a reward difference into a preference probability

    Suppose a hidden utility function $r(x,y)$ assigns a score to response $y$ for prompt $x$. A Bradley–Terry model defines

    $$P(y_A\succ y_B\mid x)=\frac{e^{r(x,y_A)}}{e^{r(x,y_A)}+e^{r(x,y_B)}}=\sigma\!\left(r(x,y_A)-r(x,y_B)\right),$$

    where $\sigma(u)=1/(1+e^{-u})$ is the sigmoid function. Only the difference matters. Equal utilities predict a 50–50 comparison. A utility difference of $\log 3\approx1.099$ predicts that A wins with probability 0.75. The utility scale here absorbs the preference model’s temperature; multiplying all utilities changes the predicted confidence.

    This is a modelling assumption about judgments. It need not describe every annotator or every task. A single scalar utility cannot exactly express a deterministic preference cycle A over B, B over C, and C over A. Disagreement may reflect noise, multiple legitimate rubrics, or missing context. The loss still runs in those cases, but its fitted score should not be mistaken for a discovered universal ordering.

    One label does not reveal an exact reward gap

    If an annotator selects A once, we cannot conclude that its utility exceeds B’s by exactly 1. If 75 of 100 comparable judgments select A, the empirical win frequency estimates 0.75, subject to sampling uncertainty and the assumptions of the collection process. Under the model, the corresponding fitted gap is $\log(0.75/0.25)=\log3$.

    Separate the model’s prediction from the observed label

    Fix one prompt $x$ and store its responses in the order $(y_A,y_B)$. Here A means first in the pair and B means second; either response can be preferred. The model produces a score difference, called the preference logit:

    $$m=r(x,y_A)-r(x,y_B).$$

    The logit can be any real number. A positive $m$ favors the first response, zero gives equal preference probabilities, and a negative $m$ favors the second. Applying the sigmoid converts this score difference into a probability. Write $q$ for the predicted probability that the first response wins:

    $$\begin{aligned} q &= P(y_A\succ y_B\mid x)=\sigma(m), \\ 1-q &= P(y_B\succ y_A\mid x)=\sigma(-m). \end{aligned}$$

    The second line uses the identity $1-\sigma(m)=\sigma(-m)$. We are modelling a binary comparison with no separate tie outcome.

    The annotator supplies a different quantity: the observed label $z$. It tells us which response was actually selected in this row:

    $$z=\begin{cases} 1 & \text{if the first response was preferred}, \\ 0 & \text{if the second response was preferred}. \end{cases}$$

    $m$ and $q$ are predictions; $z$ is the training target. For example, the model might predict $q=0.75$ while the annotator selects the second response, giving $z=0$. The label 0 means “second wins”; it does not mean that there was no preference or that the reward was zero.

    Score the probability assigned to the observed choice

    If $z=1$, the model assigned probability $q$ to what happened. If $z=0$, it assigned probability $1-q$. The likelihood of this observed label means precisely that probability, viewed as a function of the model’s prediction. One expression covers both cases:

    $$P(z\mid m)=q^z(1-q)^{1-z}.$$

    The exponents select the relevant factor. Substituting $z=1$ gives $q^1(1-q)^0=q$; substituting $z=0$ gives $q^0(1-q)^1=1-q$. We want the model to assign more probability to the observed choice. Since an optimizer minimizes a loss, we take the negative natural logarithm of that probability:

    $$\begin{aligned} \ell(m,z) &= -\log P(z\mid m) \\ &= -z\log q-(1-z)\log(1-q) \\ &= -z\log\sigma(m)-(1-z)\log\sigma(-m). \end{aligned}$$

    Here $\ell$ is the loss for one comparison. A larger probability for the observed label gives a smaller loss. Assigning it probability close to 1 gives loss close to 0; assigning it probability close to 0 gives a large penalty. This is the usual binary cross-entropy loss.

    For a concrete calculation, hold the prediction fixed at $m=\log3$, so $q=0.75$. The loss depends on which choice the annotator made:

    $$\begin{aligned} \ell(\log3,1)&=-\log(0.75)\approx0.288, \\ \ell(\log3,0)&=-\log(0.25)\approx1.386. \end{aligned}$$

    The model predicted that the first response was more likely to win, so a first-win label gives a smaller penalty. A second-win label gives a larger penalty because the model assigned that observation only 25% probability. These are two possible labels for the same prediction, not two losses to add for one row.

    Why chosen/rejected datasets need only one term

    A dataset with generic fields response_a and response_b needs a label to identify the preferred response. A dataset with fields chosen and rejected stores that information in the field names: it has already put the preferred response first.

    When we read each row as $(y_{\mathrm{chosen}},y_{\mathrm{rejected}})$, the label is therefore always $z=1$. Define the logit in that same order and substitute 1 into the general loss:

    $$\begin{aligned} m_{\mathrm{cr}} &= r(x,y_{\mathrm{chosen}})-r(x,y_{\mathrm{rejected}}), \\ \ell(m_{\mathrm{cr}},1) &= -1\log\sigma(m_{\mathrm{cr}})-0\log\sigma(-m_{\mathrm{cr}}) \\ &= -\log\sigma(m_{\mathrm{cr}}). \end{aligned}$$

    The label has become implicit in the ordering; it has not been discarded. A target of 1 also does not force the model’s current prediction to be 1. The model may currently score the chosen response lower, giving a negative margin and a large loss. Training uses the observed preference to correct that prediction. This is the chosen/rejected likelihood convention in the original DPO paper’s reward-model formulation, Equation 2.

    DPO later constructs this preference logit from policy-to-reference log-probability ratios. The next chapter derives that score; the binary-label logic we have just worked through stays the same.

    Swapping a pair must preserve who won

    Suppose the annotator preferred the response named A. If the stored order is (A, B), the label is 1 because A is first. If we reorder the same responses to (B, A), A is now second, so the label must become 0. The score difference also changes sign:

    $$m'=-m,\qquad z'=1-z.$$

    With both changes, the likelihood of the annotator’s choice is unchanged. For this first-win example,

    $$\ell(-m,0)=-\log\sigma(-(-m))=-\log\sigma(m)=\ell(m,1).$$

    Here is the same $m=\log3$ example in all three storage arrangements. The model’s scores for the actual responses A and B stay fixed throughout.

    The last row now instructs the model to favor B, reversing the original observation. In chosen/rejected storage, swapping the two response strings while keeping their field names makes this exact mistake: the loss still treats the chosen field as the winner.

    How does the derivative show the direction of the training signal?

    The derivative with respect to the logit is

    $$\frac{\partial\ell}{\partial m}=\sigma(m)-z=q-z.$$

    At $q=0.75$, a first-win label gives derivative $0.75-1=-0.25$. A gradient-descent step on $m$ subtracts that negative number, increasing $m$. A second-win label gives derivative $0.75-0=+0.75$, so descent decreases $m$. These are opposing directions, though their magnitudes differ. The DPO gradient chapter carries this derivative through to the policy scores.

    What “offline” does and does not promise

    An offline DPO step scores existing responses. Generation was needed somewhere to obtain candidates, and it is still needed to evaluate the trained model. The narrower claim is that fresh policy sampling is not required inside this particular optimization loop. DPO can also be used within an iterative process that periodically generates and labels new candidates; that changes the data-collection procedure.

    Offline data only compares what was collected. If every rejected answer is obviously irrelevant, the model may learn to identify that artifact while learning little about subtle mistakes made by its current policy. Conversely, a carefully labelled pair can contain useful information even if its preferred response is not something the starting policy would frequently generate. Coverage and learnability need to be considered together.

    The question that leads to the DPO equation

    A reward-based approach could first fit $r(x,y)$ to comparisons, then optimize a policy against it with a penalty for leaving a reference distribution. DPO asks whether that reward can be expressed through the policy itself. The next chapter solves the regularized policy problem and shows exactly which normalization term disappears in a same-prompt comparison.

    Check your understanding: Does an A-win label mean the model should predict P(A beats B) = 1?

    It is one positive observation, not proof of certainty. Repeated contradictory labels can imply a finite optimum. If every observed label is perfectly separable, the unregularized empirical logistic loss instead keeps rewarding an increasing margin; finite data and early stopping matter.
