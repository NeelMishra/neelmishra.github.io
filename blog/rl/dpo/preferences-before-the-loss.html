    DPO Starts with a Comparison, Not a Reward Number

    September 2026 · 6 min read + experiments

    Understand preference pairs, the Bradley–Terry model, and the offline learning problem before assembling the DPO equation.

    Read the explanationTry the calculation

    Begin with one comparison

    Direct Preference Optimization (DPO) trains a language model from examples of which answer was preferred. Start with that observation. We will introduce the policy and reference-model probabilities in the next chapter; neither PPO clipping nor GAE is needed for this first step.

    The two answers must share the same prompt

    A typical row contains a prompt, a chosen response, and a rejected response:

    {
  "prompt": "Explain why seasons change.",
  "chosen": "Earth's tilted axis changes sunlight angle and day length.",
  "rejected": "Summer happens because Earth is much closer to the Sun."
}

    “Chosen” means preferred according to the dataset’s judging rule. It can be the better of two imperfect answers. Keep the same earlier messages and context for both responses, and retain the judging rubric when available. Otherwise, it becomes hard to tell whether training or the labels caused a problem.

    Model how likely one answer is to win

    Let $x$ be a prompt, $y_A$ and $y_B$ its two candidate answers, and $r(x,y)$ a hypothetical quality score. We do not observe that score directly. The Bradley–Terry model predicts a preference from the difference between the two scores.

    Call that difference $m$, the preference logit. Pass it through the sigmoid function, $\sigma(m)=1/(1+e^{-m})$, to get a probability $q$:

    $$\begin{aligned}m&=r(x,y_A)-r(x,y_B),\\q&=P(y_A\succ y_B\mid x)=\sigma(m).\end{aligned}$$

    The symbol $\succ$ means “is preferred to.” A zero difference predicts a 50–50 choice. A positive difference favors A; a negative difference favors B. This is the preference model used in the DPO paper, Section 3.

    An observation is different from a prediction

    A single A-win does not reveal an exact quality gap. It is one observed choice. People may disagree, and the model’s probability is a way to represent uncertainty about those choices.

    Keep the label separate from the logit

    Store the candidates in the order (A, B). Let the label $z$ be 1 if A was preferred and 0 if B was preferred. The model predicts $q$; the dataset supplies $z$. For example, the model can predict $q=0.75$ while the observed label is $z=0$. That means it favored A, but the judge chose B.

    Penalize the probability assigned to the observed choice

    If A wins, the model assigned probability $q$ to what happened. If B wins, it assigned probability $1-q$. Training should increase that probability. We minimize its negative logarithm, called the negative log likelihood:

    One expression covers both rows. The label turns on the appropriate term, and $1-q=\sigma(-m)$ lets us write it directly using the logit:

    $$\ell(m,z)=-z\log\sigma(m)-(1-z)\log\sigma(-m).$$

    For the prediction $q=0.75$, an A-win gives loss $-\log(0.75)\approx0.288$. A B-win gives the larger loss $-\log(0.25)\approx1.386$. These are alternative labels for one row, not two losses to add.

    Chosen/rejected storage makes the label implicit

    When the preferred answer is always stored first, the label is always 1. Define $m$ in that same chosen-minus-rejected order. Substituting $z=1$ removes the second term:

    $$\ell(m,1)=-\log\sigma(m).$$

    The model can still assign the chosen answer a lower score. In that case $m$ is negative and the loss is large. The label describes the training target, not the model’s current belief. Move the score difference in the figure and watch the probability and loss change:

    Changing order must preserve the winner

    If A won, (A, B) needs label 1 and (B, A) needs label 0. Both describe the same observation. Swapping the answers without changing the label says B won instead. In a chosen/rejected dataset, the field names already encode the label, so swapping their contents reverses the training signal.

    The gradient chapter will explain how the loss changes policy probabilities. For now, the important connection is simply: score the probability of the choice that was actually observed.

    What offline training means here

    A standard DPO step scores response pairs already in the dataset. It does not need to generate new responses inside that step. Generation was needed to collect candidates, and is still needed to evaluate the trained model. Data quality and coverage matter because the loss only sees the comparisons supplied to it.

    Where will the quality scores come from?

    We introduced hypothetical scores to explain preferences. DPO’s next step is to express their difference using the trainable policy and a fixed reference model. Chapter 2 builds that connection, so we can train directly from pairs without fitting a separate reward model first.
