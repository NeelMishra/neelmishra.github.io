    Read the DPO Loss: Margins, Gradients, and the Two Roles of β

    September 2026 · 5 min read + experiments

    Calculate one preference update, understand the reference-relative margin, and distinguish relative improvement from absolute response likelihood.

    Read the explanationTry the calculation

    Calculate one pair from four scores

    Chapter 2 produced a comparison between two policy-to-reference log ratios. To calculate it, score the chosen and rejected responses under both models. Each score below is the natural log probability of a whole response given its prompt:

    QuantityChosen responseRejected response
    Current log probability−4−7
    Reference log probability−6−6
    Current minus reference+2−1

    Subtract reference from current to obtain each response’s gain. Call them $g_w$ for chosen and $g_l$ for rejected. The table gives gains +2 and −1. Multiply their difference by the positive scale $\beta$ to obtain the preference logit $m$, then apply the chosen-first loss:

    $$m=\beta(g_w-g_l),\qquad L=-\log\sigma(m).$$

    At $\beta=0.2$, the logit is $0.2[2-(-1)]=0.6$. The predicted preference probability is about 0.646, and the loss is about 0.4375. This preference probability is not the chance of generating the chosen answer: that answer’s generation probability is $e^{-4}\approx0.0183$.

    The comparison measures change from the reference

    Suppose the reference favors chosen over rejected nine to one, while the trainable policy favors it six to one. Chosen is still more likely, but its relative advantage has weakened. DPO’s gain difference is negative in this case.

    When policy and reference are identical, both gains are zero. Every pair therefore starts with $m=0$, preference probability 0.5, and loss approximately 0.6931. This is a useful check before training; a zero margin does not mean a zero gradient.

    Incorrect comparisons receive a stronger push

    For gradient descent, lowering the loss encourages the chosen gain to rise and the rejected gain to fall. The strength depends on how confidently the model already predicts the observed preference. Differentiating the loss gives a positive weight $c=\beta\sigma(-m)$:

    $$\frac{\partial L}{\partial g_w}=-c,\qquad \frac{\partial L}{\partial g_l}=+c.$$

    Descent subtracts a derivative, so the negative derivative encourages an increase in the chosen score. A strongly negative margin receives a large weight; a confidently positive margin receives a small one. For the table’s example, $c\approx0.0709$. The full policy-parameter gradient follows by the chain rule, as shown in the DPO paper’s loss-gradient analysis.

    Separate the objective from the size of one step

    In the fixed-reward derivation, larger $\beta$ makes departure from the reference more expensive. Inside this loss, it also changes the margin and the gradient weight. At initialization, $m=0$ and the weight is $\beta/2$.

    So increasing $\beta$ does not automatically make every optimizer step smaller. Compare settings using the same data, learning rate, and token-scoring convention, then measure the generated answers and reference drift.

    A better ratio does not guarantee a higher chosen probability

    Imagine the chosen probability falls from 0.30 to 0.15, while the rejected probability falls from 0.20 to 0.05. Chosen-to-rejected odds improve from 1.5 to 3, although the chosen answer itself becomes less likely. A third answer can receive the probability that both lost.

    With a fixed reference, this increases the DPO margin and lowers the pair loss. It illustrates what a relative objective can permit; it is not a prediction of one isolated optimizer step. Track chosen and rejected likelihoods separately, and evaluate actual generations.

    Use a stable version of the loss

    The code mirrors the calculation. softplus(-margin) is a numerically stable way to compute the negative log sigmoid. Each input has one whole-response score per pair; the final mean averages pairs:

    gain_w = policy_chosen_logp - reference_chosen_logp.detach()
gain_l = policy_rejected_logp - reference_rejected_logp.detach()
margin = beta * (gain_w - gain_l)
loss = torch.nn.functional.softplus(-margin).mean()

    The authors’ implementation uses the equivalent -logsigmoid form. The reference remains fixed. Next, we compute those response scores from the correct token positions.
