    Read the DPO Loss: Margins, Gradients, and the Two Roles of β

    September 2026 · 5 min read + experiments

    Calculate one preference update, understand the reference-relative margin, and distinguish relative improvement from absolute response likelihood.

    Read the explanationTry the calculation

    Keep the four scores in a small table

    QuantityChosen responseRejected response
    Current log probability−4−7
    Reference log probability−6−6
    Current minus reference+2−1

    All logarithms are natural logs. The reference-relative difference is $\Delta=(\ell_w-\ell_w^{\mathrm{ref}})-(\ell_l-\ell_l^{\mathrm{ref}})$. The preference logit is $m=\beta\Delta$, and the per-pair loss is $L=-\log\sigma(m)=\operatorname{softplus}(-m)$. For this table with $\beta=0.2$, the loss is about 0.437488.

    Do not confuse $\sigma(m)$ with the language model’s probability of generating the chosen response. Here that generation probability is $e^{-4}\approx0.0183$, while the modelled preference probability is about 0.6457. They are distributions over different outcomes: one over responses, the other over the result of a comparison.

    Why subtract the reference margin?

    DPO asks how the policy’s relative odds changed from the reference. Consider a reference that already prefers the chosen response nine to one. A current policy with odds six to one still assigns the chosen response higher absolute probability, but it has reduced the preference relative to the anchor. Its reference-relative margin is negative: $\log6-\log9=\log(2/3)$ before multiplying by $\beta$.

    At initialization, if the policy is exactly the reference, both gains are zero for every pair. Consequently $m=0$, predicted preference probability is 0.5, and loss is $\log2\approx0.6931$. This is true even if the reference itself strongly prefers one response. It is a useful implementation invariant, assuming identical scoring modes and tokenization.

    Derive the adaptive weight

    Differentiating the scalar loss gives $dL/dm=-\sigma(-m)$. The reference is constant, so

    $$\nabla_\theta L=-\beta\sigma(-m)\left[\nabla_\theta\log\pi_\theta(y_w\mid x)-\nabla_\theta\log\pi_\theta(y_l\mid x)\right].$$

    In gradient descent, the chosen log-probability score receives an upward incentive and the rejected score receives a downward incentive. If the pair is badly misclassified, $m$ is very negative and $\sigma(-m)$ is near one. If the margin is already large and positive, that multiplier is small. The optimizer therefore spends less effort on comparisons it already predicts confidently.

    Why β has two explanations that sound contradictory

    In the exact fixed-reward problem, larger $\beta$ means a stronger KL penalty and an optimum nearer the reference. Inside a finite DPO training step, the gradient coefficient is $\beta\sigma(-\beta\Delta)$. At initialization, $\Delta=0$, so its magnitude is $\beta/2$: a larger $\beta$ gives a larger initial coefficient at the same learning rate.

    For a fixed positive $\Delta$, increasing $\beta$ also pushes the sigmoid toward saturation, which can eventually reduce the pair’s weight. For a negative $\Delta$, that saturation works in the other direction. Hence “larger β always makes a smaller update” is false. The fixed-reward optimum, the current margin, the optimizer, and finite training time are different parts of the problem.

    Reward units also matter. Multiplying a known utility by a constant changes both Bradley–Terry confidence and the reward-to-KL tradeoff unless the corresponding scales are adjusted. Tune $\beta$ with the dataset and training setup held explicit, then measure policy behavior and divergence. Do not copy a coefficient without checking the scoring and reduction conventions it assumes.

    A lower DPO loss need not raise absolute chosen probability

    With a uniform three-response reference and $\beta=1$, compare policies $\pi_1=(0.30,0.20,0.50)$ and $\pi_2=(0.15,0.05,0.80)$ on the single preference A over B. A’s probability falls from 0.30 to 0.15, but A:B odds rise from 1.5 to 3. The loss falls from $-\log0.6\approx0.5108$ to $-\log0.75\approx0.2877$.

    These are two feasible policies showing what the objective permits, not a claim that one isolated tabular gradient step follows this exact path. In a neural model, shared parameters and other examples couple many responses. Log chosen likelihood and rejected likelihood separately; a growing margin alone does not prove that the model generates better answers more often.

    Implement the scalar operation stably

    gain_w = policy_chosen_logp - reference_chosen_logp.detach()
gain_l = policy_rejected_logp - reference_rejected_logp.detach()
margin = beta * (gain_w - gain_l)
loss = torch.nn.functional.softplus(-margin).mean()

    -F.logsigmoid(margin) is equivalent. Avoid computing log(sigmoid(margin)) as two naive operations for large negative margins. Every input should be a vector with one whole-response score per pair, and the final mean is over pairs. The authors’ implementation makes the reference subtraction and stable logistic operation explicit. DPO reference implementation.

    The most common practical error happens before these four lines: scoring the wrong tokens or using a mean token log probability in place of a sequence log probability. That is the subject of the implementation chapter.

    Check your understanding: At policy = reference, is the gradient zero because the margin is zero?

    No. The pair loss is $\log2$, but its derivative with respect to the margin is −1/2. The chosen and rejected score gradients usually differ, so the training signal can be nonzero even though the current relative gain is zero.
