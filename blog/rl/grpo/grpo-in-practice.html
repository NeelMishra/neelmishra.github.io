    A small implementation checklist keeps the elegant group idea from turning into an opaque training run.

    Trace the loopChoose a method

    Freeze the right things at the right time

    For each prompt, sample $G$ completions with the rollout policy. Save their token log probabilities, masks, and the policy version that produced them. Score the completions, compute the group mean and standard deviation, and freeze those advantages for the update that follows.

    for prompt in batch:
    completions, old_logp = sample(policy_old, prompt, G)
    rewards = verifier(completions)
    advantages = (rewards - rewards.mean()) / (rewards.std() + eps)
    loss = clipped_ratio_loss(policy, old_logp, advantages)
    loss += reference_kl(policy, reference)
    optimizer.step()

    This sketch leaves out token alignment and batching on purpose. Those details are where many silent bugs live. Check that prompt tokens are excluded from response rewards, that padding does not enter the ratio, and that the old denominator is not accidentally recomputed from the current policy.

    Make the run explain itself.

    Log one inspected prompt with all completions, raw rewards, mean, standard deviation, normalized advantages, response lengths, ratio statistics, and reference KL. A single trace often reveals a masking or sign error faster than a day of watching a mean reward curve.

    Compare runs on the same budget

    GRPO can look cheaper because it avoids a critic, but it still spends compute on multiple sampled completions and verifier calls. Compare methods with the same number of prompts, sampled tokens, and evaluation checks. Report the fraction of zero-spread groups alongside held-out correctness.

    The three questions to ask before training

    1. Can I score answers consistently? If the verifier is noisy or easy to exploit, improve evaluation before increasing the optimizer.
    2. Will a prompt produce disagreement? Measure group spread on a held-out slice. A flat group cannot teach a relative ordering.
    3. What must stay near a reference? Choose the KL coefficient and sequence reduction deliberately, then inspect length, entropy, and KL together.

    You can now return to the larger map

    GRPO is not a new kind of policy gradient. It is a particular answer to the baseline problem: spend samples on a same-prompt comparison, then keep PPO’s ratio guardrail and a reference anchor. With that map in mind, the PPO series explains the critic and trajectory machinery, while the DPO series explains how fixed preference pairs remove online rollouts entirely.

    What would count as evidence that GRPO helped?

    Use a held-out evaluation tied to the task, with the same token and verifier budget across baselines. A higher training reward is only one signal; correctness, group spread, KL, response length, and failure examples complete the picture.
