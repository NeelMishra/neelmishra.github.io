    When a Group Says Nothing

    September 2026 · 5 min read + experiments

    GRPO is wonderfully direct: compare siblings and move toward the winners. That also makes its blind spots easy to name.

    Find the signalUse the checks

    The zero-variance trap

    If all four completions receive the same score, then $r_i-\mu=0$ for every $i$ and the standard deviation is zero. With the usual small $\varepsilon$ in the denominator, every advantage is still zero:

    $$[r_1,r_2,r_3,r_4]=[1,1,1,1]\quad\Longrightarrow\quad[\hat A_1,\hat A_2,\hat A_3,\hat A_4]=[0,0,0,0].$$

    The batch may contain four excellent answers, but it cannot tell the policy which sampled answer to prefer. An all-wrong group has the same problem. This is not a numerical bug to hide with a larger epsilon; it is missing contrast.

    Prompt difficulty is part of the data design

    Easy prompts often produce all-correct groups; impossible prompts often produce all-wrong groups. Both waste samples for a relative baseline. The useful middle is a prompt where the current policy sometimes succeeds and sometimes fails.

    That does not mean discarding easy examples. They can still teach formatting, length, or a process reward. It means measuring group disagreement and changing the sampling mix when most prompts are flat. Increasing $G$ can help a borderline prompt reveal a successful completion, but it cannot make a deterministic verifier disagree when the policy is already certain.

    A verifier can be confidently wrong

    For math or code, an exact checker is attractive because the reward is reproducible. Yet a checker can miss a required explanation, accept a brittle exploit, or reward a test that does not cover the real task. A reward model has a different failure mode: it may prefer polished wording over a correct derivation.

    Keep raw completions beside their scores. Sample a small audit set, inspect disagreements, and report separate metrics for reward, exact correctness, length, and KL. If the verifier changes, treat that as a new experiment; a rising GRPO reward is not evidence by itself that the target behaviour improved.

    Reward scale and response length still matter

    Within-group normalization removes a shared offset and much of the scale difference, but it does not make every objective equivalent. Per-token versus per-sequence aggregation changes which responses receive more gradient. A long answer can accumulate a large log-ratio or KL contribution. Length bonuses can become an accidental shortcut.

    The handoff to a choice

    GRPO is a good fit when you can sample several answers and score them with a trustworthy, reasonably dense signal. When comparisons are already curated offline, DPO may use the data more directly. When states need a value estimate across long trajectories, PPO keeps the critic for a reason.

    Should I increase the group size whenever spread is low?

    Sometimes, but first inspect why spread is low. More samples can uncover a rare success on a difficult prompt; they cannot fix a verifier that gives every answer the same score or a task distribution that contains no learnable contrast.
