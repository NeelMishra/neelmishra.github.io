    When a Group Says Nothing

    September 2026 · 4 min read

    Before trusting a rising reward curve, check whether each group supplies a useful comparison.

    Find the signalUse the checks

    Identical rewards give zero advantages

    Suppose all four answers pass the verifier and receive reward 1. Every reward equals the group mean, so subtracting that mean gives four zeros. Dividing by the spread cannot create a difference that was absent.

    All-wrong answers with rewards [0, 0, 0, 0] also give zero advantages. Try the all-correct setting in the group calculator. This follows directly from the normalization used in DeepSeekMath’s outcome-supervision rule.

    Zero advantages mean zero reward-based update for this group. The separate reference penalty can still change the policy. Increasing the small numerical constant in the denominator will not recover a learning signal.

    Check why the group is flat

    With a binary correctness reward, easy prompts may produce only successes and very hard prompts only failures. A prompt the current policy sometimes solves is more likely to supply useful comparisons.

    First inspect the answers. Are they duplicates? Is the verifier returning a constant? Or is success simply rare? A larger group can uncover a rare success, but costs more generation and scoring. Change one sampling setting at a time and measure the resulting mix of groups.

    Formatting or partial-credit rewards can distinguish answers that share the same correctness score, provided those differences reflect behavior you actually want. Adding the same bonus to every answer leaves the group advantages unchanged.

    Check the answers behind the scores

    A reproducible checker can still reward the wrong behavior: code may pass incomplete tests, or a math answer may match the expected final number while giving an invalid explanation. A learned judge may favor polished wording.

    Save completions beside raw scores. Inspect a small sample, including unexpectedly high-scoring answers, and evaluate on held-out problems with independent checks where possible. A higher training reward alone does not establish better task performance.

    Normalization does not remove every design choice

    Subtracting the group mean removes a shared reward offset; dividing by the spread largely removes a shared positive scale. But tiny, noisy reward differences can become substantial normalized weights. Inspect raw rewards alongside advantages.

    Response length also enters through averaging. The paper’s reduction averages token contributions within each answer before averaging answers. Averaging every token in the batch together instead gives longer answers more total weight. Keep the reduction fixed when comparing runs.

    RecordWhat to inspect
    Correctness and fraction of zero-spread groupsAre answers improving, and do groups still provide a reward signal?
    Raw rewards and normalized advantagesAre small scoring errors being amplified?
    Held-out results and saved completionsDoes the verifier reward the intended behavior?
    Response lengths and sample diversityAre answers becoming repetitive or exploiting a length incentive?
    Token ratios and reference divergenceHow far did the policy move from the rollout and reference policies?

    Inspect one group before scaling up
