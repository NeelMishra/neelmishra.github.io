    Turn a Group into an Advantage

    September 2026 · 5 min read + experiments

    The critic’s job is to say what a prompt is worth. GRPO asks a smaller question: which answer in this group was better than its neighbours?

    Read the explanationMove the rewards

    Four attempts, one shared question

    Suppose a verifier asks whether a model solved “What is $17\times 19$?” correctly. We sample four completions from the same policy and score them $[1.0, 0.7, 0.2, 0.0]$. The absolute scale is up to the verifier. What matters first is their order.

    Subtract the group mean

    Let $r_i$ be the score for completion $i$ and let $G$ be the group size. The group mean is

    $$\mu=\frac{1}{G}\sum_{j=1}^{G}r_j.$$

    For our four answers, $\mu=(1.0+0.7+0.2+0.0)/4=0.475$. Subtracting it centres the scores: $[0.525, 0.225,-0.275,-0.475]$. The policy gradient can now increase the log probability of above-average answers and decrease the log probability of below-average answers.

    This is a baseline, just like the critic’s value estimate in PPO. The difference is where the estimate comes from. A critic learns across states and prompts; a group baseline is computed freshly from siblings sampled for the same prompt. It is cheap and local, but it has no information outside this group.

    Divide by spread so prompts share a scale

    One prompt may produce rewards clustered around 0.9, while another ranges from 0 to 1. If we only subtract the mean, a high-variance prompt can dominate the batch. GRPO commonly normalizes by the group standard deviation $\sigma$:

    $$\hat A_i=\frac{r_i-\mu}{\sigma+\varepsilon},\qquad \sigma=\sqrt{\frac{1}{G}\sum_{j=1}^{G}(r_j-\mu)^2}.$$

    For $[1.0,0.7,0.2,0.0]$, the population standard deviation is about $0.396$, giving approximate weights $[+1.33,+0.57,-0.69,-1.20]$. The weights sum to zero. That zero-sum property prevents a prompt from pushing every completion up at once.

    Read the sign before the size.

    A positive weight means “make this sampled completion more likely relative to its group.” It does not mean “this answer is objectively good.” A group of four wrong answers can still contain a least-wrong answer with a positive weight.

    The handoff to the objective

    We have replaced a learned value prediction with four numbers computed from one group. The remaining question is familiar from PPO: how far may the new policy move when it reweights those answers? The next chapter puts $\hat A_i$ inside the clipped ratio objective, then adds the reference-model term used for language models.

    Why not use raw rewards directly?

    Raw rewards mix prompt difficulty with answer quality. A baseline removes the shared part. Normalization also makes the update less sensitive to the verifier’s units, although it cannot repair a systematically wrong verifier.
