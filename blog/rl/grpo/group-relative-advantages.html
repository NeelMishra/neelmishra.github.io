    Turn a Group into an Advantage

    September 2026 · 4 min read + experiments

    The critic’s job is to say what a prompt is worth. GRPO asks a smaller question: which answer in this group was better than its neighbours?

    Read the explanationMove the rewards

    Compare several answers to the same question

    Sample four answers from the same policy for one prompt. An illustrative scoring rubric gives partial credit, producing rewards [1.0, 0.7, 0.2, 0.0]. We want a positive learning weight for answers above the group’s average and a negative weight for answers below it.

    This chapter explains the outcome-supervised version of Group Relative Policy Optimization (GRPO): one score per completed answer. DeepSeekMath, Section 4.1.2, describes this use of a sampled group instead of a learned critic.

    First subtract the group average

    Let $G$ be the number of answers, and $r_i$ the reward for answer $i$. Write $\mu$ for their mean. Add the rewards and divide by the group size:

    $$\mu=\frac{1}{G}\sum_{j=1}^{G}r_j.$$

    For the four rewards above, the mean is 0.475. The first answer’s difference is $1.0-0.475=0.525$; the last answer’s difference is $0-0.475=-0.475$. Their signs already tell us which side of the group average they fall on.

    A PPO critic predicts a baseline from the state. This baseline comes directly from the sampled siblings. It includes the answer being scored, so it is not an independent critic estimate and does not inherit the earlier baseline proof unchanged.

    Then express the difference relative to the group’s spread

    A difference of 0.1 is large in a tightly clustered group and small in a widely spread group. GRPO commonly divides by the group’s standard deviation, written $\sigma$. For these examples, compute it using the average squared distance from the mean:

    $$\sigma=\sqrt{\frac{1}{G}\sum_{j=1}^{G}(r_j-\mu)^2}.$$

    The resulting advantage $\hat A_i$ is the answer’s centered reward divided by that spread. A tiny positive constant $\varepsilon_{\mathrm{num}}=10^{-8}$ prevents division by zero:

    $$\hat A_i=\frac{r_i-\mu}{\sigma+\varepsilon_{\mathrm{num}}}.$$

    Here the standard deviation is about 0.396. The weights are approximately [+1.33, +0.57, −0.69, −1.20]. Read the sign first: positive means better than the sampled group, not necessarily objectively correct.

    The weights sum to zero, but the policy update need not: different answers have different log-probability gradients. If every reward is identical, every weight is zero and this group supplies no reward-based update. The failure-modes chapter explains what to do with such groups.

    Use these numbers as fixed actor weights

    Compute the group statistics once for the collected answers. In the outcome-supervised version, every response token in answer $i$ receives its answer’s weight $\hat A_i$. Keep those weights fixed during the optimizer passes.

    Next, combine them with PPO’s token probability ratios and clipping rule. Group normalization changes the training signal; it does not itself limit how far the policy can move.
