    Keep the PPO Guardrail

    September 2026 · 5 min read + experiments

    GRPO changes the baseline, then reuses PPO’s most useful safety feature: compare the new policy with the one that collected the samples and clip the ratio.

    Follow the updateSee the two cases

    The same sample, a different weight

    From Chapter 1, completion $i$ has a group-relative advantage $\hat A_i$. During sampling, the old policy assigned it probability $\pi_{\text{old}}(y_i\mid x)$. During optimization, the trainable policy assigns a new probability $\pi_\theta(y_i\mid x)$. Their ratio is

    $$\rho_i(\theta)=\frac{\pi_\theta(y_i\mid x)}{\pi_{\text{old}}(y_i\mid x)}.$$

    The actor wants to make $\rho_i$ larger when $\hat A_i$ is positive and smaller when it is negative. Several optimizer passes over one rollout can push too hard, so PPO clips the ratio to $[1-\epsilon,1+\epsilon]$.

    Write the objective in layers

    The clipped policy part is the PPO surrogate with a group advantage:

    $$L_{\text{clip}}=\mathbb{E}\left[\min\left(\rho_i\hat A_i,\;\operatorname{clip}(\rho_i,1-\epsilon,1+\epsilon)\hat A_i\right)\right].$$

    For language models, the sampled response is a sequence of tokens. Implementations aggregate token log-ratios over the response, while the exact details of masking and reduction must match the chosen objective. GRPO then discourages drift from a frozen reference policy with a KL term:

    $$L_{\text{GRPO}}=L_{\text{clip}}-\beta\,D_{\mathrm{KL}}(\pi_\theta\,\|\,\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}).$$

    Signs depend on whether your code maximizes a reward objective or minimizes a loss. The invariant is easier to remember: the group advantage chooses the direction, clipping limits the incentive from a reused sample, and the reference term keeps language behaviour anchored.

    Two rows explain the min

    Group signalNew ratioWhat clipping protects
    $\hat A_i>0$ — better than siblings$\rho_i=1.30$If $\epsilon=0.2$, the objective uses $1.20\hat A_i$; extra pushing earns no extra credit for this sample.
    $\hat A_i<0$ — worse than siblings$\rho_i=0.70$The objective uses $0.80\hat A_i$; it prevents one bad sample from being driven down without bound.

    The ratio compares policies on the same sampled text. It is not a score of answer quality. A high ratio on a negative-advantage answer means the new policy is increasing the probability of something the group judged below average—the clipped term pushes back.

    There are two frozen policies

    Keep the roles separate while reading code. The old policy supplied the denominator and was frozen for the rollout. The reference policy supplies the KL anchor and is usually the supervised-fine-tuned model. They can start from the same checkpoint, but they serve different purposes and may diverge after updates begin.

    This separation is the bridge from the algebra to a trainer: store old log probabilities with the sampled completions, recompute current log probabilities during optimization, and score reference log probabilities without updating the reference model. The group baseline is computed before the optimizer loop, then held fixed for that rollout.

    The handoff to the hard part

    The equation is now familiar: a group provides the sign, PPO provides the local guardrail, and KL provides a language-model anchor. But a baseline can only compare what it sees. Next, we test the groups themselves: what happens when all four completions are right, all four are wrong, or the verifier gives a seductive but incorrect score?

    Does GRPO remove clipping because it already has several samples?

    No. Multiple completions reduce some variance in the baseline; they do not stop repeated optimizer passes from changing the policy too far. GRPO keeps the ratio comparison and its clip for the same local-reuse reason as PPO.
